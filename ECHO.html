Peter Lynch — EchoStar Corporation (ECHO) — The Contrarian Optimist's Case

Framing: what kind of stock is this?

This is not a fast grower — every reportable subscriber metric in the dossier is declining (Section 5). It is not a stalwart either — the OIBDA base is shrinking and there's no dividend. This is a turnaround wearing an asset play's clothes: a company that spent a decade warehousing spectrum, tried and failed to build a national 5G network, got forced by its own balance sheet to liquidate that spectrum to two buyers who badly wanted it, and is now sitting on the proceeds while the market still prices it off the corpse of the failed buildout. My job is to price the thing it is becoming, not the thing the trailing 10-K describes.

1. THE TEN-BAGGER TEST

A literal ten-bagger from $89.80 is not realistic — this isn't an undiscovered small-cap, it just entered the S&P 500 [MEDIA: 24/7 Wall St., Mar 23, 2026]. Hughes

CULTURAL ANTHROPOLOGY — ECHO (EchoStar Corporation)

Framing note

EchoStar presents an unusual case for this lens: the company itself has stopped trying to win on culture. Management's own language — "asset-light growth," "cash-rich company" [SEARCH: Light Reading, quoting Akhavan] — describes a retreat from the operating businesses this analysis is built to evaluate, into a financial-engineering story (spectrum sale proceeds, a SpaceX equity stake, cash). I evaluate the four brands as they exist today, and I flag explicitly where the "consumer_brand" moat tag in the dossier (Section 2) has stopped describing anything real.

1. CULTURAL ROLE

None of EchoStar's four consumer-facing businesses is a ritual or a status symbol. All four are utilities, and all four are utilities of last resort rather than utilities of choice:

  • DISH TV (satellite pay-TV): historically a utility for households outside cable footprints — rural America, primarily. It has never been a cultural verb the way "Google it" or "Netflix and chill" are. Nobody says "let's DISH it." It is chosen because cable isn't available or streaming bundles cost more, not because it carries identity value.
  • Sling TV: was positioned as the culturally modern, a-la-carte alternative for cord-cutters who still wanted live TV — a genuine attempt at cultural relevance circa 2015. That positioning has aged out: the company itself now attributes Sling's own subscriber losses (189,000 in Q1 2026) to subscription VoD, live-streaming competitors, and new a-la-carte products like ESPN Unlimited and Fox One [SEARCH/company release, Section 5]. Sling is now being disintermediated by the exact behavior it was built to capture — consumers going straight to single-service, single-purpose streaming instead of a bridge product.
  • Boost Mobile: a value/prepaid brand. Its cultural role is price arbitrage, not identity or status — nobody is a "Boost person" the way some consumers self-identify as "iPhone people." Its own hybrid-MNO pivot (primary connectivity now via AT&T towers, continued T-Mobile access, EchoStar's own RAN being decommissioned [SEARCH, Section 3]) converts Boost from an owned-network challenger brand into a resold-capacity value play — a weaker cultural position than either its former "we own the network" pitch or a dedicated low-cost MVNO's simplicity.
  • Hughes/HughesNet: rural broadband as utility of last resort. Its cultural role is entirely substitutional — chosen when nothing better exists. That "nothing better" condition is precisely what LEO broadband is erasing: Hughes lost more than 21% of its broadband subscribers in the past year to Starlink competition, and Hughes Satellite Systems itself filed Chapter 11 on August 3, 2026, stating it would refocus on enterprise, government, and defense customers "following losses in its legacy consumer internet market" [SEARCH, moat-threat section]. That is a company's own filed admission that its consumer cultural role is over.

No brand in the portfolio is embedded in a cultural moment (home buying, social connection, entertainment discovery) the way, say, a property portal or a social platform is. They are back-office-of-life utilities: how you get TV or a phone plan if you have no better option.

2. TRIBAL DYNAMICS — THE CAPTIVE VS. DEVOTEE TEST

  • DISH TV's tribe is captives, not devotees, and the dossier shows the prison is emptying anyway: satellite lost 177,000 subscribers in Q1 2026 alone, and total Pay-TV lost 366,000 in Q1 and roughly 241,000 in Q2 2026, closing at 6.39 million (4.68M DISH TV, 1.71M Sling) [SEARCH/company release, Section 5]. It swung from +212,000 subscribers in the year-ago quarter to -118,000 in Q2 2026, and had already posted a surprise loss of 9,000 in Q4 2025 against Street expectations of 160,000+ additions [SEARCH, Section 5]. A tribe with genuine brand loyalty absorbs a network-architecture change (moving primary connectivity to AT&T) without a swing of this magnitude. This one didn't.

3. GENERATIONAL ANALYSIS — THE "MY KIDS" TEST

This is the most falsifiable and damning section of the dossier for a cultural read.

  • Cord-cutting by age cohort is measured and severe: only 34% of 18-29 year-olds get TV via cable or satellite in 2025, down 31 percentage points from 2015; only 46% of 30-49 year-olds do, down 27 points; the 50-64 cohort is down 14 points since 2015; only the 65+ cohort's 5-point decline was not statistically significant [SEARCH: "Cord cutting has grown since 2015," moat-threat section]. Every business sits on the declining side of its category's generational technology shift: satellite TV → streaming, GEO broadband → LEO broadband, owned-network prepaid wireless → MVNO wireless.

Buffett Verdict

EchoStar is not an “Inevitable” fortress. Its durable spectrum moat has largely been sold, while every reported operating business is losing subscribers.

What remains is primarily a special-situation holding company:

That makes ECHO a prospective cigar butt—not a wonderful company at a fair price. At $89.80 [SEC/market data], the cigar’s remaining value cannot yet be measured reliably enough to buy.

1. The Anti-Fragile Moat

The Chaos Test: Failed

An anti-fragile business should become more valuable when weaker competitors retreat. EchoStar instead reached a point where its “tenuous financial position forced its hand,” according to Charlie Ergen [SEARCH: Light Reading]. It skipped an approximately $326M interest payment in May 2025 [SEARCH: Robbins Geller], sold its prime spectrum, terminated its national 5G buildout, and placed major subsidiaries into Chapter 11.

The operating evidence is similarly fragile:

  • Pay-TV lost approximately 241,000 subscribers in Q2 2026, ending with 6.39M [SEARCH: company release].
  • Wireless lost approximately 118,000 subscribers, ending with 7.38M [SEARCH].
  • Hughes consumer broadband has been losing customers to Starlink; Hughes revenue fell 10.6% in a reported quarter [SEARCH: SpaceNews].
  • The moat-threat dossier says Hughes lost more than 21% of broadband subscribers over the preceding year [SEARCH].

A recession might help Sling or Boost attract value-conscious consumers, but the dossier contains no evidence that this effect is overcoming structural churn. These businesses are not getting stronger through chaos; they are being simplified because financial stress made the former strategy untenable.

The $1B Competitor Test: Mostly Failed

A rival could not recreate nationwide FCC spectrum licenses for $1B. That was the genuine moat. But EchoStar sold most of that scarce resource to AT&T and SpaceX.

The surviving operating businesses are much more vulnerable:

  • DISH and Sling face streaming and à-la-carte substitutes with low switching friction.
  • Boost purchases primary connectivity from AT&T and retains access to T-Mobile [SEARCH: EchoStar release]. It no longer controls the critical infrastructure.
  • Hughes faces Starlink, a better-capitalized LEO competitor with lower latency and rapidly expanding scale.
  • Consumer brands such as DISH, Sling, Boost, and HughesNet have awareness, but subscriber losses show that mind share is not creating captivity.

A competent rival armed with $1B could not kill all of EchoStar, but it could intensify pricing, device subsidies, content competition, and customer acquisition pressure in the operating businesses. The more important point is that existing rivals are already doing so.

Pricing Power: No Evidence

The dossier provides no evidence that EchoStar can raise prices without accelerating churn.

Pay-TV generated excellent current cash flow—$600.7M of Q2 OIBDA on $2.249B of revenue, a 26.7% margin [SEARCH; CALC]—but lost approximately 241,000 subscribers during the same quarter [SEARCH: company release]. That resembles harvesting, not pricing power.

Boost’s Q2 OIBDA margin was only 5.5% [CALC], while subscribers fell by approximately 118,000 [SEARCH]. Hughes is losing business to a technologically superior substitute.

Real pricing power preserves volume. EchoStar’s evidence shows shrinking volume across every disclosed subscriber category.

2. Strategic Evolution

Feature Absorption: More Replacement Than Absorption

There are pieces of intelligent adaptation:

  • Sling was an early attempt to absorb streaming into the pay-TV ecosystem.
  • Boost customers will receive access to Starlink direct-to-cell capabilities [SEARCH].
  • Moving Boost onto AT&T towers lowers the need to fund a proprietary radio network.

But economically, EchoStar is surrendering ownership of the critical layers:

  • Its 5G radio network is being decommissioned.
  • AT&T now owns major spectrum formerly controlled by EchoStar.
  • SpaceX received the AWS-4, H-block, and AWS-3 spectrum involved in the reported transactions.
  • Starlink is simultaneously EchoStar’s investment, partner, and principal threat to Hughes.

Owning SpaceX shares is financially attractive if the valuation and liquidity claims prove real. It does not mean Hughes has absorbed the LEO threat. The competitor has absorbed EchoStar’s spectrum.

Commoditizing the Complement: Strategically Inverted

EchoStar is lowering the capital intensity of wireless by treating radio access as a purchased complement from AT&T and T-Mobile. That may improve Boost’s accounting economics and remove billions of future construction requirements.

But this does not reinforce a core monopoly. It leaves Boost dependent on suppliers that also compete for the same customer. EchoStar has commoditized its own former infrastructure while giving counterparties greater strategic control.

The pivot may create a better reseller, but it does not create a fortress.

Verdict: everything with revenue today is prosumer-grade and actively being disrupted. The one segment with a plausible durable workflow moat is unsized.

3. Structural vs. Cyclical — Will Any of This Come Back?

None of ECHO's declines are cyclical. Each is structural and, per management's own words, largely irreversible:

  • The 5G buildout retreat was not a strategic choice to redeploy capital — Chairman Ergen said the "tenuous financial position forced its hand," that the company would "win the battle, lose the war," and called it a forced pivot [Section 3]. That is a company describing its own capitulation, not a company describing a cyclical trough it will re-enter when conditions improve. The spectrum is sold. There is no "coming back" to build a national 5G network.
  • Pay-TV's decline is generational, not cyclical — an 18–29-year-old who never subscribed to satellite TV is not a cyclical loss waiting to reverse when the economy improves; it's a structural, permanent shift in media consumption [cord-cutting data, Section above].
  • Broadband's decline is a technology-driven structural replacement: LEO satellite architecture (Starlink) has a permanent latency and cost advantage over Hughes's GEO architecture. This is why Hughes's own consumer subsidiary just filed for bankruptcy rather than waiting out a cycle.

There is no secular tailwind visible anywhere in ECHO's owned, revenue-generating operations. The only structural tailwind the company benefits from is indirect: it sold out of a race it was losing and, in the SpaceX transaction, ended up holding equity in the entity that is structurally winning (Starlink revenue an estimated $10.6B with 54% EBITDA margins and subscribers doubling for two straight years to 9.2 million [MEDIA: PitchBook, unaudited estimate of a private company]). Is management investing defensively or offensively? 1. SPECIES CLASSIFICATION

EchoStar is not one organism; it is a colony that has just shed most of its original tissue. HISTORIAN OF DISRUPTION — ECHO (EchoStar Corporation)

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

Iridium LLC, original entity (1991-2000). Motorola-backed satellite phone venture built on an exclusive global spectrum allocation — the same resource_ownership moat type as ECHO's. Cost over $5 billion to build, launched commercial service in 1998, targeted 500,000 subscribers, captured roughly 55,000. Filed Chapter 11 in August 1999, thirteen months after launch, because terrestrial cellular networks scaled faster and cheaper than satellite could match. The satellite assets were sold out of bankruptcy for approximately $25 million — roughly half a cent on the dollar of invested capital — to a new group that relaunched as the much smaller, niche Iridium Communications. Lesson for ECHO: an exclusive spectrum license does not protect equity value once a terrestrial or lower-cost substitute wins the underlying utility function, and the "reboot" survivor was unrecognizable in scale from the original. This is the single closest historical analogue in this dossier to what is now happening at Hughes.

LightSquared / Ligado Networks (2005-present). Held valuable L-band satellite/terrestrial-hybrid spectrum and planned to build a nationwide LTE network reusing it. The FCC and the GPS industry determined the signal would interfere with GPS receivers, stranding the spectrum for its intended use. Filed Chapter 11 in 2012, emerged as Ligado in 2015, and as of the 2020s still had not been able to commercially deploy the spectrum at scale, missing interest payments and returning to restructuring talks more than a decade after the original filing. Lesson: a resource_ownership moat that depends on continued FCC goodwill can be stranded by regulatory action with no cure period — and the dossier's own moat-threat log shows ECHO facing four live proposed or enacted FCC actions (AWS-3 reauction by June 2026, Upper C-band auction by July 2027, CBRS shared-spectrum expansion, the $2.4B decommissioning trust already imposed on the AT&T deal) that are the same species of risk that killed LightSquared's business model, not merely its stock price.

Nortel Networks (peak 2000, Chapter 11 January 2009). A telecom equipment maker with a valuable patent portfolio (~4,000 patents) that, like ECHO, tried to monetize an intangible asset base as the operating business collapsed. The patents were auctioned in 2011 for $4.5 billion to the "Rockstar" consortium (Apple, Microsoft, RIM, Ericsson, Sony, EMC) — a headline-grabbing sum — but years of subsequent inter-creditor litigation determined that operating-company common shareholders received essentially nothing; secured creditors and pension claims stood ahead of them. Lesson directly on point: asset monetization during the death of an operating business does not automatically flow through to equity. ECHO's own dossier documents the analogous mechanism already in motion — a court has denied a motion to dismiss fraudulent-transfer and breach-of-indenture claims over the January 2024 transfer of spectrum, ~3 million DISH TV subscribers, and Sling TV from DBS to EchoStar or unrestricted subsidiaries [moat-threat section, status: enacted]. That is the Nortel pattern — value moved toward the parent, contested by creditors of the entity it was moved from — playing out in real time, unresolved.

Blockbuster (Chapter 11, September 2010). Included for contrast, not similarity. Blockbuster's moat was a >9,000-store real-estate and distribution network; management publicly dismissed Netflix as a "niche business" as late as 2008 and filed for bankruptcy roughly four years after Netflix's 2007 streaming launch. ECHO's management does not match this denial pattern — Ergen has explicitly called the spectrum pivot "forced" and stated the company would "win the battle, lose the war" if it fought the FCC [SEARCH: Light Reading]. 2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

BlackBerry / Research In Motion (2013-2016 pivot). Device market share collapsed from roughly 20% in 2009 to under 1% by 2016 as the iPhone and Android took over. Under CEO John Chen, BlackBerry killed its own hardware business in 2016 and pivoted fully into enterprise security software and patent licensing, becoming a smaller but survivable company rather than fighting for handset share it could never recover. This is the closest survivor-pattern match to ECHO's decision to stop building its own 5G radio access network and instead decommission Boost's RAN in favor of a hybrid MNO deal riding AT&T's towers and T-Mobile's network. Both moves say: stop paying to lose a capital-intensive race you cannot win, and convert the residual asset (patents; spectrum) into cash or licensing income instead.

IBM (1990s-2014, repeated divestitures). Sold its PC division to Lenovo in 2005, exited hard-disk manufacturing in 2002, and in 2014 paid GlobalFoundries $1.5 billion to take its chip-manufacturing business off its hands — accepting a negative sale price to exit a declining, capital-intensive category and redeploy capital into higher-return services and software. ECHO's spectrum sales, at a positive price, are the easier version of this same discipline: shed the declining capital-intensive asset rather than continue funding it defensively.

Sirius XM Radio (2008-present). Faced structural disruption from both terrestrial radio and on-demand streaming, survived a near-bankruptcy in 2009 via a Liberty Media rescue, and rather than trying to out-innovate Spotify, consolidated (Sirius+XM merger into a satellite-radio monopoly) and monetized its installed base and auto-OEM distribution as a cash-generative niche rather than a growth story. It still generates real free cash flow today without having "won" against streaming. This is the plausible outcome for ECHO's Pay-TV segment specifically: $600.7M of Q2 2026 OIBDA on a shrinking 6.39M-subscriber base [SEARCH] looks like a cash cow in managed decline, not a business anyone expects to grow again — survival, not victory.

T-Mobile's absorption of Sprint's spectrum (2020). T-Mobile turned Sprint's stranded 2.5GHz spectrum — the core asset of a sub-scale, dying competitor — into the foundation of its 5G network leadership by deploying it rapidly at scale. The mirror-image lesson for ECHO: spectrum value is realized by whoever can deploy it at national scale, and a sub-scale holder selling to the scaled deployer (AT&T buying ECHO's 3.45GHz and 600MHz spectrum) is the economically correct decision for the seller — the alternative is Sprint's pre-2020 path of hanging on with an under-deployed asset until it loses independence on worse terms.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

This is a binary/event-driven problem wearing a sum-of-the-parts costume.

Normally, a satellite-TV-and-wireless company is a "clean analytical" problem: count subscribers, project cash flow, discount it, done. You could do that here, and the council did. The answer for the operating businesses alone — Pay-TV, Boost, Hughes, minus what's owed to creditors — comes out to somewhere between −$16.53 and +$3.24 per diluted share. Round it off: the actual TV-and-phone business, run through every reasonable assumption, is worth approximately nothing to a common shareholder.

And yet the stock trades at $89.80.

The entire gap — well over 95% of the price you're paying — is one number: what is EchoStar's stake in SpaceX actually worth? And that number cannot be looked up. SpaceX doesn't trade on an exchange. The sources in the file describing the stake disagree by roughly 5x: a company-linked carrying value of $11.1 billion, versus third-party media estimates of $32 billion and $53 billion. That $42 billion spread is larger than EchoStar's entire $26 billion stock-market value.

This is why it behaves like a binary event rather than a normal valuation. In a binary problem — a drug trial readout, an M&A vote, a jury verdict — one external, unobservable event dominates every other variable, and everything you'd normally analyze (management quality, market share, margins) becomes noise until that event resolves. That's exactly the shape here: the Pay-TV subscriber trend, the Boost turnaround, the Hughes bankruptcy — all real, all analyzable, all almost irrelevant, because none of them move the stock's fair value by more than a few dollars, while the SpaceX number alone moves it by over $100. A genuine sum-of-the-parts problem is still "clean analytical" — you add up divisions you can price. This one has a part nobody can price, and that part is the whole ballgame. That combination is why it's classified as binary/event-driven, not clean analytical, despite looking, at first glance, like an org chart you just add up.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

When one unpriceable asset can swing the answer by more than the value of the whole company, use this framework instead of a normal DCF:

  1. Split the balance sheet into "priced" and "unpriced" buckets. Priced = public securities, disclosed cash, quoted debt. Unpriced = private stakes, contested legal claims, anything with no market.
  2. Collect every estimate of the unpriced bucket — don't blend them into one guess. A single blended number hides the disagreement that is the actual finding.
  3. Check whether the range of the unpriced bucket, in per-share terms, exceeds the value of the whole company. If yes, you are no longer doing valuation — you are doing option pricing.
  4. Flip the question. Instead of asking "what is this worth," ask "what does the unpriced asset need to be worth to justify today's price?" That turns an unanswerable question into a checkable one — you can debate whether SpaceX is worth $500 billion or $2.4 trillion; you cannot debate an unknowable "fair value" directly.
  5. Distrust manufactured precision. Watch for equations that look like proof but are actually tautologies (identities that are true by construction, corroborating nothing) — a classic trap in exactly this kind of problem.
  6. Size the position like an option, not like a stock. If the dominant input can resolve far below what's priced in, you can lose most of your money regardless of how well-run the rest of the business is.
  7. Name the resolving event and wait for it. An IPO, a disclosed mark, a lockup expiry, a court ruling. Until it happens, the thesis doesn't develop — it just sits, exposed to the same uncertainty, every day you hold it.

4. How They Make Money

Three shrinking subscription businesses: DISH TV and Sling TV (satellite and streaming pay-TV), Boost Mobile (wireless service, now bought wholesale from AT&T's and T-Mobile's towers instead of EchoStar's own network), and Hughes (satellite internet, for consumers and increasingly government/defense customers). On top of that, EchoStar just collected $20.25 billion cash from AT&T and an equity stake from SpaceX for spectrum it no longer needs — a one-time asset conversion, not a repeatable revenue stream.

The FCC told EchoStar its spectrum was underused and threatened outright license revocation; the company itself called it a "forced pivot." What's left is brand loyalty in three businesses that lose customers every single quarter: Pay-TV shed roughly 607,000 subscribers in the first half of the year, Boost swung from adding 212,000 subscribers a year ago to losing 118,000, and Hughes lost more than a fifth of its broadband base to Starlink and is now in Chapter 11. Only 34% of Americans aged 18–29 still take cable or satellite TV. Be honest about the verdict here: eleven of twelve council experts rated the moat SEVERELY impaired, and the five-member Moat Tribunal was unanimous. There isn't a moat left to defend — there's a melting ice cube with a good brand name on it.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

Everything hinges on the SpaceX stake being worth close to the top of its range. If it settles nearer the bottom — the $11.1 billion figure that's actually tied to a company financial statement, rather than the $32–53 billion media estimates — there is no floor underneath you. The legacy businesses, on their own, are worth roughly zero to $3 a share. If the SpaceX mark disappoints, or if EchoStar can never actually sell the stake for what it's carried at, there is nothing else in this company to catch you.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Imagine buying a house for $89.80 per "share." You walk through it and most of the rooms — the TV room, the phone room, the internet room — are small, cluttered, and visibly shrinking; a fair appraisal of all of them together comes to somewhere between negative $16.53 and positive $3.24. But there's one enormous room at the back of the house that the appraiser isn't allowed to enter. Different appraisers, looking through the keyhole, guess its value anywhere from $11.1 billion to $53 billion — a $42 billion spread, bigger than the entire $26 billion sale price of the house.

To justify what you're paying today, that one room needs to be worth an amount implying SpaceX itself — the company next door — is worth somewhere between $1.3 trillion and $2.5 trillion. Run the honest math across every reasonable combination of assumptions, and the stock is priced above fair value in nine of twelve scenarios. It's only cheap in the scenario where SpaceX is worth roughly $2.4 trillion — in the neighborhood of the most valuable companies on earth today.

That's not a valuation call anyone can make from public information. It's a bet on the size of a room nobody can measure. The council's verdict: PASS, 0% position — one buy vote (Lynch), seven waits, three passes, one sell (Burry) — not because the business is worthless, but because you cannot honestly price what you'd be buying, and the price being asked already assumes the best possible answer to the one question nobody can currently answer.

One: it is applied to cash. Of the $25.3B gross asset base, $21.8B is cash and near-cash. Holding-company discounts are observed on holdcos owning illiquid operating stakes with tax leakage on realisation. Charging 30% against a parent's own unrestricted bank balance — at a company with a $5 billion buyback authorisation already voted by the board — is not an empirical adjustment. THE REAL WARREN BUFFETT'S TAKE

Charlie's got the arithmetic, and he's right about all of it. Let me talk about the part that doesn't need arithmetic, because I think this memo reached a defensible destination by an indefensible road, and it's worth separating those.

Circle of competence

Here's the question I'd actually ask, and it isn't the one the memo asks.

The memo says the problem is that we can't value the SpaceX stake. That's not really the problem. We can estimate it — there's a transaction mark from a sophisticated buyer, and that's better evidence than most things I've relied on in fifty years. The memo says so itself, and then loses its nerve.

The real question is simpler and it doesn't have a number in it. Do I understand what a minority shareholder of EchoStar actually owns?

Let's see. You own a share with essentially no vote — one man holds 90.3% of the votes on 50.9% of the economics. You get no dividend. The board authorised a $5 billion buyback and the indentures won't let them do it. Two subsidiaries are in Chapter 11. There's a Section 363 auction of the estate's alter-ego claims against the company you own. There's a securities investigation with no company response found. Two insiders sold in March 2026 near $113 with no 10b5-1 disclosure found. The CEO changed twice in nine months with no stated reason, the second time by resigning effective immediately. And the largest asset is unlisted, unmarked, and of unestablished salability.

Now — I don't need a SpaceX valuation to answer that. I have no idea what I own, and neither does anyone else outside that building. That's not a spread problem. That's a disclosure and governance problem, and it doesn't get fixed by the Q3 10-Q.

So on the question of whether to own it, the memo lands in about the right place. It just gets there through six pages of arithmetic that don't hold up, when the honest answer was available in a paragraph. And the arithmetic route matters, because the arithmetic route produces a trigger price of $30–45 — which tells a reader that this becomes a fine investment at $32. It doesn't. Nothing here becomes fine at $32. The governance facts don't improve when the price falls. If anything they get worse, because a lower price means the market has learned something you haven't.

That's the real cost of dressing a governance conclusion up as a valuation conclusion. You end up publishing a number that invites somebody to buy the thing.

The toll bridge

There isn't one anymore, and I want to be fair about what happened. This wasn't a moat that eroded. It was a moat that got sold, at what looks like a decent price, by a man with a gun to his head.

Ergen's own words: the FCC investigation left "only one logical path"; he believed the company "stood on solid legal ground" but its "tenuous financial position forced its hand" — "we would win the battle, lose the war." That's a forced seller, and forced sellers usually get robbed. He got $20.25 billion in cash and equity in the most valuable private company on earth. That is a very good outcome for a forced seller, and the memo never quite credits it. It describes the extraction accurately and then charges a 30% discount to the man who performed it.

What's left is not a toll bridge. Pay-TV lost 241,000 subscribers in one quarter — 3.6% of the base, running near 16% a year. Boost went from +212,000 a year ago to −118,000. Hughes lost 21% of its broadband subscribers and is in Chapter 11 because it couldn't repay $1.5 billion that came due August 1. And the Anthropologist's number is the one I'd have led with: 34% of Americans aged 18 to 29 still take cable or satellite, down 31 points since 2015. You cannot fix that with pricing, promotion, or management. The customers are not being replaced. There is no version of this where the operating businesses become the reason you own the stock.

So on the ceiling: the memo is right that there's no upward adjustment available. Nobody here has pricing power. Nobody here has an inevitable product.

Capital preservation — rule number one

Here's where I part company with the memo's tone, and it's the most important thing I have to say.

The memo treats the whole $41.9 billion SpaceX spread as the risk. It isn't. A wide range on an asset you own is not a risk of permanent loss. If the stake marks at $11 billion instead of $32 billion, you paid too much — that's an opportunity cost, and a real one, but you own a real thing at a real price.

The risk of permanent loss in this file is one paragraph long, and it's the alter-ego claims. If veil-piercing succeeds, subsidiary liabilities reach the non-debtor parent, the $20.25 billion stops being insulated, and the common goes to a number that starts with zero. That is the risk. It is the only risk here that is genuinely a Rule Number One risk. And the memo prices it at $1.5 billion, midway between a $125 million settlement precedent and "unquantified anywhere," and moves on.

I'd rather have a memo that said: the valuation range is wide but survivable, and here is the one thing that can take you to zero, and I cannot bound it. That's a Rule Number One conclusion, and it's actionable — you don't buy it at any price, and the trigger price section shouldn't exist at all. Instead we got a memo that spent its energy on a spread that can't kill you and capped the thing that can.

On the discipline the memo claims

I want to be direct about the framing in the first paragraph, because it invokes Charlie's authority and I don't think it's earned.

Saying "too hard" is a wonderful discipline. But the discipline is about not starting. Charlie's edge was refusing to open the file. This memo opened the file, read twelve expert reports, built a bridge, ran a stress test, computed a hurdle to one decimal, priced a control discount to the dollar, and reached a central value of $58.20 against a price of $89.80. At that point you have an opinion, and you owe the reader the sentence. The sentence is: at my central estimate this is 54% overvalued; the range is wide enough that I could be wrong; I'm passing.

That's a real verdict. It can be checked against reality in two years. TOO UNCERTAIN cannot. And I've noticed over a long life that the conclusions which can never be wrong are also the ones that never taught anybody anything.

At $89.80, the market is paying $31.11 billion for the SpaceX stake. SpaceX itself paid about $21.6 billion of its own stock for that spectrum, on September 2025 metrics. So the market is asking you to believe SpaceX has appreciated about 44% since September 2025, and to accept a 90%-controlled wrapper, no dividend, no vote, two bankrupt subsidiaries and a veil-piercing auction as the delivery vehicle.

You may think 44% is conservative for that company over that period. You may think it's rich. Either way, that is a question a person can have an opinion about, and it took me one paragraph and no new data to ask it. It is not in the memo.

(i) It is circular. Under ASC 321, an equity security in a private issuer without a readily determinable fair value is carried at the transaction price of the exchange that created it. So:

carrying value after the exchange  ≡  fair value of consideration received  ≡  transaction price T
gain recognised                     =  T − (book basis of spectrum surrendered)
therefore:  book basis + gain       ≡  T           — by construction, for any T

The identity $11.1B + $8.46B ≈ $19.6B cannot corroborate the $19.6B transaction price, because under GAAP the left-hand side is defined as the transaction price.
