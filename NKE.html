Munger's Verdict

MUNGER SYNTHESIS — NIKE, Inc. (NKE) — 2026-10-05 — Price $33.60

The data, as the dossier corrected it. The share count is 1,483.5M, the implied count. The reported 1,202.11M is a single share class, and any per-share figure built on "1.2B shares" is about 23% wrong. The 10-K weighted average of 1,479.8M corroborates the implied count, which puts market cap at about $49.9B. The pipeline's "TTM" row is FY26 (the year to 2026-05-31) exactly. Q1 FY27 comes from press-release snippets and a Zacks summary, not a filed release. FY26 contains the $986M IEEPA tariff recovery. Every multiple below names its earnings base. The pipeline labels this a cyclical/timing problem (p = 0.60): net margin fell from 11.1% (FY24) to 6.7% (FY26), and pipeline ROIC fell from 30.2% to 16.4%.

0. The sentence the council did not have

Every expert left two questions open: on which line the $986M refund was booked, and whether the $684M still owed at year-end sat in receivables. The 10-K's "Other Matters" paragraph, which is in the raw dossier but not in the refined one, answers both. Nike "recognized a benefit of $986 million in Cost of sales … for the recovery of IEEPA tariffs paid, for which $965 million and $21 million … was classified within North America and Converse, respectively, largely offsetting the impact of the IEEPA tariffs recognized during fiscal 2026." The $684M receivable is "reflected within Accounts receivable, net" [SEC: 10-K, raw dossier]. That has three consequences.

First, the booking inference is confirmed. Q4 FY26 printed a 49.2% gross margin against 40.3% a year earlier. Taking $986M out of $5,393M of gross profit gives 40.2% of $10,972M. FY26 gross margin is therefore 42.9% reported and ~40.8% ex-refund, against 42.7% in FY25.

Second, and more important, the refund was not a windfall on top of a normal year. It reversed a cost charged inside the same year. Reported FY26 is therefore approximately Nike's year without IEEPA tariffs: EBIT $3,850M (8.3%), net income $3,108M, EPS $2.10 diluted, and gross margin +20bp. The 10-K's margin bridge has no tariff line because the charge and the refund net out.

The council's "core" is EBIT $2,864M (6.2%), net income ~$2.32B, EPS ~$1.57 and gross margin ~40.8%. That is Nike's year with a full year of tariffs the Supreme Court ruled unauthorized on 2026-02-20. Neither base is "the" core. Which one recurs depends on replacement tariffs, and the dossier is silent on those (Event C). The swing is ~$0.79B after tax, ~$0.53 a share at the 20.3% rate. The threat register cites 46% (Vietnam) and 32% (Indonesia) rates and a later 20%/40% US–Vietnam deal [MEDIA]. My belief that these were IEEPA-era rates is background knowledge, not in the dossier. Whatever applies now is not in the dossier either, so §4 carries the tariff regime as an explicit, varied grid input.

The Psychologist, Historian and Sherlock each read the CEO letter's "gross margins slightly expanded" as resting on the refund. On a tariff-neutral basis margins did slightly expand. The fair charge is narrower: the letter leaves out that the nine-month margin (41.0% vs 43.5%) and Q3 (40.2% vs 41.5%) carried both the tariff and the discounts.

The cash reading changes the same way. FY26 FCF of $2.18B paid the tariffs and got only $302M back before year-end. Tariff-neutral FY26 FCF is ~$2.87B [DERIVED: $2.18B + $0.684B received after May 31]. On that base the ~$2.43B dividend is a payout of 85% of FCF (FCF ÷ dividend 1.18x). On reported FCF it is 111% (0.90x). Owner earnings become ~$2.09B rather than $1.41B, an owner yield of 4.2% rather than 2.8%.

What the experts got wrong is asymmetry, not a double count. Ex-refund EPS ($1.57) and ex-refund-cash FCF ($1.88B) each charge FY26's IEEPA cost exactly once. Each is a legitimate tariff-burdened base, and I use $1.57 myself for the floor. The error is that the experts stripped the one-off credit and called the remainder "core" without saying that the same year carried the charge the credit reversed. That presents the tariff-burdened year as the clean one and the reported year as flattered.

The real double charge sits in the moat narrative (§1). Several experts read the tariff-driven fall in ex-refund gross margin (~190bp) as evidence that pricing power was lost. That charges the tariff once in the earnings base and again in the moat.

Third, the FY27 guide is below both bases. That is what stops me from turning the point into a bull case. The guide is revenue down "in the high-single-digit range" and adjusted EPS of $1.15–$1.35, excluding about $0.15 of Pace costs [MEDIA per Zacks]. The guide is annual. The raw Zacks text says CFO Denton "introduced a clearer financial framework, including full-year guidance" [MEDIA per Zacks, raw dossier]. Q1 earned $0.48 (vs a $0.43 consensus), which leaves $0.67–0.87 for Q2–Q4 combined, about $0.22–0.29 a quarter.

The guide is consistent with more than one decomposition, and it does not tell me the tariff regime. All of the following run FY26 through an 8% revenue decline ($46.4B to ~$42.7B), hold SG&A at $16.11B (it was $16.09B the year before), add $50M of net interest income and tax at 20.3% [DERIVED]:

On the like-for-like adjusted basis, the guide ($1.15–1.35) sits between the two tariff cases. At least three readings fit it about equally well:

The strongest evidence for those levers is one the council underweighted. North America is where the 10-K says the reset "has made the most progress", and the playbook worked there:

  • Revenue $20,511M, +4.8% (+5% currency-neutral); wholesale +14%; footwear units +6%.
  • Gross margin +210bp and reported EBIT $5,376M (26.2%), up 14% [SEC: raw 10-K segment discussion].

North America took $965M of the refund, so its "ex-refund" 21.5% against 24.2% is the tariff-burdened lens. On a tariff-neutral basis it improved. The same lens produces the "four geographies flat at $10,458M, ex-refund $9,493M (−9%)" reading. North America is the only segment where the playbook has fully run, and it is also where Performance ($16B, growing high single digits [MEDIA per Zacks]; ~34.5% of revenue [CALC]) lives.

The counter-evidence is real. Wholesale overall slowed to −1% in Q1 FY27. Q1 revenue was $11,213M (−4%, −5% currency-neutral), and NIKE Brand ($10.95B) fell −4% "primarily due to declines in Greater China and EMEA". The channel split is now 60.5% wholesale and 39.2% direct, against 57.8% and 42.1% in FY25 [CALC from SEC]. That reverses the 2020 Consumer Direct Acceleration, and the 10-K's own revenue trend is $51.4B, $46.3B, $46.4B.

4. Valuation: stress test, scenario grid, required growth

Stress test. The dossier has no STRESS TEST block (scorecard item 10 ❌), so I use Burry's. With SG&A fixed and FY26 ex-refund FCF as the base, FCF turns negative after a ~10–13% revenue decline. At −20% it is roughly −$1.1B to −$1.9B before a $2.43B dividend. On the tariff-neutral base ($2.87B FCF at a 42.9% gross margin) the zero-FCF point moves out to ~18% [DERIVED].

The FY27 guide (a high-single-digit decline) sits just inside the first threshold. The business survives a 20% revenue drop on its balance sheet: $9.03B of cash and short-term investments against $7.94B of borrowings, net cash $1.08B (maturities are not in the dossier). The dividend would not survive it.

FY26 cash was back-loaded. FCF by quarter was $0.01B, $0.39B, $0.28B and $1.50B, on revenue of $11.72B, $12.43B, $11.28B and $10.97B. Q4 was 69% of the $2.18B, which came from $2.87B of OCF less $0.68B of capex (5% of revenue). FCF per share went $3.14, $4.36, $2.20 and $1.47 (FY23–26).

0. Is this a "disruption" at all?

Nike is not facing a Kodak-style technology substitution. The dossier points to three separate pressures:

  1. A self-inflicted channel error. The 2020 Consumer Direct Acceleration cut Amazon, Zappos, Belk and Dillard's [MEDIA]. Direct fell from 42.1% to 39.2% of revenue and wholesale rose from 57.8% to 60.5% [CALC from SEC], so the reversal is well under way.
  2. Category attackers. On and Hoka hold "roughly 19% combined share of the U.S. premium running market" [MEDIA per FinancialContent]. Anta and Li-Ning are taking share in China. The Moat Threat Search puts Anta at 15% of China sportswear against Nike at 23% (Adidas 20%) and says Anta and Li-Ning together hold 28% of China sneaker sales [MEDIA].
  3. Policy shocks. Tariffs on a footwear base that is 52% Vietnam, 27% Indonesia and 16% China [SEC].

So the right analogues are brand-cycle and channel-mistake companies (Reebok, Converse, Under Armour; Adidas, Coach), not Kodak or BlackBerry. The casualty pattern that matters is losing the core category to a focused rival while managing for the P&L.

1. Dead analogues (the casualties)

Reebok, 1987 to 2005 [BACKGROUND]. - Reebok passed Nike in US athletic footwear around 1987 on the aerobics boom, and Nike retook the lead within about two years. - Reebok then chased fashion and celebrity endorsements instead of owning a performance category. It never rebuilt a core and was sold to Adidas for roughly $3.8B in 2005-06. - The decline took 10 to 15 years, not a collapse. Revenue stayed large while the franchise became a second-tier brand. - Lesson for Nike: the damage was never a single bad year. It was share loss in the category that defined the brand, hidden by a still-large top line.

Converse, 1970s to 2001 [BACKGROUND]. - It was the default basketball shoe, then lost that category to Nike and filed Chapter 11 in 2001. Nike bought it in 2003 for roughly $305M. - The decline took about 20 years. The final stage came after repeated ownership and strategy changes. - Nike now owns this failure mode: Converse revenue fell 31% in FY26 to $1,174M, EBIT $18M (1.5%) vs $240M (14.2%) in FY25, and the drag is expected "throughout fiscal 2027" [SEC]. Converse is only 2.3% of Q1 FY27 revenue [CALC], so it is a footnote to Nike's numbers and not what drives them.

Under Armour, 2016 to date [BACKGROUND]. - It peaked on growth near $5B of revenue in 2016-17 and has since gone through several restructurings and CEO changes while revenue went nowhere. - It is not dead, but it is the best example of a "zombie": a programme is announced, the savings are booked, and the brand keeps weakening. - Nike's Win Now plan slipped a year, from end-2025 to end-2026 [MEDIA per Front Office Sports]. Pace is layered on top, with "~$1B implementation cost for ~$2.5B savings" and restructuring pressure "into fiscal 2028" [MEDIA per Zacks]. That is the shape of the Under Armour pattern.

2. Survivor analogues (the adapters)

Adidas, 2022 to 2025 [BACKGROUND]. - It ended the Yeezy partnership in October 2022, took a large inventory and earnings hit, and reported roughly break-even results in 2023. - A new CEO then rebuilt wholesale relationships and made the terrace and lifestyle models (Samba, Gazelle) the new fashion cycle, and operating profit recovered in 2024-25. - Recovery took about 2 to 3 years from the shock. The dossier shows the effect on Nike: Adidas "surged back in lifestyle" and Citi/AlixPartners surveys show Nike losing mind share [MEDIA]. - Nike's own wholesale re-entry copies this playbook, and Adidas is a competitor that already ran it.

Coach, FY2013 to FY2017 [BACKGROUND]. - Over-distribution through outlets and heavy promotion diluted the brand, and sales fell roughly 17% from about $5.1B to about $4.2B. - Management cut promotional exposure, pulled back from discount channels and took the short-term hit. - Recovery took about 3 to 4 years from the decision to the new base. - Nike's FY26 revenue is down 9.7% from the FY24 peak of $51.4B [CALC]. If FY27 falls "in the high-single-digit range" [MEDIA per Zacks], that implies about $42.7B, roughly 17% below peak [CALC on an unverified guide]. That matches Coach's peak-to-trough, though a similar percentage proves nothing on its own.

Levi's, mid-1990s onward [BACKGROUND]. Treat this as a partial survivor. Revenue fell by about 40% over a decade and the company was rebuilt at a smaller scale. A brand can survive and still leave shareholders with years of poor returns.

Base rate across the five analogues. Two recovered fully (Adidas, Coach), two never regained their prior position (Reebok, Under Armour), and Converse did not survive as an independent company. That is roughly 40 to 50% full recovery. It is the prior I use, and it is judgment from recall, not a measured statistic.

1. Cultural role

Nike is a status symbol and a ritual at once, which is rare, but the two are decoupling.

  • Ritual / default. Nike tops teens' shopping lists and carries Gen Z and Millennial consideration of 55.4% and 52.6% [MEDIA per YouGov, Piper Sandler]. Performance, the part of the business tied to training and sport as a habit, is $16B (~34.5% of FY26 revenue [CALC]) and growing high single digits [MEDIA per Zacks]. This is the ritual layer, and it is working.
  • Status / fashion cachet. This layer is where the leak is. Adidas "surged back in lifestyle" (Samba, luxury collaborations) and surveys show Nike losing mind share [MEDIA per Citi/AlixPartners]. The dossier says Sportswear and Jordan Brand "remained under pressure" [MEDIA per Zacks]. Status goods are priced by scarcity and heat, not by ubiquity. Nike spent 2020-2023 flooding channels with Dunk and Air Force 1 volume; the cultural cost of that is now being paid in markdowns ("increased markdowns and higher wholesale sales returns and discounts" [SEC: 10-K]).
  • Cultural verb? "Just do it" and the swoosh are among the few brand marks that work without the company name. That is a real asset, but the dossier contains no NPS, retention or search-share data to show the verb is still as strong as it was (scorecard item 7 flags the absence).
  • Cultural moments. Nike is embedded in school sport, running, basketball and the Olympic cycle. These are recurring, calendar-driven moments, which is structurally better than a single trend.

Judgment: the ritual layer is intact, the status layer is cooling, and the company is currently spending its status capital (discounting) to clear inventory. Inventory is flat at $7.5B, DIO ~103 days [SEC/CALC], so the clearing is not yet complete in a way the balance sheet proves.

2. Tribal dynamics

Tribes (from the dossier): teens and Gen Z (the default tribe), serious runners and athletes (Performance), sneakerheads and fashion buyers (Sportswear/Jordan, the tribe that has drifted to Adidas, On and collabs), older cohorts (Levi's and Skechers lead there [MEDIA]), and Chinese consumers (the tribe that has defected most visibly).

Captive vs Devotee test. Nike is not a prison. There is no switching cost beyond taste: a shopper who wears Nike on Monday can wear Hoka on Tuesday. The line "run in Hoka, train in Nike, live in On" [MEDIA] is the single most revealing cultural datum in the dossier. It describes situational loyalty: the consumer rents Nike for particular occasions. That is a community in the sense of a default, not a community in the sense of a tribe that defends the brand. Devotee is the right label in the US and with teens. Defection is possible and visible elsewhere. This matters for the moat: communities survive disruption, but a default can be dislodged by one product cycle, and Nike's lead time is long (up to two years of design lead time on footwear [MEDIA per Markets Insider], so a misread of taste costs two seasons).

Gatekeepers. Retailers were the gatekeepers Nike chose to cut in 2020 (Amazon, Zappos, Belk, Dillard's [MEDIA]) and is now re-courting. Wholesale was 60.5% of FY26 revenue vs 57.8% in FY25 [CALC from SEC]. The gatekeepers are also consolidating: Dick's agreed to acquire Foot Locker for $2.4B [MEDIA per CNBC]. Influencer and athlete culture matters more than retail here, and the dossier says nothing about athlete roster health.

Resentment signals. The proposed class action alleging Nike kept tariff-related price increases after receiving tariff refunds is [proposed status, no ruling in the dossier]. It is minor as a legal matter, but it is a trust datum: a brand that charged premium prices to teens and then booked a $986M refund [SEC: 10-K] is exposed on the narrative that it is extracting rent. The greenwashing suit was dismissed [enacted: motion to dismiss granted]. Labor allegations in Indonesia are recurring, not new.

Nike's pricing power is being used in reverse: FY26 included "increased markdowns and higher wholesale sales returns and discounts" [SEC]. Nike is a former keystone in convalescence. For three decades the brand was the organising node of the sneaker ecosystem: wholesale partners, athletes, suppliers and rival brands all arranged themselves around it. The evidence that it still holds that role: - Gen Z / Millennial consideration 55.4% / 52.6% [MEDIA per YouGov, Piper Sandler]. - Performance category ~$16B (~34.5% of FY26 revenue [CALC]), growing high single digits [MEDIA per Zacks]. - North America growth in FY26 (+4.8% [SEC]) and again in Q1 FY27 [SEC].

A keystone's removal collapses the ecosystem. Remove Nike and the shelf space is taken immediately by Adidas, On, Hoka, New Balance and Anta, which means Nike is replaceable at the margin. That makes it a dominant species rather than a true keystone.

Premium running and performance (On, Hoka). On and Hoka hold "roughly 19% combined share of the U.S. premium running market" [MEDIA per FinancialContent]. China (Anta, Li-Ning). - Sources in the threat register disagree on levels: Anta 15% vs Nike 23% of China sportswear, and Anta plus Li-Ning 28% of sneaker sales [MEDIA, undated], alongside a headline that "Anta overtook Nike." - The direction is clear. Colonisation speed. From the dossier: the 2020-2024 withdrawal to the 2026 share loss is roughly five to six years. Test (c) — the 10-K's tariff sentence, against the raw dossier

The quotation is genuine. Raw dossier line 579 reads verbatim: "we recognized a benefit of $986 million in Cost of sales … largely offsetting the impact of the IEEPA tariffs recognized during fiscal 2026. As of May 31, 2026, we received $302 million and recorded $684 million of outstanding IEEPA tariff receivables reflected within Accounts receivable, net".

The netting reading is corroborated twice in the raw 10-K. - The consolidated gross-margin bridge (lines 1059–1070) runs ±20bp items only: warehousing, FX, other, Converse and product costs. It has no refund line and no tariff line, although the refund alone is ~210bp of margin. - The North America bridge (line 1668) attributes +210bp to "lower product costs … and higher ASP".

A windfall on top of a tariff-burdened year would have to appear in those bridges, and it does not. The memo's core claim therefore holds: reported FY26 ($2.10) is approximately the IEEPA-neutral year. "Largely" means a small residual IEEPA cost stays inside it.

Two Jev "promotions" are therefore false positives: the booking in cost of sales, and the receivable inside the $5.93B. The raw 10-K states both.

The "double count" is not supported. - Ex-refund $1.57 removes the offset and keeps FY26's recognized tariff charge once. That is the tariff-burdened year, a legitimate base if replacement tariffs cost about the same. - Subtracting $302M of refund cash from owner earnings ($1.41B = OCF − D&A − SBC; OCF already paid the tariffs) also leaves the tariff charged once.
