Berkshire Hathaway as an investment

A wide moat priced for a Buffett-era halo, on a company Abel has run for three quarters.

I would wait, not buy: my central value is $452 a share, the ceiling of the buy zone is $430, and the floor is $3501, against a price of $507.821 — the stock only earns today's price in the sum-of-the-parts' most favorable corner. The central risk: eleven of twelve council views reduce to one man deploying $380B of idle cash well2, and that man has run the company for three quarters3. The paradox: rail and utility are genuinely hard to replicate, yet GEICO is losing share on a one-click comparison4, and the gap between price and value is a trust premium earned by a predecessor, not yet re-earned by a successor. What you would own

Four largely uncorrelated businesses: an insurance group (GEICO auto, BH Primary, BHRG reinsurance) whose underwriting funds the whole company with cheap float; BNSF, a transcontinental railroad nobody can rebuild; Berkshire Hathaway Energy's franchised utility assets; and a manufacturing/service/retail collection plus a roughly $320–355B equity portfolio and about $380B of cash5. The moat is uneven — rail and utility barriers are physical and regulatory, GEICO's is brand recall against a data-driven rival, and reinsurance capital now exceeds $700B, compressing BHRG's pricing6. You are buying four separate toll roads, one of which is now less protected than it was.

Cash flow and shareholder economics

Owner yield is 3.1% against an 8–9% cost of equity10; free-cash-flow yield is lower, about 2.0%, because an insurer's operating cash flow swings with claim timing and realized-gain taxes. There is no dividend, so every point of required return must come from per-share value growth or buybacks.

The bull case and the counterargument

The strongest bull case: Abel compounds cheap float and dry powder, and buybacks already reaccelerated from about $235M in Q1 2026 to about $4.5B in Q212. The counterargument is economic: every owner-yield trigger the minority produced ($247–$385) capitalizes T-bill interest at an equity multiple — worth about 40 cents on the dollar that way, versus holding cash at par, which adds roughly $27 a share13. Evidence cuts both ways: GEICO's loss ratio improved from 81.0% to 72.3% even while losing share to Progressive (11.56% vs. 18.6–19.8%)414. The moat weakens further if GEICO's price disadvantage persists or the reinsurance glut proves structural.

Capital allocation deserves its own test

Berkshire repurchased about $4.8B in Q2 202619, after $7.85B, $9.17B and $2.92B in 2022–202420. The council's standard: repurchases below intrinsic value are informative, not conclusive, because the company's own alternative use of that cash is T-bills at ~3.3% after tax — below the 8–9% an outside buyer requires21. My interpretation: the insider buying and my own waiting can both be rational at the same price, since we face different opportunity costs. The warning: H1 2026's buyback was about 0.4% of market value and roughly 1% of the cash pile21 — a nibble, not the heavy buying Buffett did when he judged the stock clearly cheap.
