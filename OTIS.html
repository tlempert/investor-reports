WHAT I WITHDRAW FROM REVISION 1

Before anything else, the corrections. A man who opens by convicting a council of doing one division twelve times had better publish his own errors first.

1. The Private Buyer Test — struck entirely. I wrote that private market value was $28–33B EV against a $34.6B public EV, and concluded "there is no takeout put under this stock." That was my own ceiling wearing a hat: 15 × $1.60B + $6.96B net debt = $30.9B, the dead centre of the range I "found." Both decisive inputs were invented — the 12–14x multiple appears nowhere in the record (Section M reads "No peer comparison data found"), and the $0 I assigned New Equipment ignores $240M of FY2025 segment profit. Run the same multiple on total segment profit and you get $31–37B, which brackets the public EV. A test that cannot fail upward is a mirror with a frame around it. The record contains no comparable transaction, so I have no private-market view. "Delete 'someone will take it out'" is itself deleted.

2. "Levered like an LBO" — false, and I used a number I had already struck. Net debt/EBITDA is 3.0x with 10.9x interest coverage. That is a BBB industrial. Worse, I dismissed negative equity as a Carrier spin-off artifact when it inflated ROIC and then spent it as evidence of leverage when it justified a higher discount rate — on the one variable I called decisive. You cannot strike a number for being meaningless and then spend it. The balance sheet does not support the top of the hurdle band.

3. "Valuing on cash sidesteps the German item" — the mirror of the error I caught. Otis's own FY2025 reconciliation subtracts $216M of German tax refund from free cash flow and states refunds "continue into 2026." Cash is more exposed than earnings, not less. So my headline 6.4% owner yield was the contaminated peak. The correct figure is 5.8%, on the normalized stream — and this correction cuts against my own bull framing, not for it.

4. The feedstock argument was ~2x overstated by my own arithmetic. 83,000 units installed against ~96,000 net portfolio adds, before any churn. Otis adds more elevators to its book than it manufactures — it wins units it did not build. A 5% order decline is roughly a 2.5% cut to gross feedstock. This holds at any churn rate and partially rescues Lynch. ($60k/unit is my assumption, not in the record; it cross-checks at 83k/735k = 11.3% global unit share against Otis's ~11% installed-base share.)

5. My PASS characterization was a straw man. Every PASS expert published a trigger price and a starter position defensible in the band. They said not at this price, not not at any price. The label was wrong; the reasoning was not.

6. Two experts contributed original arguments, not one. Lynch's mix accretion, and the Historian's R&D-versus-buyback capital-allocation construction, which I adopted as my own "real complaint" while denying its existence on another page. And the identical bull case is 11 of 12, not 12 of 12.

THE 50% FACT — NOT HOLLOWING OUT, NOT A PLATEAU EITHER

Independents hold ~50% of Otis's serviced units by count but materially less by value. It is a level with no time series. The flow statistics, which the refined dossier dropped and no expert saw, are stronger: Otis grew its maintenance portfolio 4% for the fourth consecutive year to ~2.5M units, at a record 25.1% Service margin (FY2025).

But I quoted the numerator and skipped the denominator sitting two sentences above it: the global installed base grew mid-single-digit to 23 million units. Otis is 2.5M/23M = 10.9%, against ~11.0% a year earlier. Growing 4% into a base growing 5%, with $109M of purchased portfolio inside that 4%, means Otis's share of the world's elevators is flat to slightly falling. And churn is a named, live offset in Otis's own bridge.

The correct reading is narrower than mine was: a slow relative fade with the value premium intact. It still refutes the three experts who called erosion the base case. It does not support "plateau."

The “No” Test — Focus

Otis passes the focus test.

This is not a collection of 50 unrelated products. Its business forms one coherent lifecycle:

  1. Install elevators and escalators.
  2. Maintain and repair them.
  3. Modernize them as they age.

Service represented 59% of revenue in 2023—$8.397B of $14.209B [SEC per 10-K]. New Equipment creates installed units; Service monetizes them repeatedly; Modernization renews them. Each activity reinforces the same installed base.

The problem is not product sprawl. It is that the weakest business—New Equipment—is the gateway to the strongest one.

The Love Metric — Love or Captivity?

The dossier provides neither NPS nor a numerical NRR or churn rate. That absence matters: there is no direct evidence that customers love Otis.

What we do have is mixed:

  • Q1 2026 maintenance pricing was approximately +3%, and portfolio growth was +3%, partially offset by churn [MEDIA per Q1 2026 call].
  • Service organic growth accelerated from +5% in Q1 [MEDIA] to +9% in Q2 [SEC].
  • Repair organic growth reached +10% [MEDIA].
  • Yet Otis itself identifies cancellations and retention, while independent providers can reportedly charge 20–40% less on non-proprietary equipment [SEARCH per Otis vs KONE vs Schindler vs TK Elevator: Ranked (2026)].

Elevator maintenance is mandatory, failure-sensitive, and protected by proprietary controllers, parts, and software. That produces retention, but retention is not love. Customers may stay because changing providers is risky or inconvenient.

The private-residence elevator recall, involving serious injuries [MEDIA per CPSC], also damages the trust required for an “insanely great” product.

The Integration — Does Otis Own the Widget?

Mostly, yes.

Otis controls:

  • Physical elevator platforms.
  • Proprietary controllers and components.
  • Installation.
  • Maintenance and repair.
  • Modernization.
  • Otis ONE real-time monitoring and diagnostics [SEARCH per Otis Unveils New Generation of Digitally Native Elevator Platforms].
  • A 2.4 million-unit installed base [MEDIA per Q1 2026 call].

That integration creates genuine advantages. Proprietary technology raises switching costs, while service-route density supports scale economics. Otis behaves like a landlord in its own installed base rather than merely renting access to another company’s platform.

But its control is not absolute. Independent providers already service a large aggregate unit portfolio, particularly after warranties expire [SEARCH per the 2025 Otis Annual Report]. Right-to-repair requirements may open documentation, tools, software, parts, and diagnostics to competitors. Otis owns the widget today, but regulators and independents are trying to unlock it.

The Next Big Thing — Creating the Future?

No. Otis is improving an old, necessary system rather than creating a new market.

Modernization is the most attractive growth vector:

  • Orders increased 9% in constant currency [SEC per Q2 2026].
  • Backlog increased 26% in constant currency [SEC].
  • Management describes the modernization market as growing double digits, supported by aging equipment and Chinese stimulus [MEDIA].

But those drivers—aging equipment, regulation, and stimulus—would exist without Otis. Remote diagnostics and predictive maintenance improve productivity, yet Mitsubishi and other competitors possess comparable systems [SEARCH per Mitsubishi/Mendix and Transforma Insights].

Five years of revenue near $14B further contradicts a market-creation narrative. Otis is digitizing and extending the life of the installed world, not inventing a new one.

The Decisive Operating Evidence

The best discriminator is the organic growth spread between Service and New Equipment because it directly tests whether the annuity is becoming large enough to overcome installation weakness.

In Q2 2026:

  • Service organic growth: +9% [SEC].
  • New Equipment organic growth: −1% [SEC].
  • Spread: 10 percentage points.

That is encouraging, especially with Service already representing 59% of 2023 revenue [SEC]. But New Equipment economics are deteriorating more sharply than the consolidated sales figure suggests: Q1 margin fell 240 basis points to 3.3%, and operating profit declined $27M in constant currency [MEDIA]. Q2 guidance was subsequently reduced for operating profit and EPS [MEDIA].

The Service engine is winning the revenue race. It has not yet demonstrated that it can completely neutralize New Equipment’s margin deterioration.

Financial Reality and Valuation

I exclude the German tax-litigation item from normalized earnings. TTM net income of $1.52B includes a roughly $191M net benefit—about 13% of the reported figure—so normalized TTM net income is approximately $1.33B [CALC from dossier figures].

I do not use the reported 82–160% ROIC as evidence of quality. It is a denominator artifact caused by negative equity of approximately $5.51B [MEDIA], rooted in the 2020 separation [SEC]. It says little about the economic return of the operating business.

At $71.77:

  • Owner yield is 6.2% [CALC].
  • The appropriate local cost-of-equity hurdle is 8.0–10.0%, as specified in the dossier.
  • The price is 23% above the $58.22 optimistic DCF anchor [CALC], even though that model assumes 7.3% growth against five years of essentially flat revenue.

The 6.2% owner yield is inadequate against an 8.0–10.0% required return. Holding owner earnings constant, an 8% yield implies approximately $56 per share; a 10% yield implies approximately $45. That is the range where the investment verdict begins to change.

Otis has focus, integration, and a strong recurring-service machine. What it lacks is evidence of customer love, genuine market creation, and an adequate current return for the risks.

Warren Buffett — Moat & Ecosystem Analysis

Bottom line

Otis is neither an inevitable fortress nor a dying cigar butt. It owns a valuable, durable service franchise attached to a cyclical and increasingly competitive equipment business.

The installed base, route density, safety requirements, and recurring maintenance need are real structural advantages. But proprietary lock-in is vulnerable to independent servicers and right-to-repair intervention. At $71.77, the price demands more growth than five years of flat revenue justify.

This is a good franchise at an unfair price.

1. The anti-fragile moat

The Chaos Test

Otis is resilient, but not truly anti-fragile.

Every installed elevator must continue operating, passing inspections, and receiving maintenance. Owners cannot defer failures indefinitely without shutdowns, fines, tenant complaints, and liability exposure [SEARCH, Elevator Code Compliance: Requirements & Checklist Guide]. This makes the service stream unusually defensive.

The numbers confirm that resilience:

  • Service represented $8.397B, or 59% of 2023 revenue [SEC, 10-K].
  • Service organic growth was +5% in Q1 2026 [MEDIA, Q1 call] and +9% in Q2 [SEC, Q2 release].
  • Q1 maintenance pricing was approximately +3%, while the portfolio grew +3% [MEDIA, Q1 call].
  • Modernization orders rose +9%, with backlog up +26% constant currency [SEC, Q2 release].

But economic stress damages the acquisition engine. New Equipment organic sales fell 1%, orders fell 5%, and Asia-Pacific orders declined more than 20% [SEC, Q2 release]. China sales fell more than 20% [MEDIA, Q2 report].

When construction competitors fail, Otis may capture installed units and service contracts. Yet recessions also reduce new installations. The service annuity absorbs chaos; the total company does not necessarily become stronger from it.

The $1B Competitor Test

A $1B challenger could hurt Otis locally, but could not reproduce its global ecosystem quickly.

The defensive assets are:

  • A 2.4 million-unit installed base [MEDIA, Q1 2026 call].
  • Dense technician routes that lower travel and response costs.
  • Proprietary controllers, diagnostic systems, parts, and service knowledge.
  • Nationwide coverage and leading market share [SEARCH].
  • Long-lived relationships with building owners and facility managers.

A challenger cannot purchase route density or field knowledge overnight. However, the challenger does not need to kill Otis outright. Regional independents reportedly offer service on non-proprietary equipment for 20–40% less than OEMs [SEARCH, Otis vs KONE vs Schindler vs TK Elevator: Ranked (2026)]. Independents collectively service about half of industry units, although their share by value is smaller [SEARCH, 2025 Otis Annual Report].

Verdict: $1B cannot destroy the ecosystem, but it can accelerate service unbundling and selectively take profitable older units.

Pricing power

Otis has demonstrated current, but bounded, pricing power.

Approximately +3% Q1 service pricing occurred alongside +3% portfolio growth [MEDIA, Q1 call]. That is the clearest positive moat evidence: prices rose and the portfolio still expanded.

However, Otis contracts are reportedly $6,000–12,000 annually versus $5,500–11,000 for KONE and Schindler [SEARCH, Elevator Blueprint]. Management also identifies churn as an offset [MEDIA, Q1 call]. Customers are therefore accepting price increases, but not without competitive resistance.

The conclusion is modest pricing power in essential service—not the unconstrained pricing power of an unquestioned monopoly.

2. Strategic evolution

Feature absorption: the Borg Check

Remote diagnostics are more likely to strengthen Otis than replace it.

Otis ONE monitors equipment health and performance continuously [SEARCH, Otis Unveils New Generation of Digitally Native Elevator Platforms]. Predictive monitoring can reduce emergency callouts, improve technician productivity, and make failures more predictable. It also deepens the information advantage of whoever controls the equipment data.

This is successful feature absorption: digital monitoring becomes part of the service contract.

The danger is that industry-standard sensors and diagnostics could eventually lower the skill and information advantages of OEM maintenance. Trials elsewhere have reportedly improved repair and maintenance labor productivity by 25% [SEARCH, Transforma Insights]. If independent servicers obtain equivalent data and software access, digitalization could commoditize some diagnostic work.

For now, Otis is absorbing the threat. Regulation could later force it to share the benefit.

Commoditizing the complement

There is no evidence in the dossier that Otis is deliberately making adjacent technologies cheaper or open to expand its core monopoly.

Its strategy appears closer to controlling the complement: proprietary controllers, parts, software, and diagnostic access help preserve service retention. That works economically but increases regulatory exposure. EU and proposed US right-to-repair rules may require access to documentation, tools, software, and parts [SEARCH, EU Right to Repair Directive: What You Need to Know].

This is an effective moat today, but it is less permanent than route density, safety expertise, or the physical installed base.

5. The Yellow Pages Test

Otis does not resemble Yellow Pages at the fundamental level.

Yellow Pages depended on information scarcity and controlled distribution. Elevators are physical, safety-critical assets with mandated inspections, specialized labor, and expensive failure consequences. Those needs do not disappear because software improves.

The moat nevertheless has permanent and temporary components.

Permanent or long-lived:

  • Elevators require recurring physical maintenance.
  • Safety and inspection requirements create non-discretionary demand.
  • Route density creates genuine scale economies.
  • Skilled technicians are scarce; reported annual pay is $106,580 versus $49,500 for all occupations [SEARCH, US Bureau of Labor Statistics].
  • The installed base ages into repair and modernization demand.

Potentially temporary:

  • Proprietary access to software and diagnostic tools.
  • Restrictions on independent parts and documentation.
  • Warranty-driven service retention.
  • Customer habit and fear of switching providers.

The principal decay pattern would be Otis retaining nominal unit share while independents capture higher-margin repairs, force cheaper contracts, or obtain diagnostic access through regulation. Current +3% pricing and +3% portfolio growth [MEDIA, Q1 call] say that decay has not yet become dominant.

Cigar-butt price

Below approximately $45, the investment begins offering a 10% owner-yield margin sufficient to compensate for China, regulation, and aftermarket competition. Around $56, it only clears the low end of the local hurdle.

Definitive verdict

Otis has a moderate-to-strong, durable service moat, but it is not an inevitable fortress. The physical service ecosystem is lasting; proprietary lock-in is contestable and partially exposed to technological standardization and regulation.

It is not a cigar butt, and the moat is not presently showing Yellow Pages-style collapse. But at $71.77, normalized earnings and owner earnings do not meet the 8–10% local required return.

Moat quality: durable but contestable. Investment verdict: PASS until approximately $45–56, with the preferred entry nearer $45 for a full 10% hurdle.

5. Habit disruption analysis

Habit depth

The behavior is:

  • Recurring and effectively continuous, rather than a discretionary annual purchase.
  • Usually a passive default once an OEM maintenance relationship is established.
  • Reinforced by switching risk: building owners may value reliability, documentation and accountability more than the lowest possible price.

This makes the habit strong, but not permanent. Buildings aging beyond warranty represent a natural reconsideration point.

What could break the habit?

A 10x better alternative is unlikely to mean a radically better elevator. It would more plausibly be an independent servicer offering equivalent uptime, faster response, transparent diagnostics and materially lower cost. Reported independent discounts of 20–40% [SEARCH] are meaningful, although not automatically “10x better.”

Forced migration is the most serious trigger. Right-to-repair requirements that open software, diagnostic tools, documentation and spare parts would directly weaken proprietary lock-in.

Trust violation is also material because elevator safety is non-negotiable. The CPSC recall involving entrapment injuries—including spinal, abdominal and hip injuries [MEDIA per CPSC]—is serious. The units were older residential products, however, and the dossier does not establish widespread customer defections.

  • Modernization backlog: up 26% constant currency [SEC].

My discriminating evidence is Service portfolio growth—not total Service revenue—because pricing and repair activity can temporarily conceal lost maintenance units. A persistent decline in unit retention would reveal actual habit erosion. At present, positive portfolio growth suggests the customer habit is bending, not breaking.

“My kids don’t use it” test

This test is favorable for Otis but applies differently than it would to a consumer brand. Future generations will continue to use elevators; building owners, property managers and regulators choose the service provider.

Younger decision-makers may be less loyal to an OEM name and more receptive to open systems, performance dashboards and flexible service contracts. The underlying elevator habit is generationally durable; the habit of paying the original manufacturer for maintenance is less secure.

Financial integrity and hurdle discipline

I do not use the reported 82–160% ROIC as evidence of quality. It is distorted by Otis’s negative $5.51B equity position [MEDIA], rooted in the 2020 separation and accumulated deficit [SEC]. The denominator does not represent a normal economic capital base.

Management’s behavioral signals are better than the stock’s valuation. Leadership reports uncomfortable details, revises guidance downward when necessary and anchors its narrative in operational metrics. The main concern is premature optimism around China and a history of substantial repurchases at prices that now appear demanding.

Why the persona's default analogue list doesn't fit

Kodak, Blockbuster, and MySpace are all demand-substitution stories — a new product category made the old one unwanted (digital photos, streaming, Facebook). Otis has no such threat: nobody stops needing elevators serviced. Otis's threat, as the dossier's own Threat Register lays out, is a value-chain unbundling story — an OEM that sells a capital good cheap (or at cost) and monetizes it for decades through a captive, high-margin aftermarket, facing independents who attack specifically the aftermarket while leaving the core product demand untouched. That is a different animal with a different, well-documented history: razor-and-blade or "installed base" businesses where third parties broke into the blade. I'm substituting analogues from that lineage.

1. CASUALTY-PATTERN ANALOGUES (Aftermarket Erosion, Not Corporate Death)

None of these companies died. That is itself the first finding — worth flagging before the case studies, not after.

Eastman Kodak's copier/micrographics equipment servicing arm — Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services (S. Ct. 1992). This is not the film-Kodak story; it is Kodak's separate business-equipment division, which tied replacement parts for its copiers to its own service technicians, freezing out independent service organizations (ISOs). ISOs sued under antitrust law; the Supreme Court ruled against Kodak on the tying claim in 1992, forcing Kodak to sell parts to independents. Kodak's captive service margin in that division compressed steadily through the 1990s as ISOs captured share on older, out-of-warranty units. The business unit persisted for years afterward at lower profitability — it did not vanish, it de-rated.

IBM mainframe and peripheral maintenance, 1970s-1980s. Plug-compatible manufacturers and independent maintenance organizations (Itel, Memorex, Control Data, and others) undercut IBM's captive service and hardware-replacement revenue on IBM-architecture mainframes, while a standing federal antitrust suit (United States v. IBM, filed 1969, dropped 1982) constrained IBM's ability to bundle service with hardware to block them out. IBM kept its mainframe franchise, but the service-attach economics it enjoyed in the 1960s never fully returned — independents captured a durable structural share of the maintenance dollar, particularly on aging installed units.

Automotive OEM dealer service networks vs. independent garages, 1970s-present. The closest structural twin to Otis: a durable capital good (a car, an elevator) sold once, serviced for a decade-plus, with the OEM holding a proprietary-parts/diagnostics advantage early in the asset's life. Independent garages now perform the large majority of out-of-warranty vehicle service industry-wide; OEM dealers retain the profitable warranty-era years and complex diagnostics, then lose the aging fleet to independents. This transition took decades, not years, and OEM dealer networks never disappeared — they just stopped being the default choice once a vehicle aged past warranty. Right-to-repair legislation (Massachusetts Question 1, 2020; ongoing federal proposals) is the same regulatory vector named in Otis's own Threat Register #1, playing out in a directly comparable industry in real time.

Pattern across all three: independents win the old, simple, out-of-warranty segment of the installed base first. The OEM retains the new, complex, still-under-technology-advantage segment. None of these OEMs were destroyed. All of them saw service-line profitability compress over 10-20+ years as the independent-served share climbed toward a plateau, not toward 100%.

2. SURVIVOR-PATTERN ANALOGUES

Rolls-Royce civil aerospace — "Power-by-the-Hour" / TotalCare. Facing independent MRO (maintenance, repair, overhaul) shops that could service jet engines more cheaply than Rolls-Royce itself, Rolls-Royce responded not by cutting price to match independents but by owning the diagnostic data layer — engine health monitoring, proprietary performance models, predictive-maintenance IP — and re-selling the service contract as an outcomes subscription that an independent MRO structurally cannot replicate without the OEM's own engine models. This required sustained R&D and software investment ahead of the threat, not after it bit. It is the model Otis says it is emulating with Otis ONE (real-time, 24/7 equipment monitoring) — the strategic shape is right; the question below is whether the investment behind it is sized to match.

HP Inc. printer consumables. Facing third-party and remanufactured ink/toner cartridges, HP defended aftermarket share for two decades via firmware ("Dynamic Security") that detects and locks out non-HP cartridges, continually re-engineering the lockout faster than clone makers could defeat it. This is a survivor case by technical arms race rather than by service quality — HP has kept the large majority of its consumables revenue, but at the cost of recurring lawsuits, FTC scrutiny, and an active 2024 EU right-to-repair complaint. It is the closest precedent for what happens if Otis leans harder on proprietary-controller lock-in rather than on service value: it can work financially, but it invites exactly the regulatory attack the dossier's Threat Register #1 and #2 already flag as live.

John Deere — live, unresolved, and useful for calibration rather than a verdict. Deere invested heavily in remote diagnostics and precision-ag software as its value-add layer (the survivor move) while simultaneously restricting independent access to diagnostic tools (inviting the casualty-adjacent regulatory response). The FTC sued Deere over repair restrictions in January 2025; a 2023 memorandum of understanding with the American Farm Bureau Federation was signed but farmers say it wasn't fully honored, and the fight continues. Deere is neither clearly winning nor clearly losing this fight after roughly a decade of pressure — which is itself the most useful data point in this section: these fights run 10+ years without resolving, and a company can be simultaneously investing like a survivor and litigating like a casualty at the same time.

Otis Worldwide (OTIS) — The Business, The Trap, and The Price

August 21, 2026 · Price $71.77 · Verdict: WAIT · Buy zone $42–$58 · Position: 0%

1. What This Company Actually Does

Think of Otis as the world's largest razor-and-blades business, except the blade contract lasts fifty years instead of a month. Otis sells you the razor — the elevator itself — at a price so thin it barely covers the cost of making it. Then it signs you up for the blades: a maintenance contract that runs for the entire life of the building, at $6,000 to $12,000 per elevator per year, for as long as people keep riding up and down. Otis has about 2.5 million of these blade subscriptions running right now.

Here's what makes it more durable than a shaving company: you can switch razor brands at the drugstore in five seconds. You cannot swap elevator brands without ripping out proprietary controllers, rewiring the shaft, and shutting the building down for months. So once Otis installs the razor, it holds an enormous structural advantage in selling you decades of blades — even though the razor itself is a mediocre business that's currently shrinking.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

This is a clean analytical problem — the right answer sits in the numbers, not in a judgment call about the future of an industry. Both of Otis's businesses report quarterly, and whether the growing half (Service) is outrunning the shrinking half (New Equipment) is arithmetic you can check on the tape. No need to speculate about disruption or unknowable futures.

But clean-analytical doesn't mean easy — it means the trap is different. It isn't "we can't know"; it's "we calculated correctly and still got fooled," because one of the inputs was never a fact about the business to begin with.

Otis reports Return on Invested Capital of 82% to 160%, depending on the year. Read cold, that says: this is one of the greatest businesses ever built, turning every dollar of capital into another dollar and a half of profit. Except it isn't a business fact — it's an accounting artifact.

ROIC is profit divided by capital invested — equity plus debt. Otis's equity is negative $5.51 billion: total liabilities of $15.98 billion exceed total assets of $10.54 billion. That deficit isn't distress; it's a scar from Otis's 2020 spin-out from United Technologies, when the parent loaded the new company with debt but not a matching slug of equity. Divide a perfectly normal $1.5 billion of profit by a denominator that's near zero — or negative — and the ratio measures a mathematical accident, not quality. Otis is a genuinely good business. This particular number, the one that looks most impressive, tells you nothing at all.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

A reusable framework, not a one-off fix:

  1. Suspicion before admiration. Any return ratio that looks spectacular — 80%, 100%, 160% — deserves a raised eyebrow before it earns a raised glass. Great businesses post good returns; only broken math posts impossible ones. Ask first: what's in the denominator, and could it be near zero for a reason that has nothing to do with quality?
  2. Know the three things that shrink book equity toward zero. Spin-offs (Otis's 2020 separation handed it debt but not matching equity), aggressive buybacks (retiring shares shrinks the equity account even as the business thrives), and write-downs (a one-time loss that permanently dents the denominator). None of these tell you the business got better or worse at earning money — they just move the denominator.
  3. Reach for a denominator that survives. Don't abandon return analysis — substitute a sturdier yardstick. Return on total assets can't go negative. Or skip the balance sheet and compare cash flow to enterprise value — what the whole business would cost to buy, debt included.
  4. When one company runs two businesses, value them separately. Otis's New Equipment segment is low-margin and cyclical; its Service segment is a stable, recurring annuity. Blending them into one company-wide ratio or multiple hides that you're really looking at two investments wearing the same ticker.

Applied to Otis: the healthier read is that free cash flow has run $1.4–1.7 billion a year for five straight years — steady and real. That's the business. The 160% ROIC is scar tissue talking.

4. How They Make Money

Otis's economics run on a three-step ladder. First, install — sell and erect a new elevator, often near breakeven or at a thin single-digit margin, because winning the installation is really about winning the right to service it. Second, maintain — once installed, that elevator needs monthly inspection, safety compliance, and repair for as long as the building stands, and Otis charges $6,000 to $12,000 a year for it, a business with far higher margins than installation. Third, modernize — every 20 to 25 years an aging elevator needs its motors, controls, and cabs overhauled, which is effectively a second installation sale into a captive, already-serviced building.

The economics are inverted from what you'd expect: the "product" business loses money to win the "subscription" business, and the subscription is the profit engine. Service is now about 59% of Otis's revenue and growing every year; New Equipment is the other 41%, and it's currently shrinking.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

Otis's moat rests on three legs. First, an installed base of 2.5 million maintained units — a customer list decades in the making. Second, proprietary controllers: the software and electronics that run an Otis elevator are built around Otis parts and technicians, raising the cost of switching servicers. Third, route density — a technician already working three buildings on a block services a fourth more cheaply than an outsider starting cold.

The results show up in the numbers: the maintenance portfolio grew 4% for a fourth consecutive year, and Service margin hit a record 25.1% in the most recent fiscal year. Not a company coasting on an old moat — one still digging it deeper.

Two honest caveats. Independent servicers already hold roughly half of all serviced elevators by unit count, even if they capture less by dollar value; buildings that age out of warranty increasingly shop around. And the modernization backlog, the bull case's favorite statistic, is decelerating — from +30% constant currency at the end of 2025 to +26% at mid-2026. The moat is real, but it isn't widening on every front.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

The single risk worth losing sleep over: independent servicers converting their unit-count share into real pricing power. Today, holding half the units by count but a smaller slice by value means most independents compete for smaller, less complex buildings and haven't yet cracked the high-value, proprietary-technology segment. If that changes — if regulation forces Otis to open its diagnostic tools and spare parts to outside repairers, as European "right-to-repair" rules are already discussing — the annuity stops being a monopoly-like recurring stream and starts behaving like a commodity service, priced down toward the independents' 20–40% discount. That's the mechanism that would turn a 25% margin business into something much less special.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Think of buying Otis like buying a rental house. At $71.77 a share, you're paying a price that yields about 5.8% a year in owner cash flow — like a house renting for 5.8% of its purchase price. That stream is growing organically at roughly 1% a year, the way rent might drift up slightly. Add the two and you're underwriting a total expected return near 6.8% a year.

The problem: a reasonable required return for a business this exposed — international, cyclical in half its revenue, facing a live regulatory threat to its most valuable segment — is 8.5% to 9.5%. You're being asked to accept a lower return than the risk justifies. That's not a business problem; Otis is a fine, durable company. It's a price problem: you'd be overpaying for the rental yield on offer.

One fair counterweight: this isn't a China horror story across the board. Excluding China, new-equipment orders actually grew 7% last year — the drag is concentrated, not universal. Even so, the math says wait for a better entry rather than chase it here. Fair price sits in the $42–$58 range; today's $71.77 leaves no room for anything to go wrong.
