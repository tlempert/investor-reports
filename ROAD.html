Munger's Verdict

ROAD (Construction Partners, Inc.): Munger Synthesis

Price $77.18 at the 2026-11-06 close [MEDIA per Yahoo]. Analysis date 2026-11-08. USD. The local cost of equity is 9.1–9.3%: the US 10-year at 4.86% [LIVE ^TNX] plus a total ERP of 4.46% [DATA: Damodaran; 4.23% mature market + 0.23% country], at beta 1. 1. The Moat Tribunal

Moat Tribunal: 0/5 SEVERE flags. 4 MODERATE flags, from Buffett, Biologist, Anthropologist and Psychologist. 1 MINOR/NONE: the Historian (MINOR). Outside the tribunal, Bezos, Burry, Cook, Jobs and Lynch also flag MODERATE, and Sherlock and the Futurist flag MINOR. No expert flags SEVERE.

The Historian reads the trajectory as about 60% survivor, 20% casualty and 20% unresolved. That is far below the 60% casualty level that caps the multiple, so the row is Quality (10x–18x).

The tribunal agrees on what kind of moat this is. ROAD owns 61 HMA plants with aggregates and liquid-asphalt assets [SEC], and hot asphalt can only travel a short distance, so the advantage is real and local. Nobody on the council thinks Sunbelt roads will stop being repaved. The moat is a cost moat, though, not a pricing moat:

  • Public work is primarily fixed unit price and private work is lump sum [SEC].
  • $0.8B of the $3.0B FY2025 backlog was "low bid/no contract" [SEC].
  • Gross margin is 15.6% [SEC XBRL/CALC]. The dossier's peer set (PWR, FIX, MTZ, EME, APG) contains no paving or materials company, so its 19.3% median says nothing about plant density.
  • ROIC is 8.3%, or 4.1% on the peer-table definition (conflict C1) [CALC]. The right comparator is a blended cost of capital, not the cost of equity: about 8.9% (Sherlock [JUDGMENT]) or 8.0–9.3% (Futurist [JUDGMENT]). ROIC 8.3% roughly equals it, and 4.1% is about half.
  • Invested capital includes the ~$1.5B of FY2025 deals, which closed through the year, plus $262.1M that closed in October 2025. Those assets carry less than a full year of earnings, so ROIC may understate. The acquisition returns are unproven, not destroyed.

The danger the council sees is financial (a debt-funded roll-up), not technological. That makes the moat durable but narrow.

  • Stress test. The dossier's STRESS TEST table is referenced but absent, so I use Burry's [CALC under a 25% contribution-margin JUDGMENT] on FY2025:
  • A 20% revenue decline leaves cash FCF of $12.8M and FCF after SBC of −$25.8M.

  • The Futurist's 20% volume case takes net debt/EBITDA to 4.4–5.1x.
  • The business survives a 20% drop operationally, but the equity is a levered bet on volume.

2. Tribal dynamics: the Captive vs Devotee test

Who the tribes are [SEC: 10-K]: - State DOTs: 43.4% of FY2025 revenue, none above 10% individually. - Publicly funded work overall: about 65%. - Private developers, commercial and residential: about 35%. - The 25 largest projects: 16.2% of revenue.

The DOT is not a devotee either. There is no advocacy and no switching cost I can see in the record. Communities survive disruption and prisons do not. A competitively bid shopper relationship survives only as long as ROAD's cost is lowest.

What does produce local tribal advantage is plant proximity. Asphalt cools and degrades in transit, so a paver wants an HMA plant within a short haul. ROAD operates 61 HMA plants [SEC], and the third-party moat write-up calls vertical integration "the heart of its business model" [SEARCH: "Business & Moat Analysis (2026)"]. Ownership of the critical input gives a cost advantage over rivals who buy asphalt. That is real, but it is a cost moat located in each local market, not a loyalty moat. The DOJ and the Tennessee AG sued to block APAC's acquisition of Standard Construction's HMA plants in Western Tennessee, and a settlement would require divestiture [SEARCH per DOJ release]. 1. Species classification

ROAD is not a keystone. If ROAD vanished tomorrow, state DOTs would re-let the same lane-miles to Granite, Vulcan-linked firms or the PE-backed platforms (Pave America, True North, Rose Paving, Tendit) [MEDIA per Harbor View Advisors]. ROAD is not a parasite either. Parasites extract rent from hosts that cannot leave. The host here is an auctioneer: public work is primarily fixed unit price awarded through bidding, and private work is primarily lump sum [SEC 10-K]. Government contracts are cancellable at any time with payment only for completed work [SEC 10-K Item 1A]. The hosts hold the leverage in each transaction.

Best classification: a local-niche symbiont and apex consolidator. It is a regional predator of smaller private paving firms, with a real but narrow competitive advantage: - Plant ownership. 61 HMA plants plus aggregate facilities [SEC]; ownership of the critical input is the cost advantage cited against rivals who buy asphalt [SEARCH per Business & Moat Analysis 2026]. The DOT gets reliable capacity and a price set by competitive bid. ROAD gets volume.

Lock-in versus love: there is neither. Customers do not love ROAD and cannot be locked in, since they re-bid every job. The relationship is competitive procurement with a repeat-supplier edge.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (the casualties) [JUDGMENT-HISTORY, figures approximate]

  1. Encompass Services / Integrated Electrical Services (IES), 1997-2006. These were 1990s serial acquirers of local trade contractors (mechanical, electrical, building services), put together by private-equity-style roll-up. Encompass was formed in 2000 by merging Group Maintenance America and Building One Services. It had roughly $4B of revenue and filed for bankruptcy in November 2002. IES filed in 2006. What killed them was not substitution. Acquired margins did not hold, the capital cycle turned, goodwill had to be written down, and covenants tightened as EBITDA fell. The decline took about 2-4 years from peak. Management language before the fall stressed "platform", "synergies" and "record backlog".
  2. Morrison Knudsen (MK), 1994-1996. A large heavy-construction contractor that lost money on fixed-price work it had bid too aggressively. It reported a loss of roughly $350M in 1994 and went through a rescue merger in 1996. The lesson is that fixed-price contracts with estimating error and cost-timing risk can erase several years of margin quickly. ROAD's 10-K discloses the same exposure: "pending change orders" and "claims ... probable" sit inside backlog, and the company "may be unable to pass increased costs through on projects already in backlog" [SEC 10-K].
  3. U.S. Concrete, 2010 (Chapter 11). A materials-integrated, acquisition-built regional supplier (ready-mix and aggregates) that used leverage to build local scale. The construction downturn cut volumes and the debt load did the rest. It reorganised in 2010 and was eventually bought by Vulcan. This is the closest "vertical integration plus leverage" casualty. Materials ownership does not protect a levered equity when volumes drop.

Common casualty features: acquisition-funded growth, goodwill larger than tangible earning power, a local-market business with no pricing power over the cycle, and a covenant ratchet that arrives just as earnings soften.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (the adapters) [JUDGMENT-HISTORY unless noted]

  1. Comfort Systems USA (the dossier peer FIX). It came out of the same 1997-98 roll-up wave as Encompass and IES, then survived by decentralising operations, shedding weak units, de-levering and focusing on margin over growth. It earned its way to the dossier's FIX figures: ROIC 39.5% and gross margin 24.1% [CALC, peer table]. The turning point was a period of years in which it deliberately grew slower than it could and paid down debt.
  2. Quanta Services (PWR). It nearly broke in the 2001-2003 period after aggressive acquisitions and then restructured. It is now a premium compounder with revenue growth of 20.3% and P/E of 49x in the dossier [CALC]. Survival required a deleveraging stretch.
  3. Vulcan Materials / Martin Marietta. The aggregates owners kept their moat (local quarries, scarce reserves) through the 2008-2012 downturn, though their equity still fell hard. The relevant lesson: the moat survived, the equity price did not. Vulcan affiliates sold eight Houston HMA plants to ROAD [SEC], which is the same industry consolidating toward local scale.

Survivor features: (a) the acquirer stopped buying and digested, (b) it paid down debt before the cycle turned, (c) margins were defended by selecting work rather than chasing volume, (d) local density created real pricing power.
