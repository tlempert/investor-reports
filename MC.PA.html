1b. Michael Kors (Capri), peak fiscal 2015 [HISTORICAL, analyst recall]. The closest financial analogue. An "accessible luxury" brand that rode a boom in aspirational demand (revenue peaked at roughly $4.7B), leaned heavily on wholesale and outlets, discounted to hold volume, and saw the shares fall by well over half within about three years. The brand never regained its peak valuation. Observable markers: heavy promotional activity, wholesale dependence, rising inventory against flat sales. What LVMH shares: Bain's finding that about 35% of aspirational consumers reduced or paused spending and three-quarters abandoned a purchase for insufficient value [MEDIA]. What it does not share: LVMH inventory fell 4.3% in FY2025 against COGS -2.3% [CALC], DIO 303 vs 312 peak (2023) and 297 (2022) [CALC], and gross margin is rising (66.2% FY2025 [CALC] to 67.1% H1 2026 [SEARCH]). The Kors markers (discounting, inventory build) are absent in the dossier. Match strength: moderate on demand, weak on behaviour.

1c. Swiss watch industry in the quartz crisis, 1970s-early 1980s [HISTORICAL, analyst recall]. The Swiss lost a very large share of world watch units and roughly two-thirds of industry employment within about a decade after electronic quartz movements arrived, while incumbents stayed committed to mechanical prestige and priced upward. This is a true substitution disruption and is not the right model for LVMH today, since there is no substitute product in the dossier. I include it as the template of a decline that is fast once the substitute arrives and that incumbents explain away as cyclical. The nearest LVMH-relevant substitutes, secondhand luxury (US$34.39B in 2023 to a forecast US$60.55B by 2029, about 10% CAGR [MEDIA per ResearchAndMarkets]) and "dupe" content [MEDIA per Business Insider], are both tagged Speculative in the dossier, so I give this analogue low weight.

What management said before the collapse in these cases: I will not quote verbatim, since I cannot verify exact wording from the dossier. The generic pattern in casualties was to attribute weakness to the environment and promise a return to form. In the dossier, Mr. Arnault is reported to have denied a "structural crisis" while reporting a 2% decline in 2024 [MEDIA per "Bernard Arnault denies any 'structural crisis'"], and said "in spite of everything that is thrown at us, including the taxes" on the Q4 2025 call [SEARCH per Seeking Alpha]. That rhymes with the casualty script. It is also what a leader of a cyclical business would say, so it is weak evidence either way.

2. Survivor analogues (the adapters)

2a. Coach to Tapestry, FY2013-FY2017 [HISTORICAL, analyst recall]. Revenue peaked near $5.1B in fiscal 2013, fell to roughly $4.2B by fiscal 2016 (about -17% from peak), the stock fell by roughly half, and the company then pulled back from discounting, closed stores and reset the product under a new creative lead (Stuart Vevers) before acquiring Kate Spade in 2017. Inflection took roughly four years from peak. The relevant lessons: the recovery began only after management cut promotional volume at a cost to near-term sales, and the creative reset was the proof point.

2b. Burberry, from 2006 [HISTORICAL, analyst recall]. Brand diluted by licensing and over-ubiquity of the check; a new CEO (Angela Ahrendts) bought back licences, centralised control and invested in owned retail and digital. Revenue roughly tripled over about eight years. Lesson: adapting meant taking control of distribution and pruning, and it was visible to outsiders in capex and licence buy-ins early.

2c. SMH/Swatch Group, from 1983 [HISTORICAL, analyst recall]. Survived the quartz crisis by consolidating failed Swiss makers, launching a low-cost electronic product that adopted the disruptor's technology, and keeping a premium tier for mechanical. Lesson: cannibalise your own product and hire from the disruptor.

What the survivors did differently: they (1) pruned or took control of distribution before the numbers forced them, (2) reset creative leadership, (3) tolerated margin cost for brand health, and (4) acted early, within about two to three years of the first decline.

Cultural Anthropologist: LVMH (MC.PA)

The dossier gives no trough estimate and no normalized earnings figure, so I do not claim a peak, mid-cycle or trough scenario beyond the CEO's remark that a 22% operating margin is "way above the average of the last 20 years" [SEARCH per Seeking Alpha Q4 2025 transcript]. 1. Cultural role

LVMH is mostly a status symbol business with ritual layers, not a utility.

  • Louis Vuitton, Dior and (further down) Tiffany sell social signalling and rites of passage (the first luxury bag, the engagement ring, the gift). Brand-share statistics place Chanel 6%, Dior 5%, Gucci and Louis Vuitton 4% each, Hermès 2% [MEDIA per "Luxury Goods Statistics and Facts (2026)", basis not given], and a ranking of 400+ brands puts Chanel, Louis Vuitton and Hermès at the top [MEDIA per Malte Karstan post]. I treat these as weak evidence: the basis is not stated.
  • Wines & Spirits (Hennessy, Moët, Yquem) is a different cultural layer: ritual consumption (champagne at celebrations, cognac in Chinese and American gifting). It is only about 6.7% of group H1 revenue [CALC: €2.6B / €38.6B], so it is texture, not the thesis.
  • Sephora and Perfumes & Cosmetics (H1 2026 revenue €3.91B, flat organic, profit -2% to €417M [MEDIA]) are the "affordable entry" layer. This is where the aspirational buyer lives, and it is where the dossier shows softness.
  • Embeddedness in cultural moments: Louis Vuitton Monogram 130th anniversary and a new Beijing flagship [SEARCH per Q1 2026 release]; Dior under Jonathan Anderson, whose first designs Mr. Arnault cited as a source of Q2 growth [SEARCH per LVMH Letter to Shareholders, July 2026]. The company is using creative direction as a cultural reset lever. That is the right lever, but one cycle of designs is not evidence the reset holds.

What the numbers say about cultural strength: gross margin 67.1% in H1 2026, +30 bp [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors] while revenue fell 3% reported. A brand that can hold gross margin through a demand dip still has pricing power at the core. Utilities do not do that.

2. Tribal dynamics

There are two tribes inside one company, and the data suggests they are behaving differently.

The wealthy core. Not directly measured in the dossier (LVMH's own client split is not supplied). Indirect evidence: organic growth turned positive (Q1 +1%, Q2 +3% [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors]) and the operating margin held at 22.5%. This tribe looks like devotees: they buy because the object carries meaning, and they tolerate price.

The aspirational tribe. Bain estimates the global luxury customer pool fell from about 400 million (2022) to 340 million (2025), with another 20-30 million likely to go, concentrated among aspirational buyers; about 35% reduced or paused spending and three-quarters abandoned a purchase for insufficient value [MEDIA per Bain via a China-outlook article and a YouTube case study]. These are partly captives of status anxiety, not devotees. When the value equation breaks, they leave without a cultural cost to themselves. The Louis Vuitton 2022 price increase of roughly 7% [MEDIA per "The Economy" snippet, status Speculative in the threat register] and the alleged nationalist backlash in China fit this: price-led trust erosion in the tribe with the least loyalty.

Captive vs Devotee test. LVMH is mostly a community, not a monopoly prison: nobody is forced to buy a bag. That is good for survival through disruption (communities survive; prisons do not) but it also means demand is voluntary and can be withdrawn. The only captive-like element is the Arnault family's control (50.01% of capital, 65.94% of voting rights as of 2026-02-19 [MEDIA per AMF-based article]), which protects the brand from short-term investor pressure to discount, a genuine cultural advantage for a status-goods house.

Gatekeepers. Dossier-visible gatekeepers: creative directors (Anderson at Dior), the department-store buyers who are reportedly shifting budget toward contemporary brands [MEDIA per Malte Karstan], and the media that frames "price hikes backfire" narratives. LVMH owns part of its own gate (Sephora, DFS, department stores), but DFS is being partly sold [MEDIA], which shrinks the owned gate.

Conduct signals that damage tribal trust. Manufactures Dior Srl was put under one-year court administration in Italy over subcontractor labour exploitation (since lifted; Dior itself reportedly faced no criminal probe [MEDIA per Reuters, Globe and Mail]). For a brand whose product is "craft and heritage", an exploitation story on a handbag line is a direct hit on the brand's cultural claim, even if the financial effect is nil. I note it as a cultural risk, not a quantified one: the dossier gives no sales effect.

3. Generational analysis (the "My Kids" test)

  • Gen Z buys luxury from age 15, three to five years earlier than millennials [MEDIA per CNBC]. That helps: the entry funnel starts earlier.
  • But BCG/WWD data says younger consumers are 20 points less likely to buy from the same house consistently [MEDIA]. Earlier entry plus lower loyalty means the first purchase is cheaper to win and the second is harder to hold. For a company whose margin depends on repeat purchase, that is the central generational risk.
  • Over 40% of Gen Z and Alpha use AI weekly for fashion shopping [MEDIA per BCG/WWD 2025]. If AI intermediaries compare value across brands, the status premium faces an unfamiliar competitor for attention. The dossier gives no evidence yet of share loss from this.
  • Secondhand luxury was US$34.39B in 2023, expected to reach US$60.55B by 2029 at about 10% CAGR [MEDIA per ResearchAndMarkets.com, status Speculative]. Dupe culture on social media is cited without a year [MEDIA per Business Insider]. Culturally, a generation that treats resale value and authenticity as part of purchase logic can still love the brand while paying LVMH less per lifetime. Resale does not hurt the brand's prestige (it can support it), but it takes some first-sale volume.
  • Values mismatch: Gen Z prizes transparency and sustainability. The threat register shows the EU unsold-goods destruction ban from 19 July 2026 (enacted), tighter greenwashing rules, and a Dior labour case. These are compliance issues, but they also touch the values this cohort uses to judge a house. Evidence of cohort-level damage is absent.

Generational verdict on the "My Kids" test: passes for the top tier (Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany as inherited cultural objects), uncertain for the aspirational tier. I cannot quantify cohort usage, since LVMH's customer age mix is not in the dossier.

1. Species classification

I classify LVMH as a symbiont at the top of its host pyramid, with a parasitic edge at the base.

  • Not a keystone. Remove LVMH and the luxury ecosystem does not collapse. Chanel (6% brand share), Dior (5%), Gucci and Louis Vuitton (4% each) and Hermès (2%) [MEDIA per "Luxury Goods Statistics and Facts (2026)", basis not given] are interchangeable occupants of the same niche. Note that Dior and Louis Vuitton are both LVMH brands, so LVMH is a colony of competing species under one holding structure, not one species. Capital, not necessity, holds the colony together.
  • Symbiotic at the wealthy tier. The host there buys status and heritage. The company supplies scarcity (DIO 303 days [CALC], so it stores the product rather than discounting it) and the host pays a high price voluntarily. Gross margin was 66.2% in FY2025 [CALC per key_metrics.json] and 67.1% in H1 2026, up 30 bp [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors].
  • Parasitic edge at the aspirational tier. Bain reports the global luxury customer pool fell from about 400 million (2022) to 340 million (2025), with another 20-30 million likely to go, concentrated among aspirational buyers. About 35% of aspirational consumers cut or paused spending, and three-quarters abandoned a purchase for insufficient value [MEDIA per Bain, via a China-outlook article and a YouTube case study]. A source claims Louis Vuitton raised prices about 7% worldwide in 2022 [MEDIA per "Louis Vuitton: A Study in Pricing Strategy"; the threat register marks this Speculative]. When a host leaves after a price increase and the species compensates with price, that is extraction from a shrinking population. It is the signature of a parasite, not a symbiont. The data does not say how much of LVMH's revenue sits in the aspirational tier, so I cannot size how much of the company is exposed.

Lock-in versus love. There is no contractual lock-in: contracted backlog is none disclosed, and the customer is a consumer buying at will. The 66-67% gross margin sustained through a -4.6% revenue year is the evidence of love, because a captive host would not need a brand to hold price. Counter-evidence: BCG/WWD says younger consumers are 20 points less likely to buy from the same house consistently [MEDIA], and Gen Z buys luxury from age 15 [MEDIA per CNBC]. Love is therefore transferring from houses to individual product moments, and that does not favour a conglomerate with 75+ brands [MEDIA per "Who Owns Louis Vuitton"] unless it keeps winning the moment (Dior under Jonathan Anderson is the current test).

Currency is weather, not climate.   • Secondhand luxury, US$34.39B in 2023 growing to US$60.55B by 2029, about 10% CAGR [MEDIA per ResearchAndMarkets.com]. This is a decomposer niche: it recycles the host's accumulated capital and sells to the aspirational buyer at a lower price point. LVMH owns part of its own distribution (Sephora, DFS, Le Bon Marché and others [MEDIA per Yahoo, Wikipedia]), which reduces dependence on wholesale hosts. DFS is partly being sold [MEDIA]. That reduces channel ownership at the travel-retail edge, where customer flows (China, tourists) are most volatile. That looks like pruning, not retreat.

3. Invasive species threat

Do new entrants play by the same rules? Mostly yes, with one exception.

  • Same-rules competitors: contemporary brands taking department-store budget [MEDIA]. These compete on price-value, the dimension where the aspirational host is already hesitating ("insufficient value", Bain).
  • Game-changers: secondhand platforms and AI shopping agents (above), plus the regulatory mandates below. Whether these are slow-colonizing or fast-colonizing in this ecosystem, the dossier does not say, and I will not assert a colonization speed. The secondhand series (about 10% CAGR) is the only rate given, and it is a market-size rate, not a share-capture rate.
  • Regulation as an invasive pressure on the species' own methods (from the threat register):
  • Resale price maintenance. On 14 October 2025 the European Commission fined Gucci, Chloé and Loewe over €157M in total for restricting retailers' ability to set prices [SEARCH per European Commission, Loyens & Loeff]. One source headline names Kering, Richemont and LVMH; the supplied text does not allocate the fine by company, and no LVMH response was found. This matters for the biology: controlling the price at which a retailer sells is how a luxury species protects its scarcity signal. Enforcement shrinks the toolkit. LVMH's wholesale share is not disclosed, so exposure is unsized.
  • Unsold-goods destruction ban. The ESPR bans destroying certain unsold apparel, accessories and footwear from 19 July 2026, status Enacted [SEARCH per Anthesis, ecosistant]. This lands on a company carrying €22.66B of inventory and 303 days of DIO [CALC]. A species that kept scarcity partly by culling surplus loses that valve and must manage supply earlier, or discount, resell or donate. Inventory fell 4.3% against COGS down 2.3% [CALC], so the company reduced stock faster than output in FY2025, which is consistent with adaptation. No cost is quantified in the dossier.
  • Greenwashing directive (transposition by March 2026; French fines up to 10% of turnover [SEARCH per Reed Smith]). Status Proposed/Enacted, mixed. Impact on LVMH's marketing claims is not given.
  • China brandy duties up to 34.9% from 5 July 2025; LVMH among cognac houses reported exempt if they agreed to minimum pricing [SEARCH per The Drinks Business, FashionNetwork]. Wines & Spirits is only about 6.7% of H1 revenue [CALC: €2.6B / €38.6B], so this is a local, not a systemic, wound.

Points of failure:

  • Geography. France, Japan and the US are named chief markets [MEDIA per Wikipedia]; China, the Middle East conflict (about 1 point of Q1 organic drag [MEDIA]) and US demand are named drags. Revenue by region is not given.
  • Individual dependence. Bernard Arnault, age 77 [MEDIA per Wikipedia], is Chairman and CEO, and the family holds 50.01% of capital and 65.94% of votes (AMF, 2026-02-19) [MEDIA]. Antoine Arnault was announced to join the executive committee on 2026-02-09 [MEDIA per Wikipédia FR]. No succession date is stated and the CFO is unnamed in the data. Control stability helps long-term thinking and shields against hostile pressure, but it is a single organism's lifespan.
  • Supply chain conduct. A Milan court placed Manufactures Dior Srl under one-year judicial administration over alleged labour exploitation by subcontractors; controls were later lifted, and Dior itself faced no criminal probe per one source [SEARCH per Reuters, Bloomberg, Globe and Mail]. This is a brand-integrity wound in the one asset (Dior) the bull case depends on. LVMH said the supplier code process is "lengthy and challenging" [MEDIA per Reuters].

  • Diversification. Six segments and 75+ brands, but the profit is concentrated. Diversification across niches exists in name; in profit terms it is thinner than the brand count suggests.

Could one shock collapse the whole position? Not in one move. The stress test says adjusted FCF turns negative only near a -45% revenue decline [CALC]. The realistic shock is slower: aspirational hosts leave, price rises stop working and the 2.4x operating leverage turns a 10-20% revenue loss into a 24-48% FCF loss. At -20% revenue, FCF is €7.44B, a 4.0% yield on the €187.8B market cap [CALC], which is about half the 8.19% hurdle.

Is the ecosystem diversifying or concentrating? Concentrating at the top (wealthy clients carry the volume as the aspirational base thins) and fragmenting at the edges (secondhand, dupes, contemporary brands, AI intermediaries). The first raises dependence on fewer hosts; the second lowers the species' control over the channel. Both reduce resilience.

The 10-year arc. If LVMH keeps rotating into the next generation of houses (Dior now, then whichever brand's moment arrives), it behaves like a portfolio of species with staggered life cycles, and the family structure lets it fund the slow ones. If the aspirational base does not return and secondhand plus AI intermediaries move the purchase decision outside the house, it becomes a smaller, more concentrated top-tier franchise. That is still profitable, but worth less than today's price assumes. The data does not let me assign probabilities between the two paths beyond the survival numbers below.

  • Realised vs implied: owner earnings were €11.61B (2022), €11.23B, €11.13B, €10.87B (2025) [CALC], a decline of 6.4% over three years, about -2.1% a year. The price asks for +2.3% a year against a trailing -2.1%. Pipeline FCF moved the other way (€12.75B -> €14.21B), and the €3B gap between pipeline FCF and the company's >€11B operating FCF is unexplained in the data (possibly IFRS 16 lease payments, unverified). The owner-earnings figure is the more conservative one and agrees with the company's number.
  • Known understatements of owner earnings' cost: SBC reads zero because of a yfinance defect, and €16.63B of lease liabilities are not netted [CALC]. If lease payments sit in financing, owner earnings overstate true cash to equity. At an illustrative €3B haircut (the unexplained FCF gap, not a sourced lease figure), owner earnings would be about €7.9B and implied g about 3.8%, above the cap. I flag this as a sensitivity, not a finding.

2. TAM vs SAM

  • The TAM is real, not hype: a mature, branded consumer category. The question is the shape of the SAM, and the data says it is shrinking in customers and held up by price and mix. Bain estimates the global luxury customer pool fell from about 400 million (2022) to 340 million (2025), with 20-30 million more likely to leave, concentrated among aspirational buyers; about 35% of aspirational consumers reduced or paused spending and three-quarters abandoned a purchase for insufficient value [MEDIA per Bain, secondary articles]. That is -15% in three years and up to about -22% by the end of the further decline.
  • LVMH's own organic revenue fell only about 1% in FY2025 [SEARCH per Investing.com], so revenue per remaining customer rose. That is pricing power working, but growth that rests on price and wealthy-client mix has a ceiling set by the "insufficient value" abandonment figure above. Louis Vuitton's 2022 price rise of about 7% and the "price hikes backfire" narrative [MEDIA, threat register item 8, status Speculative] sit on the same fault line.
  • Serviceable slice: the wealthy core. Sources conflict on industry growth (luxury earnings +3.7% and -0.3% a year in two excerpts; Simply Wall St revenue growth 5.30% a year in one excerpt and -2.5% over three years in another [MEDIA]); the pipeline's FY2025 revenue growth is -4.6% [CALC]. I treat the category as roughly flat in volume with low-single-digit nominal growth.
  • Concentration: Fashion & Leather Goods was €9.25B of the €19.1B Q1 2026 revenue, about 48% [CALC from SEARCH/MEDIA figures]. If Louis Vuitton is about half of FLG and Dior 20-25% [MEDIA per TD Cowen estimates], then LV is roughly 24% and Dior roughly 10-12% of group revenue, by my arithmetic on one quarter. The growth thesis is therefore largely a two-brand thesis. Segment operating profit is not in the data, so I cannot show where the profit sits.

3. Workflow Defensibility

This is a B2C brand, so "professional workflow lock-in" does not apply; there is no enterprise customer and no NRR (customer mix ~100% individual [dossier estimate]). Defensibility is brand scarcity, owned distribution (over 5,000 stores, 75+ brands, about 211,000 employees [MEDIA per "Who Owns Louis Vuitton"]) and scale.

  • Evidence of pricing power: gross margin 67.1% in H1 2026, up 30 bp, operating margin 22.5% on revenue down 3% reported [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors].
  • Evidence of erosion: ROIC 19.5% (2022) -> 14.8% (2025), net margin 17.8% -> 13.5% [CALC]; younger consumers are 20 points less likely to buy from the same house consistently [MEDIA per BCG/WWD]; Gen Z starts buying luxury at age 15, three to five years earlier than millennials [MEDIA per CNBC], so the funnel opens earlier but loyalty is thinner.
  • Weight of inventory: €22.66B at 303 days DIO [CALC], equal to about 12% of market cap. High inventory is part of the scarcity model but is also the exposure if demand falls, and the EU ban on destroying unsold apparel from 19 July 2026 [MEDIA, threat register item 2, Enacted] removes one way of clearing it. I rate this as a modest friction, not a thesis driver.
  • This is a "Prosumer-like" risk only at the aspirational tier. At the top tier (Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany) the product is a status good with no substitute inside the same brand; at the aspirational tier the substitute is a contemporary brand, a secondhand piece or a dupe.

4. Structural vs Cyclical

  • Cyclical and transitory: FX. Currency took 5 points off H1 2026 revenue and 7 points off Q1; organic was +2% while reported was -3% [SEARCH per LVMH Letter to Shareholders, July 2026; Investing.com]. The FX drag on operating profit was about €700M in H1 2026 [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors; MEDIA]. Organic trend improved from +1% (Q1) to +3% (Q2) [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors]. Wines & Spirits +5% organic in H1 is off a base that fell 9% in FY2025 [MEDIA]. Middle East conflict took about 1 point off FLG in Q1 [MEDIA].
  • Structural and not yet answered: shrinking aspirational pool (Bain, above); China demand weakness and the "common prosperity" overhang [MEDIA, Speculative]; secondhand luxury from US$34.39B (2023) to a projected US$60.55B (2029), about 10% a year [MEDIA per ResearchAndMarkets, Speculative]; younger buyers' lower loyalty.
  • Tailwinds: Gen Z early entry, Japan and Europe strength in the Q2 call excerpt [SEARCH], Dior reset under Jonathan Anderson credited by Mr. Arnault with Q2 growth [SEARCH per Letter to Shareholders, July 2026], Tiffany HardWear in Watches & Jewelry (+7% organic in Q1 2026 [MEDIA]).
  • My read: the 2% organic recovery is mostly cyclical normalization on a depressed base, and the structural headwind is volume attrition at the bottom of the pyramid. I do not see a structural growth engine in the data that would justify a rate above the 3.18% cap.

5. THE DISINTERMEDIATION SCENARIO

The specific behavior change: AI shopping assistants and agents mediating discovery and comparison for younger buyers (over 40% of Gen Z and Alpha use AI weekly for fashion shopping [MEDIA per BCG/WWD 2025]), combined with a growing resale and dupe channel that gives them a ready answer ("the same bag for less") [MEDIA, threat register item 9]. The mechanism is not that an AI replaces a Louis Vuitton bag; it is that an AI intermediary makes the aspirational tier's price-for-value comparison explicit, which is exactly the "insufficient value" objection three-quarters of aspirational shoppers already raised [MEDIA per Bain].

Where it bites and where it does not: - Least exposed: the top tier, where scarcity, store experience, the 5,000+ owned stores and the 22%+ operating margin [SEARCH] depend on the brand controlling access, not on a portal. An AI agent cannot bypass a brand that does not sell through the agent's channel. - Most exposed: the aspirational tier (where the customer attrition is already concentrated), Selective Retailing (Sephora, DFS, department stores, where LVMH is itself an intermediary), and Perfumes & Cosmetics (flat organic in H1 2026, profit -2% to €417M [MEDIA]). Wholesale and department-store buyers are already shifting budget toward contemporary brands [MEDIA per Malte Karstan post]. Wholesale share is not disclosed.

Scenario: demand down 20% over five years (about -4.4% a year compounded [CALC]). The dossier's stress test [CALC] gives: - Revenue €64.65B (from €80.81B); adjusted FCF €7.44B (from €14.21B); adjusted FCF margin 11.5% (from 17.6%). The stress model says FCF turns negative only at about -45% revenue decline, so this is a margin hit, not a solvency threat. Caveat: the cost-stickiness shape is derived from a single year (FY2025), and a five-year glide gives management time to cut costs, so this may overstate the damage; equally, an aspirational loss comes with mix effects that I cannot size. - Valuation at the 8.19% hurdle on €7.44B: zero growth gives €90.8B, about €184 per share; with 2% growth after stabilization, €122.6B, about €249 per share [CALC, my arithmetic]. That is 35%-52% below the current price. The FCF yield on the current price would be 4.0% (€7.44B / €187.8B) against the 8.19% hurdle. - Moat: gross margin of 66-67% is evidence the brand can defend price, so the first-order effect is volume and operating leverage, not a collapse of the moat. The moat is intact at the top and eroding at the bottom: MODERATE.

Management posture: defensive renewal, not new value. Evidence: Dior reset under Jonathan Anderson; Monogram 130th anniversary and a new Beijing flagship [SEARCH per Q1 2026 release]; the Marc Jacobs IP sale for about $850M (completed 2026-09-01) and a partial DFS sale [MEDIA]; cost and capex discipline holding the margin at 22.5% [SEARCH]; operating investments of €2.1B in H1 2026 [SEARCH per The Dutch Investors]. These protect and refresh the existing position. The dossier shows no AI, digital-channel or resale-capture investment and no figure for either, so I cannot call management offensive against the named threat. R&D reads €0.00B in the forensic block (a yfinance zero, not a disclosed zero), so no R&D inference is possible. Goodwill fell €24.8B -> €18.3B (2022-2025) [CALC], cause unknown (currency, impairment or disposals), so I do not read it as pruning or write-down.

Regulatory add-on: the Gucci, Chloé and Loewe resale-price-maintenance fines (€157M, 14 October 2025 [MEDIA]) concern other houses in the evidence text, though one headline in the dossier lists LVMH; the supplied evidence does not name an LVMH fine and no LVMH response was found. As a precedent it constrains price-control over wholesalers, which weakens one lever against dupes and discounting. I do not size it.

By my own rule, LVMH is not cheap on growth. It is cheap on franchise quality at a cyclical low.
