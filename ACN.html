On "too hard." The strongest challenge is that an unobservable dominant risk on a seven-year fuse belongs in the Too Hard pile. Tested against the tripwires it fails to reach two: zero SEVERE; no political or binary regime variable dominating; one PASS citing a calculable metric; no heroic margin required. My answer: an unobservable risk is not an uncalculable dominant variable. The dominant variable is price against honest earnings power, and I calculated it twice by two routes at $10.1–10.5B. The pyramid risk is a tail, and it is bounded — by net cash, no leverage, and a cost base that shrinks with revenue. I now say that without the module's spurious −35% precision, which is a weaker version of the same argument and I should have made it that way the first time.

Why would it be mispriced? Less than I claimed, and here is the counter-fact I owe the reader. ACN trades at 14.5x against a peer median of 14.6x (CTSH 13.1x, INFY 14.6x, IBM 20.7x) — from the dossier's own Section M, quoted by nobody. ACN is priced in line with its sector. So I withdraw any claim to a relative-multiple anomaly. Whatever edge exists here is not "the market has mis-rated Accenture against its peers"; it is narrower and more specific: the market, like the council, is capitalising an earnings figure that does not charge $2.09B of stock compensation, and is reading trailing indicators (revenue mix, headline bookings, nine-month GAAP income) over leading ones (booked mix, raised FCF guidance, normalised earnings). If that is right at all it is probably right sector-wide, which is a far bigger and less defensible claim than I need — so I will say only that a 42% drawdown has priced deceleration as decline, and that this is worth a small position, not a table-pound.

Accenture controls workforce deployment better than most competitors, but it does not control the foundational AI platforms reshaping its services. Its defense must be integration expertise and client trust, not ownership of the underlying technology.

Verdict: Diversified labor machine, but increasing dependence on technology controlled by potential competitors.

5. Habit Disruption Triggers

How embedded is the customer habit?

Accenture’s habit is institutional rather than consumer:

  • Large transformations are multi-year engagements.
  • Hiring Accenture is an active choice at procurement, but becomes a passive default once implementation begins.
  • Replacing it mid-program creates operational disruption and personal career risk for the executive sponsor.
  • Scale and familiarity reinforce the “nobody got fired for hiring Accenture” behavior.

The habit is therefore episodic at contract award, but effectively daily during delivery. Switching costs are strongest after a project begins and weaker at renewal or when a new technology cycle starts.

What could break the habit?

A 10× better alternative

AI-native competitors could deliver comparable outcomes with much smaller teams, faster implementation, and outcome-based pricing. If clients can buy a result rather than thousands of billable hours, Accenture’s scale could change from an advantage into overhead.

This is the most important threat.

Generational change

The next generation of CIOs and procurement leaders will likely adopt AI-enabled implementation services, but not necessarily Accenture’s traditional labor-heavy delivery model. They are less likely to regard a 779,000-person workforce as proof of quality and more likely to ask why that much labor is necessary.

The “My Kids Don’t Use It” answer is therefore conditional: future buyers will still purchase enterprise transformation, but they may not accept the inherited unit of value—consultant hours.

Accenture remains adoptable if it becomes the trusted orchestrator of AI systems, governance, integration, and accountability. It becomes generationally obsolete if it protects utilization and headcount instead.

Forced migration

The EU Data Act’s switching and portability requirements, applicable from September 12, 2025, can weaken customer lock-in. Multi-sourcing, insourcing, AI regulation, export controls, and possible outsourcing taxes could also force changes in delivery arrangements.

These threats weaken switching costs around the edges; they do not yet eliminate the complexity of replacing Accenture inside a major transformation.

ACN — Historian of Disruption: Pattern Match Against Prior Labour-Automation Waves

A note on analogue selection

The stock list this persona defaults to — Yellow Pages, classifieds, travel agents (as pure casualties), Blockbuster, BlackBerry, MySpace, Kodak — is built for consumer media and hardware disruption: a product or distribution channel gets replaced wholesale. Accenture is not that shape of business. It sells judgment, delivered by labour, priced by the hour or the outcome, embedded in multi-year client relationships. The right analogues are episodes where a billable-labour, relationship-embedded, knowledge-services model met a software substitute — because that is the actual structural test this dossier poses. I have kept Kodak's behavioral lesson (a firm that invented the disruptive technology and throttled it to protect the legacy margin pool) because it recurs below, but replaced the rest with better-fitting cases.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

Retail travel agents (1996–2006)

Market position: ~80%+ of US leisure air/hotel bookings ran through retail travel agents through the mid-1990s, a commission-based labour model (agents = the "billable hour" of travel). What killed them: Expedia (1996) and Travelocity gave consumers direct, self-service booking; airlines then zeroed out agent commissions (American Airlines cut base commission to zero in 2002, others followed within a year). The decline was fast once the commission cut hit — ASTA-member agency counts roughly halved within a decade, concentrated in the 2001-2005 window. Management commentary before the collapse leaned on relationship value and "our expertise can't be replicated online" — a claim that was true for complex itineraries and false for the 80% of bookings that were simple point-to-point trips. The lesson: the commodity slice of a labour-based service dies first and fast; the complex slice survives (see Survivor #3 below — this is the same industry, split in two).

Legal Process Outsourcing / contract document review (2009–2015)

Market position: a multi-billion-dollar cottage industry of contract attorneys and LPO shops billing by the hour for discovery document review — the single most labour-intensive, lowest-judgment task in litigation, and structurally identical to the "billable junior analyst" work this dossier's dossier flags as under attack at ACN. What killed it: predictive coding / Technology-Assisted Review (TAR) software (Relativity, Recommind), given a legal green light by Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe (2012), which could triage document sets at a fraction of the labour cost. Once courts accepted TAR output as defensible, review headcount needs for a given case volume fell by a large multiple within roughly 3-5 years. Firms that had built their economics on billable review hours (rather than judgment/strategy) lost that revenue line almost entirely; the work didn't move to a new full-employment model, it just got smaller.

Encyclopaedia Britannica's editorial and door-to-door sales labour force (1993–2012)

Market position: the dominant reference-knowledge brand, sold by a large commissioned sales force and produced by a large paid editorial/expert staff — a labour-intensive knowledge product, priced at a premium for authority (directly analogous to "nobody got fired for hiring Accenture" pricing power). What killed it: Microsoft Encarta (1993), bundled free with PCs, then Wikipedia (2001), free and continuously updated. Britannica's print run collapsed from ~120,000 sets/year in the late 1980s to a few thousand by the mid-2000s; it stopped printing entirely in 2012. Management's public position through the 1990s was that free/crowdsourced content could never match Britannica's authority and editorial rigor — a defensible claim about quality that turned out to be irrelevant to a market that would trade authority for free and "good enough."

Common thread across all three: the labour being disrupted was the commodity, repeatable, lowest-judgment slice of the business, and the kill mechanism was a substitute that didn't need to match quality — only to be cheap and fast enough that the price gap could no longer be justified. None of the three collapsed because someone built a better encyclopedia, travel agent, or document reviewer. They collapsed because the market stopped paying labour rates for work a machine could do adequately.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

IBM's pivot from hardware to services (1993–2002, and again 2010s–2020s)

Facing threat: the mainframe hardware business that had defined IBM was being commoditized by cheaper distributed computing; IBM lost ~$8B in 1993, then the largest annual loss in US corporate history to that point. What it did differently: under Gerstner, IBM deliberately cannibalized its own hardware margins to build IBM Global Services, betting the company's future on selling integration and expertise around other people's hardware, not just its own boxes. It started adapting immediately upon threat recognition (1993), not after several quarters of denial, and it kept re-doing this: divesting the PC business to Lenovo (2005) to fund higher-margin services and software, then the 2019 Red Hat acquisition ($34B, its largest ever) to buy relevance in hybrid cloud before its own R&D could organically get there. This is the closest structural precedent to Accenture's situation: a labour/services incumbent facing a technology shift it did not invent, choosing to buy and build around the new technology rather than defend the old revenue line.

Goldman Sachs' cash equities trading desk automation (2000–2017)

Facing threat: electronic/algorithmic trading replaced human market-making. The often-cited data point: Goldman's New York cash equities trading desk went from roughly 600 traders in 2000 to about 2 by 2017, with the volume those 600 people used to handle now run by ~200 computer engineers and automated systems. What it did differently: Goldman did not fight to keep the trading-floor headcount employed doing the old job — it let the labour-intensive function shrink and redeployed capital and hiring into the roles algorithms couldn't do (structuring, banking, engineering, risk). Firm-wide headcount and profitability did not collapse with the trading floor; the mix of what people were paid to do changed. This is the direct precedent for Accenture's stated strategy: revenue per employee rising because low-judgment labour is automated away while the firm is paid for the higher-value work wrapped around it.

Corporate travel management (Amex GBT, BCD Travel, CWT) — the other half of Casualty #1

Facing the same threat that killed retail travel agents: online self-service booking. What it did differently: rather than compete with Expedia on simple point-to-point leisure bookings, corporate travel management companies moved entirely into complex, high-touch, policy-embedded, multi-stakeholder programs — duty-of-care compliance, expense-system integration, negotiated corporate rates across thousands of routes — work that a self-service website structurally cannot replace because the value is in the integration and accountability, not the booking transaction itself. This segment not only survived the same disruption wave that killed retail agents, it consolidated and grew. This is the single closest analogue to Accenture's moat thesis in the dossier: switching costs and multi-year embedded programs are exactly the "complex corporate" half of a labour-services market that automation tends to leave standing, even as it guts the commodity half of the same industry.

Common thread: every survivor moved up the value chain into the harder, more embedded, more judgment-dependent work, financed that move by cannibalizing or shrinking the commodity part of the old business itself (not waiting for a competitor to do it), and treated the technology as a tool to sell or wield, not merely a cost to defend against.

Global scale → broader delivery capability and reusable expertise → lower delivery cost per outcome → trusted execution of larger transformations → more client data and experience → stronger global scale.

The strongest evidence that this loop is presently accelerating is revenue per employee:

  • FY2025 revenue increased 7% [MEDIA].
  • Headcount declined from 791,000 to 779,000, or 1.5% [MEDIA].
  • Revenue per employee consequently rose approximately 8.6% to $89,474 [CALC].

That is real productivity leverage: more revenue from a smaller workforce. On the dossier’s decisive AI test, the bull case currently wins. AI appears to be something Accenture sells and uses, not merely something replacing its billable work.

But revenue per employee is not identical to operating leverage. Net margin remained approximately flat—11.2% in FY2024 versus 11.0% in FY2025—while nine-month FY2026 net income grew only 1.7% [MEDIA]. Productivity gains have therefore not yet translated into materially faster earnings growth.

Flywheel friction

The demand side is slowing:

  • FY2026 revenue guidance was cut to 3–4% from 3–5% [MEDIA].
  • Q3 FY2026 bookings declined to $19.3B from $19.7B [MEDIA].
  • Federal services represent approximately 8% of 2024 revenue [MEDIA] and face contract reviews and cancellations.
  • Traditional hourly billing is vulnerable as AI reduces the labour required for comparable output.

There is also evidence of internal drag. The $865M restructuring programme [MEDIA], including $615M of severance in one quarter and another $250M expected [MEDIA], follows more than $2B of severance over three years [MEDIA]. Repeated restructuring can improve productivity, but it can also indicate that yesterday’s organisational design keeps reappearing.

Velocity verdict

This is not yet a doom loop. Revenue grew while headcount contracted, and FY2025 GenAI bookings reached $5.9B out of $80.6B total bookings [MEDIA]. However, the flywheel’s demand input is weakening. A second and third consecutive quarter of declining bookings would suggest that productivity is masking contraction rather than powering growth.

The decisive failure signal is revenue per employee turning down. That could occur if revenue declines faster than headcount can be reduced, price compression overwhelms AI productivity, or customers shift implementation work to boutiques, internal teams, or frontier-model vendors.

CULTURAL ANTHROPOLOGY OF ACCENTURE (ACN)

1. CULTURAL ROLE: Utility Wearing an Institution's Clothes

Accenture is not consumed by a mass public, so the usual "ritual vs. utility vs. status symbol" test has to be run inside its actual tribe: enterprise CIOs, CFOs, and procurement committees. Within that tribe, Accenture functions as risk insurance disguised as a services purchase. The dossier's own moat language captures it precisely: the "consumer_brand" moat type here is really "the 'nobody got fired for hiring Accenture' default among CIOs" ([CALC] ACN ROIC +6.9pp above peer median of IBM/CTSH/INFY).

That phrase is not incidental color — it is a direct descendant of "nobody ever got fired for buying IBM," the defining cultural verb of enterprise technology procurement for three decades of the 20th century. That lineage matters because it tells you what kind of cultural asset this is and how such assets historically die: not by a single disruptive product beating IBM's product, but by a slow erosion of the career-safety function of the purchase, until buyers stopped needing the excuse. Accenture's brand carries exactly that same career-insurance weight today ("multi-year transformation programmes embedded in client operations; mid-programme replacement is career-risking for the buyer" — dossier's own switching-cost language). This is a utility that borrows institutional gravity from the fact that choosing it protects the chooser, not because using it is a source of identity or delight. That is a durable but narrower form of cultural power than a true ritual — it survives exactly as long as buyers believe the insurance still pays out.

2. TRIBAL DYNAMICS: Captive Buyers, Anxious Insiders, a New Set of Gatekeepers

The Captive vs. Devotee Test, applied to CIOs: the dossier explicitly names mid-programme replacement as "career-risking for the buyer." That is the language of captivity, not devotion. Enterprise buyers are not evangelists posting about Accenture the way developers evangelize a beloved tool — they are risk-averse professionals locked into multi-year, multi-country programmes where switching carries personal professional cost independent of programme quality. Multi-sourcing is also explicitly the norm ("multi-sourcing remains the primary contracting model for most customers" per the threat register) — so even loyal buyers hedge by running Accenture alongside 2-3 competitors on any given portfolio. This is a community of hedgers, not devotees. Communities survive disruption; hedged captives defect the moment the insurance premium (price, or reputational safety) stops looking worth it.

A second tribe — the workforce itself — is now visibly anxious. Accenture's product literally is its people, which makes internal tribal sentiment a leading indicator in a way it is not for a product company. The scale of the current churn (headcount 791,000 → 779,000, [MEDIA] $615M severance in a single quarter plus $250M expected, [MEDIA] >$2B severance over three years) is being narrated by CEO Julie Sweet in language ("exit" staff for whom reskilling is not a "viable path") that reads as managerial candor to a shareholder call and as an existential threat to anyone inside the tribe. Glassdoor-style sentiment is not in this dossier, but the language itself is the data point: this is closer to Jack Welch-era "rank and yank" rhetoric than to the "our people are our product" language that has historically been Accenture's own recruiting pitch. That is a real tension between stated brand identity and revealed managerial behavior.

Gatekeepers are shifting. The traditional gatekeepers reinforcing the brand — Gartner/Forrester analysts, industry press, referenceable Fortune 500 clients — are being joined by a new and less friendly set: frontier AI labs publicly building deployment businesses ("OpenAI announced a deployment business with embedded engineering teams; Indian IT peers fell >3% on the news" — dossier bear case #3), and financial/tech press running headlines like "AI Breaks Consulting's Billable-Hours Model." These are gatekeepers with an incentive to narrate Accenture's moat as obsolete, and unlike a traditional trade-press critique, they carry the credibility of being the technology itself rather than a commentator on it.

3. GENERATIONAL ANALYSIS: The Apprenticeship Engine Is the Actual Moat, and It Is Being Dismantled

The "My Kids" Test, applied correctly here, is not "would Gen Z want to buy Accenture" (there is no direct-to-consumer product) — it is "would Gen Z want to start a career there, and would that still work as a talent pipeline if they did?" This is the single most under-examined cultural question in the dossier's own bull/bear framing, and it matters more than the headline revenue-per-employee number, because the apprenticeship pipeline is the mechanism that produces the institutional brand over a 20-30 year horizon, not just this quarter's margin.

The dossier's threat register states it plainly: AI agents "automate market research, competitive analysis, data processing, document summarization, and proposal preparation" — this is precisely the work that has historically been the analyst-tier training ground where Accenture (and every large consultancy) turns 22-year-olds into partners over a decade-plus apprenticeship. If AI absorbs that layer of work, two things happen simultaneously: (a) the billable-hours economics that funded that apprenticeship break, exactly as the bear case argues, and (b) there is no longer an obvious "starter job" for junior staff to do while they learn the craft. R&D spend falling 29% ([SEC] $1.15B → $0.82B) while the firm claims AI leadership is a matching signal — the internal investment in building the next generation of capability is shrinking even as the external AI narrative accelerates.

Meanwhile, "84% now prefer creative and strategic work over routine analysis, which AI automates (Accenture, 2025)" [per Audencia, citing Accenture's own research] is Accenture's own data source describing exactly the generational shift working against its traditional training model, while simultaneously enabling boutique competitors to run "rapid prototyping, smaller teams, and value-based pricing" and compete with giants on far less headcount. A culturally-native, AI-first upstart does not need Accenture's 779,000-person delivery network if a five-person team with agentic tooling can do a project that used to require fifty. That is the generational threat in its sharpest form: not "will young people stop buying Accenture" but "will the economics that built Accenture's scale moat still reward scale at all."

The revenue-per-employee referee metric the dossier insists on is real and currently favors the bull — but it is a trailing, blended average across the whole 779,000-person base, dominated by senior, high-margin delivery staff. It is structurally incapable of detecting an entry-level hiring collapse until that collapse has already compounded for several years and shows up in a shrinking future partner class. Revenue/head can rise for years on senior-staff leverage even while the base of the pyramid is quietly eroding — which is exactly the risk this dossier's referee test cannot see.

4. ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE (INTERNAL): Preservation Talk, Disruption Behavior

Does Accenture's internal culture match its market's culture? Increasingly, no — and the mismatch is the most concrete finding here. The market it serves (CIOs buying "transformation") wants to be told a stability story: multi-year programmes, deep institutional relationships, low personnel churn on their account teams. The internal reality being reported is the opposite: $865M restructuring programme, CEO language explicitly built around "exit," 550,000 people being run through GenAI upskilling while the firm simultaneously guides headcount down and R&D down. This is not obviously wrong as management — a services firm facing a genuine input-cost shift (AI-augmented labor) probably should be doing exactly this. But it is a stark departure from the "our people are our product" cultural DNA that has justified premium pricing for decades, and it is being executed in public, in earnings-call language, at the same moment clients are being sold "AI Refinery" as an enterprise-stability platform. A buyer paying a premium for continuity is watching the seller narrate a mass internal culling. That dissonance is a genuine brand risk that the financial statements alone will not register until it shows up in win-rates or attrition of top delivery talent (neither of which this dossier discloses).

The more generous read: this is a firm doing what GE, IBM, and every prior-generation incumbent failed to do fast enough — cutting the org down to the AI-native shape before the market forces it, funding $1B+ of savings from an $865M outlay, and using the freed capital for $11.5B of FY2026 shareholder returns (+38% YoY) rather than propping up a doomed headcount model. Both readings are consistent with the same facts. The culture is not calcified bureaucracy (it is moving fast and cutting hard) — the open question is whether "moving fast and cutting hard" is renewal or self-cannibalization of the exact asset (deep institutional relationships, stable delivery teams) that justifies the premium price.

5. CROSS-CULTURAL COMPARISON: The Switching-Cost Moat Is Being Attacked by Regulatory Culture, Not Just Competitors

This is a genuinely global business, and the cultural terrain differs sharply by region in ways the financials don't show. Europe is culturally and politically committed to anti-lock-in as a value — the EU Data Act, "applicable from 12 September 2025," explicitly exists "to facilitate switching between data processing services" and mandates functional equivalence and data portability on contract termination. That is a regulatory culture directly targeting the switching-cost pillar of Accenture's moat, not as an incidental side effect but as an articulated policy goal. The EU AI Act adds a second layer of compliance overhead specifically on AI deployments, the exact product line (AI Refinery, GenAI bookings) the bull case depends on for growth.

India's offshore delivery model, which underpins Accenture's cost structure and scale economics, faces its own cultural-political headwind: a "proposed 25% outsourcing tax" and H-1B visa restrictions that research shows push R&D-intensive, offshore-reliant multinationals toward more offshoring, not less — meaning the political pressure and the economic response may point in opposite directions, an unresolved cross-cultural tension the dossier flags as "Proposed" status only, not yet enacted.

The U.S. federal government relationship — 8% of 2024 revenue, with DOGE-era contract cancellations already estimated to have cut FY25 growth by roughly a point — is its own distinct cultural terrain: a buyer tribe (federal procurement) currently experiencing an ideological turn against exactly the kind of large incumbent-consultancy contracts Accenture has built relationships around for decades. This is a culturally-driven, not economically-driven, headwind specific to one geography.

The Hertz lawsuit ($32M, alleging Accenture failed to deliver a viable website redesign, resulting in Hertz replacing the team and losing "precious knowledge") is a small dollar figure but a meaningful cultural signal: public, named litigation against a "nobody got fired for hiring us" brand is exactly the kind of failure narrative that travels fast through CIO peer networks and erodes the insurance-value proposition the brand depends on, regardless of the eventual legal outcome.

Accenture (ACN) — A Feynman-Style Walkthrough

Price: $181.35 | Verdict: BUY, LOW conviction | Buy zone $145–$193 | Position: 2%

1. What This Company Actually Does

Imagine a giant company that doesn't sell you a product — it sells you people who are good at fixing things. Accenture employs about 779,000 consultants and engineers whom big organizations — banks, airlines, governments — hire to redesign their technology, build it, and then keep it running. Think of it as the world's largest temp agency for extremely skilled builders: your bank doesn't want to hire and train 500 software engineers to rebuild its mobile app once every seven years, so it rents them from Accenture instead.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

Most of Accenture is what I'd call a "clean" analytical problem. Its revenue, margins, cash flow, and debt are all knowable, filed with the SEC, and cross-checkable — you don't need to guess at them, you can just look. That's the easy 90% of this analysis.

But one question sits underneath everything, and it isn't clean at all: does AI quietly break the apprenticeship pyramid that makes this business work? Accenture's profit model depends on hiring cheap 23-year-olds, billing their time to clients at a healthy markup, and promoting the best of them over 10-15 years into expensive, high-margin senior partners who sell the next round of work. If AI tools let a client get the junior-level work done without the junior — writing the first-draft code, doing the first-pass data analysis — the bottom of that pyramid erodes, and in a decade Accenture has no seasoned partners to sell $30 million transformation programs, because it never trained them.

Here is the uncomfortable part: this is not a question you can leave "to more research." It is knowable in principle — it's not a matter of taste or forecasting the unknowable future demand for consulting. It is a fact about the world that will eventually become visible. But it will not show up in Accenture's financial statements for three to four years, because entry-level hiring cuts show up as efficiency long before they show up as a missing generation of leaders, and the shortage of seasoned partners is a roughly seven-year story, not a next-quarter one.

So you have a genuinely knowable variable that is currently unmeasurable. That combination — decisive, real, and silent for years — is a distinct category of investing problem, different from both "we can calculate this precisely" and "this is genuinely unknowable." It has its own playbook, which is section 3.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

A reusable framework for the next time you meet a single decisive variable that won't reveal itself in the data for years:

  1. Name the variable explicitly, in one sentence, as a yes/no question. Here: "Does AI shrink the pool of clients willing to pay for junior labor faster than Accenture can retrain its pyramid?" If you can't state it this cleanly, you haven't found it yet — you're still describing vibes.

  2. Ask of every candidate metric: "could this number improve for the exact reason I'm afraid of?" This is the trap the analysis fell into on the first pass. The instinct was to use revenue per employee as the test — if AI is hollowing out the business, shouldn't revenue per head fall? But Accenture can (and did) fire its cheapest, most junior staff first. Cut the bottom of the pyramid and revenue per remaining employee goes up, even though the exact damage you were worried about — a dying pipeline of future seniors — is happening in that same moment. A metric that moves the wrong way when the feared event occurs isn't a weak signal. It's an anti-signal, and it will actively reassure you while the thing you feared happens underneath it. Before trusting any single metric as your "referee," ask what specific management action would make it lie to you — and then check whether that action is already cheap and available to them.

  3. Find a leading indicator, even an imperfect one, rather than waiting for the lagging one to confirm your fear. Here, that's the mix between "Consulting" bookings (advisory-heavy, junior-intensive) and "Managed Services" bookings (running things, less junior-labor-intensive), tracked on new bookings, not on revenue already billed. Bookings lead revenue by 12-24 months; watch the leading version of a metric, not the trailing one.

  4. Size the bet to the fuse length, not to your confidence. You cannot resolve a seven-year question with three years of data. That doesn't mean you sit out — it means you take a position small enough that being wrong for years doesn't hurt, and large enough to matter if you're right. That's what a 2% position is doing here: it is neither "I've solved this" nor "I have no opinion." It is "I have a real, reasoned view, priced for the years I'll spend not knowing."

4. How They Make Money

Accenture has two businesses bolted together. Consulting is the advise-and-build side: a client wants something new — a re-platformed core banking system, an AI strategy — and Accenture designs it and builds it, typically over 12-36 months. Managed Services is the run-it-afterward side: once the new system exists, someone has to operate, patch, and improve it every day, often for a decade, and Accenture increasingly keeps that contract too.

The profit engine underneath both is the pyramid. A first-year analyst might cost Accenture $70,000 a year but bill out at $150,000+ of client time. A partner might cost $500,000 but bill out at over $1 million, and also brings in the next $20 million client relationship. The markup on junior time funds training; the training produces the seniors; the seniors sell the next project and produce the next markup. It's a self-reinforcing machine — as long as the bottom keeps refilling.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

Three real defenses. First, switching costs mid-programme: once a multi-year transformation is underway, replacing Accenture mid-stream means firing the team that holds the institutional knowledge of your own systems — a career-risking decision for the executive who approved the switch, not just an inconvenience. Second, global delivery scale: almost no competitor can staff a 40-country, 3,000-person rollout simultaneously; Accenture can. Third, the CIO-default brand — for decades, "nobody got fired for hiring Accenture" has been a real career-insurance effect for the buyer, not just marketing.

But be honest about the limits: this is not a toll bridge you own forever. Every contract eventually ends, and the next award is a fresh competition against McKinsey, the Big Four, IBM, and now AI-native challengers. Accenture must re-win the client's trust at every renewal — it has no permanent lock, only a strong incumbency advantage it must keep re-earning.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

If AI tools let clients get junior-level analysis and first-draft code without paying junior humans for it, the pyramid's base narrows. Accenture would look fine for a while — margins might even look better, since juniors are the expensive-to-manage, low-margin layer. Three or four years later, the company would discover it lacks enough mid-level people who've climbed halfway up to become the next generation of rainmaking partners. By the time that shows up in reported numbers — declining bookings from an aging, thinning partner bench — the damage would already be baked in. That is the risk this position is sized for, not against.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Think of buying a house. Two questions matter: is this house priced like other similar houses on the block, and does the rent it could earn you clear your mortgage payment with room to spare?

On the first question, Accenture at $181.35 trades at roughly 14.5 times earnings, against a peer median of 14.6 times (IBM, Cognizant, Infosys). That's not a bargain house — it's a house priced at exactly what the neighborhood charges. You are not stealing this.

On the second, the honest test is "owner yield" — what you actually earn per dollar invested, after being honest that stock-based compensation is a real cost, not a free lunch. Reported free cash flow makes the yield look like 10.6%. But $2.09 billion a year of stock compensation is a genuine dilution cost to existing owners, and once you charge it properly, the honest owner yield drops to 8.81%–9.15% — against a required hurdle of roughly 9%. That means the yield sits on the bar, not clearing it with room to spare.

That's the whole story in one sentence: a good house, fairly priced for the neighborhood, whose rent just barely covers the mortgage. You don't refuse to buy a house like that — but you don't put your whole net worth into it either. You own a little.

Now the trouble.

He spent a full page disqualifying revenue-per-employee because the dollar was in it. Correct — that number is a mix measure wearing a productivity costume, and worse, it goes UP when you fire the apprentices, which is the exact risk it was appointed to detect. Fine diagnosis. Then he turned around and built his headline number — the one he calls the killer number, the one he ranks first in his own summary — on a denominator with five hundred and two million dollars of foreign currency translation sitting in it. Fix both and eleven-point-six becomes nineteen-point-seven. His own stated threshold for flipping to BUY is twenty. He is arguing against himself and doesn't know it, and he printed the number to one decimal place as though he'd measured it with a caliper.

But here is the part that actually offends me, because it isn't sloppiness, it's incentive-caused bias with a bow on it.

He runs a private-buyer test. Price it. He says those acquisitions earn eleven-point-six percent against a ten percent hurdle. Nine billion at a hundred and sixty basis points of excess is a hundred and forty-four million a year, capitalized at ten is one and a half billion, is two dollars and twenty-eight cents a share. He is claiming a thirty-five to forty-nine dollar discount for the right to stop something worth two dollars. That is not a valuation adjustment. That is a man walking his answer backward from a conclusion he'd already reached.

And he cannot have it both ways, which is the whole thing in one sentence: either the marginal dollar earns roughly its cost of capital, in which case growth is worth nothing AND stopping it is worth nothing — or stopping it is worth fifty dollars a share, in which case the acquisitions are destroying value on a scale eleven-point-six percent doesn't begin to describe. The Real Warren Buffett's Take

Now let's take our time with this one, because there's a good business in here and I don't want the argument about the memo to obscure it.

Circle of competence. I understand Accenture perfectly well. Seven hundred and seventy-nine thousand people go to work in the morning and go home at night, and the company rents out their judgment. The toll bridge. No. This is not a toll bridge. Nobody has to cross it. On that, the memo and I agree completely.

Capital preservation — rule number one. This is where I part company with the conclusion.

Look at what you actually own at a hundred and eighty-one dollars. Ten billion of cash against five billion of notes — net cash. A business that throws off ten and a half billion of honest owner earnings after you charge the stock compensation, which we should and which this memo did. There's a slow erosion risk on that pyramid, and it's real, and I'd want to be paid for it. Charlie, I don't follow the arithmetic there, and neither does the memo. I'd add one thing nobody in the council said, and it isn't in the bull's favor. This company borrowed five billion dollars last year — from zero — and bought back four point six billion of stock, and the share count went down half a percent. Four of your experts called that buyback "an insider vote with real money." It was a vote with borrowed money that mostly paid the stock-compensation bill. I don't like it. I don't like it enough to mention it twice. But it doesn't make the business bad; it makes the capital allocation ordinary, and ordinary is what this memo is trying to tell you and mostly right about.

Would Berkshire buy it? No — but let me say why, because it isn't the memo's reason. We wouldn't buy it because we buy businesses whose earning power doesn't walk out the lobby at six o'clock, and because at eight or nine times we'd have been interested and at fourteen we're not doing anything unusual. That's a Berkshire preference, not a verdict on the security. Fix the private-buyer contradiction, put an error bar on the eleven-point-six, take the interpretation off the Managed Services line until somebody finds a margin disclosure, correct the federal quote, and publish it. It'll be a better memo than the one it's correcting.
