Munger's Verdict

MUNGER SYNTHESIS: Hermès International (RMS.PA), Euronext Paris, IFRS, EUR

Analysis date 2026-10-04. Price €1,299.50. No VIC pitch exists for this ticker. The dossier's data defects D1–D12 are treated exactly as the refined dossier states them. The most important ones are the 60.3% vs 24.0% ROIC conflict (D3), the "$" labels on EUR figures (D1/D2) and the "asset_manager" classifier error (D4), which I ignore.

1. Moat Tribunal Resolution

Moat Tribunal: 0/5 SEVERE flags raised by: none. 2 MODERATE flags by: Buffett, Psychologist. 3 MINOR/NONE (Biologist, Historian, Anthropologist). Under the synthesis rule this gives the normal Quality Floor of 15–18x. The Historian calls a Survivor Trajectory at ~75% and puts the Coach/Kors casualty path at only ~25%. That is far below the 60% threshold, so the 14x ceiling cap does not apply. Both MODERATE flags rest on the same fact: the access system (the purchase-history queue and the resale premium) is less permanent than the brand. That access system is itself what the tying suit attacks: "sufficient purchase history" (E194). Neither flag disputes the 41% recurring operating margin. Buffett's verdict was "strong and presently intact." The Tribunal finds a strong moat with an amber light on scarcity pricing.

2. Bull-Bear Balance

Every bull case, as written: - Bezos: full-price demand, capacity growth and a ~41% margin sustain cash growth for years. - Buffett: the ~41% margin holds and capacity converts into cash-flow growth above his grid. - Burry: if the 41.0% H1 2026 margin persists while capacity expands, cash outruns his scenario. - Cook: pricing power, faster inventory turns and a 41% margin. - Jobs: clients keep accepting price rises under controlled production. - Psychologist: the ~41% margin holds while demand and FCF grow faster than the price implies. - Sherlock: a 41% franchise with €12.3B net cash at its 2-year low, -53% from the high, compounds at 8–10% and the multiple stops compressing. - Futurist: constant-currency growth re-accelerates to 8–10% for a decade, worth €1,380+ at 8.9%. - Biologist: an integrated artisan maker with Q2 at +7% cc that is 53% off its high. - Historian: it behaves like the survivors, and 30x on a 41% margin compounds 7–10% with €117/share of cash underneath. - Anthropologist: a Cultural Institution growing +6–7% cc through a slowdown; 30x becomes cheap if growth runs at 7.8–10.8%. - Lynch: 8% growth plus a 31% net margin once the French levy lapses reaches ~€2,200 by 2031 (~13%/yr).

Eleven of the twelve bull cases rest on one fact, the ~41% recurring margin persisting; only Jobs's does not cite it. They are one witness, not eleven.

Lynch, addressed rather than dismissed. His central claim is right: the narrative broke, not the business. EPS went from ~€43.6 to ~€42.9 while the price fell from the €2,781.45 high close, so the multiple halved from ~60x to ~30x. He rebuts most bear points with company data: - gross margin rose 0.4 points to 71.1% on "strong inventory management and high sell-through rates"; - Q2 accelerated to +7% cc; - adjusted FCF of €2.2B was up 18%; - Q4 2025 was up nearly 10%, with the Americas +12% and Asia ex-Japan +8% [MEDIA per Business 360]; - Hermès outperformed LVMH and Kering, with HNWI clients holding up while aspirational buyers weakened (E160).

He is also right that this is a stalwart, not a broken franchise.

Where I part with him is the exit multiple. His base case (6.5% revenue growth, 30% net margin, 28x exit) earns ~7.7%/yr. Blending his own bull, base and bear scenarios at his 25/50/25 scenario weights gives ~7.0%/yr. Those are Lynch's scenario weights; they are separate from my 40/60 argument weights below and feed no input of mine. Both returns are measured at today's €1,299.50, and both are below even the 8.0% floor of the hurdle, which is why his own verdict is WAIT. His €1,185 starter is a different number: the price at which his whole scenario grid earns 9%. Its weighted exit multiple is ~27.5x (32x / 28x / 22x at 25/50/25). My DCF's 3% terminal at 8.9% implies ~17x year-10 FCF over a ten-year horizon. The gap between his trigger and mine is that terminal valuation, not any fact. I accept his warning that a margin of safety can become paralysis. That is why mine is 10%, not 25%.

Burry's short is rejected. He found no accounting fracture: - receivables fell 12.5% while revenue rose 5.3%; - inventory fell 7.9%; - SBC is €0.13B, 0.8% of revenue and ~2.3% of ttm OCF; - borrowings are €0.01B against €12.31B of cash.

In his own words this is "not an evidenced solvency short." Shorting a 41%-margin, net-cash business at its 2-year low on valuation alone is a bet I will not make.

Weights. I weight the bull argument at 40% and the bear argument at 60%. The bulls win on the business and the bears win on the price, and the question in front of me is the price.

Independence. Jev reports one trigger basis (scenario grid/DCF) ×12 and only 3 key-metric families. The tally (5 PASS, 4 WAIT, 2 HOLD, 1 SELL) is therefore one observation reached twelve times: a 3.1% FCF yield sits below an 8–10% hurdle. The votes differ only in how long growth is assumed to last. The five PASS votes are price passes ("a wonderful company at a price I would pass on"), not quality failures. In this vocabulary they are WAITs with lower triggers.

Private Buyer Test. No control buyer exists. Hermès is a société en commandite par actions described as a subsidiary of H51 SAS. Family holders placed shares in a non-listed holding company built to stop an LVMH takeover, and Arnault "was happy to remain a long-term shareholder." Puech declined to join (E117). So there is no takeout floor, and the dossier contains no buyer figure. A financial buyer earning a 3.1% FCF yield (3.4% on EV), with nothing to lever or cut, would pay what the cash earns at its cost of capital, which is my DCF. Sell-side targets from January 2026 (Jefferies upgrade; average target $2,854 on the USD OTC line vs $2,402 [MEDIA per Fintel, raw dossier only, not carried into the refined dossier]) were set at the old multiple and are not a private-market value. Nothing in the dossier shows a private value above my buy zone.

Floor (moat and quality). Biologist: Symbiont (Healthy), with a ~90% 10-year franchise survival. Anthropologist: Cultural Institution (Enduring). That makes this a high-quality compounder, so the Quality Floor of 15–18x applies.

Franchise Premium test: 1. Zero SEVERE flags: passes. 2. The margin route: passes. The ~41% recurring operating margin was "flat to +0.5 point" in the FY2025 call and held at 41.0% in H1 2026, which covers FY2024, FY2025 and H1 2026. 3. Recurring revenue: fails. There is no contracted backlog or remaining performance obligation (E210), and customers are ~100% individuals, so every sale is re-won. Control comes from direct retail distribution and an integrated artisanal model; the wholesale share is not given (E181). 4. Organizational switching costs: fails. This is individual taste, not a migration.

So no 18–25x band.

Central value: €1,041. Inputs: - FY2025 FCF €4.21B; - 7% FCF growth for 10 years, then 3% terminal; - discounted at 8.9%; - plus €10.02B of net cash after leases; - divided by 105.57M shares.

All three candidate rates are judgment, not data, in different degrees: - 10.29% is the only sourced figure: the 5.28% US 10-year plus a 5.01% total ERP (4.23% mature-market plus 0.78% France). It is built on a USD risk-free rate for EUR cash flows. - 8.0% applies the full downward EUR adjustment of up to 2.3 points, which is unsourced because the dossier has no euro risk-free rate. - 8.9% is a back-solve of the pipeline's €673.84 conservative DCF with net cash added (7.6% without). That pipeline artifact prints neither horizon nor discount rate, and I reject its 1.6% growth input.

I use 8.9% as the midpoint of a judgment range, not as a sourced rate, and I sweep all three.

If it persists, +6–9% constant-currency growth delivers only ~2–5.5% in reported euros, and 7% is generous rather than conservative. The 7% assumes the drag fades.

I reject the printed g = 14.8% (ROE 25.5% × 58% retention). Retained earnings are piling up as cash: €12.8B at December 2025 became €12.9B at June 2026 after €1.9B of dividends. I also reject the pipeline's 1.6% g (DCFs of €673.84/€687.47), which describes a company that stops growing.

Framing: what disruption means for Hermès

There is no technology disruptor here. The Yellow Pages, Blockbuster and Kodak templates do not apply. Hermès cannot be displaced by a better substitute, since a Walmart Birkin lookalike [MEDIA per Business Insider, threat register item 3] is not a competitor to a client with a purchase history. The real historical risk for a scarcity brand is franchise dilution and demand normalization. It starts with a boom, then comes an over-extension of price or distribution, then the aspirational layer leaves, and finally the margin gives back the boom. The right analogues are luxury and "accessible luxury" brands, not technology casualties.

Another fact sets the arc. Revenue rose ~78% between 2021 and 2025, implying ~€9.0B in 2021 [MEDIA per Données financières RMS.PA; implied figure is CALC]. That is the post-Covid revenge-spending boom, and every analogue below also had a boom before it faded.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

Coach, 2013-2016 [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. Coach was the dominant US "accessible luxury" handbag brand, with ~$5B in revenue at its FY2013 peak and an operating margin around 30%. It leaned on outlet and promotional distribution to hold growth, and its aspirational middle-market customer was taken by Michael Kors. North America comparable sales turned negative in 2013-14. Operating margin fell to the low teens by FY2016 and the shares lost well over half of their value from the 2012-13 highs. The decline from peak margin to trough took about three years. Management said, in essence, that the brand was strong and the weakness was cyclical and promotional-environment driven, right until the 2014 "transformation" under Victor Luis and Stuart Vevers.

Michael Kors, 2014-2018 [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. Kors reached a ~25-30% operating margin and ~$4.7B revenue by FY2016, riding a logo-bag boom with heavy wholesale and outlet reach. The shares peaked in 2014 and fell ~60% within two years as comparable sales turned negative and wholesale was cut back. The tell was the share of sales through outlets and department-store promotions, and the company later pivoted to acquiring Versace and Jimmy Choo (Capri Holdings), which is what a company does when the core brand has stopped growing.

Gucci, 1980s-1993, and Pierre Cardin [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. Gucci nearly lost its franchise through over-licensing and family infighting, with products sold at downmarket price points through uncontrolled channels. It was rescued from 1994 by De Sole and Ford. Pierre Cardin licensed its name to hundreds of products, and the name stopped signalling anything. The decline was slow, a decade or more, because brand equity erodes before the income statement does.

Pattern common to the casualties: (a) the boom was read as permanent; (b) volume was pulled forward with outlets, licenses or promotions; (c) the aspirational customer tired first, and the HNWI core stayed; (d) the income statement lagged by 2-4 years; (e) management denied a brand problem until comparable sales were negative.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

Hermès itself, 1990s-2010s [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. It was a family-run saddler that diversified into 16 métiers without licensing out the core. It pruned an overextended periphery in the 1990s and kept leather goods supply-constrained. It came through the 2008-09 crisis with a much smaller revenue decline than most luxury peers. The adaptation that mattered was refusing the growth that would have damaged scarcity.

Burberry, 2006-2012 [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. Check-print over-exposure and fragmented licensing hurt the brand in the early 2000s. Under Angela Ahrendts from 2006, Burberry bought back licenses, controlled its distribution, invested in digital and flagship retail, and recovered its margin. Adaptation took roughly 3-5 years.

Gucci under De Sole/Ford, 1994-2000, and Ferrari [GENERAL KNOWLEDGE, approximate]. Gucci repriced upward, pulled licenses and restored scarcity. Ferrari deliberately produces one car fewer than demand, the same logic as the Birkin waitlist.

Pattern common to the survivors: control of distribution, pruning of dilution before sales demanded it, investment ahead of demand, and pricing power used with restraint.

7. Lynch's verdict

Lynch's rule of thumb for stalwarts: buy when the price/earnings ratio is below the growth rate (PEG near 1), and sell or at least stop buying when it is well above (PEG 3.07 here [MEDIA per Yahoo Finance]). This is a stalwart growing 7-10% earnings at 28-30x. I would not call it expensive in the way a 60x multiple was expensive, and I would not sell. I would also not pound the table. The bears are mostly wrong about the business (the dossier's own company data, gross margin +0.4 point, +7% cc, adjusted FCF +18%, contradict "the premium is eroding") and mostly right about the price (3.1% FCF yield against an 8-10% hurdle).
