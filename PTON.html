The two-ledger problem, resolved — and the ledger relabelled

I cannot argue in §3 that the falling SBC line is illusory and then freeze it here because freezing is harsher. The dollar figure measures what the compensation costs; the share count measures how it is delivered. §3's finding is that delivery is deteriorating, not that the dollar expense cannot fall. So the dollar figure fades.

But I mislabelled the fade. −13.5% is not "its own trend" — it is the FY2025→FY2026 delta, the most recent of two observations, and the slower of the two available. The two-year rate is √(198.6 ÷ 311.7) = −20.2%. I withdraw the claim that −13.5% was "the company-favourable reading": it is the harsher of the two extrapolations, and the gentler one produces no crossing in three years at all on the undiscounted base. And the reflexive mechanism in §3 argues both fades are generous: a stock at $5.39 forces the board to grant more dollars, not fewer, to retain the engineers it kept after the January cut.

3. THE SBC QUESTION — FIVE EXPERTS OVERRODE THE PIPELINE AND THEY WERE RIGHT

The dossier's owner yield and its ✅ STRONG BUY are struck before stock-based compensation. Sherlock, the Biologist, the Futurist, the Anthropologist and Lynch each recomputed and landed at 5.6%–7.6%. Nobody told them to.

SBC is a real cost here. FY2026 SBC of $198.60M against EBITDA of $227.40M — 87%. Against adjusted EBITDA of $468.2M — 42%. These are RSUs replacing cash salary at a firm that cut 11% of staff in January 2026 and must still retain the engineers it kept.

The cleanest tell needs no adjustment. GAAP already expenses SBC. FY2026 net income was $63.2M against a $2,351M market cap — 37.2x earnings. Restated: EV/adjusted EBITDA 5.2x → EV/(adjusted EBITDA − SBC) 9.1x → EV/unadjusted EBITDA 10.7x.

The real owner yield is 5.6–6.0%, not one number — the spread is entirely the maintenance charge. The pipeline's 14.0% implies a ~$59M deduction, giving $130.4M post-SBC and 5.6%; my $48M gives $141.4M and 6.0%. I use the band and name its cause rather than switching between the ends.

This is a tool defect, not a Peloton fact

The pipeline's stated definition is operating cash flow minus PP&E depreciation. Operating cash flow adds SBC back as non-cash and the formula never subtracts it again — so owner earnings is structurally pre-SBC for every company the pipeline touches, overstated by exactly SBC ÷ market cap. Here that is 8.4 percentage points: 14.0% reported, 5.6% real.

And it corrupted a specific number that I should have named. The verdict line reads "✅ STRONG BUY (Below Conservative DCF)," and the anchor it refers to is CONSERVATIVE (Owner Earnings): $8.63 — not the yield. Charge SBC and that anchor falls to roughly $3.4–$3.6. The pipeline did not merely flatter the yield; it published a fair-value anchor 2.4x too high and then compared the price to it. On a software business running SBC at 15–20% of revenue the error would be larger. Twelve experts caught it one company at a time; the formula should catch it before they ever see it. (The orchestrator reports this is now fixed upstream: PTON's owner yield prints 5.6%.)

5. BULL–BEAR BALANCE

Every expert's bull case is the same bull case. All twelve name Repowered plus the Commercial Series, and nothing else. When twelve independent seats find only one way for a thesis to work, that is a thin thesis, not a converging one.

So I sized the door. Subscribers fell 218,000 year-over-year. Repowered and Commercial must generate roughly 218,000 net additions in excess of the current run rate — 8.2% of the base — merely to hold the line flat. Not gross adds; 218,000 more than today. Management is not forecasting the hurdle being cleared: guidance is 2.550–2.570M, another ~100,000 down.

The second-hand question, where I part from the pessimists — condition stated up front. Conditional on the used buyer being genuinely incremental, swapping a hardware sale for a Repowered subscriber is a good trade: a low-margin unit exchanged for a subscriber acquired at near-zero cost feeding a ~68.6%-margin line. To the extent it cannibalises, it is a bad trade — a new unit yields roughly $231 of gross profit ($1,445 × 16%) against a $95 activation fee plus a Repowered take, so each substitution destroys about $100 and pressures a $770M equipment line already down from $3.1B in FY2021. Stern is not spinning when he calls the secondary market "one of the largest ways to acquire new members" — but the claim only pays if the members are new. The Biologist's diagnostic settles it: if it were additive, net subscribers would be stabilising, and they are not. The trade is right in principle; the channel is too small in practice.

The competitive fact that may decide the decade cuts at that funnel. iFIT's Trainer Games launched on Amazon Prime Video in January 2026 — 200 million Prime members, no separate subscription — against Peloton content at $12.99–$44/month. It does not threaten the installed base; a Bike owner is not switching. It threatens gross adds, the exact line that must grow 218,000 a year. And the market compounds at 14.4% while Peloton shrinks. Erosion inside a growing market is share loss, the worse of the two diagnoses.

I weight the bull case at 30% and the bear case at 70%. The 30% is not charity: net debt down 80% to $92.6M, leverage 0.3x, cash $1,206.6M against $1,299.1M of gross debt, first full-year profit, nothing due until 2029 — filed, audited, irreversible, and years none of the Historian's casualties had. It now also includes management's $350M floor, which on a consistent base implies improvement. The 70% is that every series measuring demand rather than cost still falls: subscribers −7.6% and guided lower, revenue down a third consecutive year, and 615 million workouts in 2025, the first annual decline in engagement itself. Engagement leads churn, and it has turned.

The Private Buyer Test. A financial buyer cannot pay staff in stock of a private company, so SBC becomes cash: EBITDA ~$270M, not $468M. At 6–8x for a declining subscription asset, EV $1.62–2.16B less net debt is $3.50–$4.73 per share — below today's price, bracketing my zone rather than exceeding it. The genuine optionality is leverage: 0.3x against $468M of EBITDA leaves room for $1.5–2.0B of debt, and that is probably why the stock is $5.39 rather than $3.00. But when the strategic bid, the value investor and the sole council bull all land within a dollar of each other and two of the three sit below spot, there is no discrepancy left to exploit.

6. THE HISTORIAN'S TEST — ANSWERED DIRECTLY

Did management fix the mechanism causing attrition, or merely optimise the economics of the customers who had not yet left? Amelio's Apple cut costs and share kept falling; 1997–98 Apple made the same cuts and shipped the iMac.

For "optimised the remaining": blended net churn improved 1.9%→1.2% after an October price increase; 200+ documented retention offers in Jan–Feb 2026 cutting $44/month to $12.99; two rounds of layoffs; a $100M savings target exceeded. Raising the rent on tenants who cannot move while slipping a rebate under the door of everyone who can is the absence of pricing power, expensively disguised.

For "fixed the mechanism": Repowered nationwide, Commercial Series growing double digits, Peloton IQ with >50% of monthly actives engaging, the Spotify partnership. Genuinely new funnels, not discounts.

My answer: Peloton has executed the Amelio half far better than Amelio ever did, and has launched but not proved the iMac half. The discriminating evidence — the product shipped and the unit line turned within four quarters — has not appeared. App-only churn is 7.0% monthly: a 14-month customer life, half of new digital subscribers gone inside 90 days. That is the funnel, unrepaired, and it is the number to watch, not blended churn, which the price increase contaminated.

Why WAIT and not SELL — on the honest cash figure. I previously defended against Burry and the Anthropologist using "$378M of FCF," the pre-SBC number §3 discredits. The argument wins without it: at $179.4M of post-SBC free cash flow, $141.4M of owner earnings, or even $114M on my own stated parameters, Peloton generates more cash than Blue Apron produced in any year of its existence, at a fraction of WW's five-turn leverage, with $1.21B of cash and no maturity until 2029. The trajectory matches the casualties; the terminal form does not. You do not short a fortress because you dislike the garrison — and Burry's own summary concedes it: that cash "could sustain FCF and trigger a violent rerating."

Separately his add level of $4.25 sits inside my zone, $0.10 from its top. We agree about the business and very nearly about fair value; we disagree about whether to hold a starter while waiting.

My reason needs no dressing up. There is no dividend. Nothing pays me to sit inside a shrinking subscriber base. When you are paid to wait, wait patiently; when you are not, wait cheaply. Sitting on your hands is free here. Owning a starter is not.

Preface: the question I was asked, and why it's the right one

Every other expert on this council can read the FINANCIAL PHYSICS table and see that FY2026 was Peloton's first full-year profit, that adjusted EBITDA rose 16% to $468.2M, and that net debt fell 80% [MEDIA]. That is not in dispute. My discipline exists to answer a narrower question: is this the shape of a company that survives, or the shape of a company that is dying slowly and profitably? History has a large sample of both, and they look identical for the first few years. The distinguishing feature is not visible in the cost line. It is visible in what happens to the customer's reason for leaving.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES — companies that cut their way to profit while their customer base kept shrinking, and never stopped shrinking

Blue Apron (2017–2023). IPO'd June 2017 near a $3B implied value. Meal-kit subscribers fell from a 2017 peak above 1 million toward roughly 300,000 within three years. Management repeatedly announced the cost structure was "right-sized" and posted brief periods of adjusted EBITDA profitability (2019, 2021) by cutting marketing spend and closing fulfillment centers — margin expansion, quarter after quarter, laid directly on top of a subscriber count that kept falling every single one of those quarters. There was no quarter where the decline reversed; there was only a quarter where the decline finally became too small to offset with further cuts. Acquired by Wonder Group in 2023 for ~$103M — roughly 97% below IPO value. The lesson isn't that Blue Apron failed to cut costs well; it cut costs very well. It never once fixed the reason people quit ordering the box.

WW International / Weight Watchers (2018–2025). The closest analogue on the board, structurally: a subscription wellness company, valuation once north of $100/share on the Oprah/DIS effect in 2018, that ran repeated cost-reduction cycles and periods of GAAP profitability across a membership base that eroded through most of the 2018–2024 stretch. WW saw a genuine external disruptor arrive — GLP-1 drugs (Ozempic, Wegovy) launching in 2022–2023 offered a categorically better substitute for the job WW was hired to do. Management's response was an acquisition (Sequence, a GLP-1 telehealth prescriber, 2023) rather than a fix to the core product's declining draw, and cost discipline could not outrun the substitution. WW filed Chapter 11 in May 2025. This is the pattern to fear for Peloton: a subscription wellness brand, profitable on paper, disrupted not by a cheaper competitor doing the same thing but by something that solves the underlying job more directly, with cost-cutting buying time rather than a future.

AOL dial-up subscriptions (1996–2010s). The purest "harvest" case in the sample. Subscriber count fell from a ~25 million peak (2002) toward near-zero over roughly a decade, and AOL became legendarily profitable per remaining subscriber during the decline — a meaningful share of that profit came from customers who had simply forgotten they were being billed and never cancelled. Cost-cutting and price extraction from an inert installed base generated a decade of accounting profit with zero stabilization of the underlying metric. The company survived as an entity (sold to Verizon in 2015 for a fraction of the 2000 AOL-Time Warner combined valuation) but the subscription business itself never found a floor — it just kept shrinking until there was nothing left to harvest.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES — companies that stabilized, not merely slowed

Apple, 1996 vs. 1997–1998 — a controlled experiment inside one company. Apple's PC market share fell from roughly 16% (1990) toward 4% (1996) as Windows commoditized the desktop. Under Gil Amelio (1996–1997), Apple cut costs and headcount aggressively — and market share kept falling and the stock kept falling with it. Cost-cutting alone did not move the trendline. Under Jobs (1997–1998), Apple also cut costs — famously killing all but four product lines — but paired the cut with a genuine fix to the customer's reason for leaving: a coherent, desirable new product (iMac, 1998) and an explicit bet that the old strategy of chasing Windows on price and specs was the actual disease. Market share and revenue stabilized within roughly 18 months of the Jobs pivot, not the Amelio one. Same company, same balance sheet stress, same starting point — the variable that mattered was whether the cost cut came bundled with a repaired value proposition or stood alone.

IBM under Gerstner (1993–2002). Mainframe hardware revenue was structurally shrinking as client-server computing disrupted IBM's core business; the company cut roughly 180,000 jobs in the early 1990s. The distinguishing move wasn't the magnitude of the cuts — it was that Gerstner redirected the freed capital into building a genuinely new growth engine (IBM Global Services) rather than just shrinking the legacy hardware business more efficiently. The stock went from roughly $10 (split-adjusted, 1993) to over $130 by 2002. The company didn't stop the mainframe business from shrinking; it built something else next to it that grew faster than the legacy business declined.

Best Buy, "Renew Blue" (2012–2016). Comparable sales were falling and "showrooming" (customers browsing in-store, buying on Amazon) was treated by most observers as a terminal diagnosis; the stock fell from ~$44 (2010) to ~$11 (December 2012), a ~75% drawdown almost identical in shape to Peloton's. Hubert Joly's turnaround included ~$1B in cost savings — but the stabilizing element was a price-match guarantee that neutralized the specific reason customers were leaving (price gap versus Amazon), plus store-within-store partnerships (Samsung, Microsoft, later Apple) that gave people a reason to walk in. Comparable sales turned positive by FY2014. Cost cuts bought the runway; the price-match and vendor partnerships stopped the bleeding.

3. THE DISTINGUISHING FEATURE — a direct answer

Across all five cases, the variable that separated stabilization from a slower death was never the size or speed of the cost cut. It was whether management ever located and fixed the specific mechanism causing customers to leave, versus optimizing the economics of the customers who hadn't left yet. Blue Apron, WW, and AOL all extracted more margin from a shrinking, unrepaired base. Apple (post-1997), IBM, and Best Buy all paired the cut with a change to the actual product or price experience that removed the customer's reason to go elsewhere.

Cost-out is finite by arithmetic — you run out of cost. Subscriber decline is not bounded by arithmetic at all; it is bounded only by whether the reason people are leaving stops operating. That is the test I apply to Peloton below.

1. CULTURAL ROLE

At its 2019–2021 peak, Peloton was unambiguously a ritual: the leaderboard, the instructor cult-of-personality, the "tap out" high-five, "Peloton output" as a social-media flex — it functioned the way CrossFit or SoulCycle functioned, as identity, not just exercise. That is a real, well-documented cultural achievement most fitness-hardware companies never get near.

What remains in the dossier is a bifurcated cultural role: for the hardware-owning core (2.662M paid connected-fitness subscribers, down 218,000 / −7.6% y/y), it still functions partly as ritual, partly as utility with switching costs — the bike is expensive ($1,445–1,745) and useless without the subscription, which is a mechanical, not emotional, retention lever. For the much larger halo of 5.8 million total members, most of whom are not paying the connected-fitness price, Peloton is closer to a commodity fitness app competing on the same shelf as Apple Fitness+ ($9.99/month) and free YouTube content.

The company's own management language — CEO Stern calling the secondhand market "one of the largest ways to acquire new members," and the pivot to a Commercial Series for gyms/hotels/corporate wellness — is itself a cultural signal. That is not the language of a company confident in consumer devotion; it is the language of a company diversifying distribution because direct-to-consumer devotion is no longer growing the base on its own.

2. TRIBAL DYNAMICS — The Captive vs Devotee Test

This is the discriminating test the dossier asks me to choose, and the dossier hands me the exact instrument to run it: compare the churn of members who are structurally captive (own the hardware) against members who are not (app-only).

  • Blended average net monthly churn: 1.2%, improved from 1.9% after an October price increase.
  • App-only monthly churn: 7.0% by Q2 FY2026, implying an average customer life of 14 months, with roughly half of new digital subscribers cancelling within 90 days.

That is not a small gap — it is a nearly 6x difference in monthly attrition between the segment locked in by a sunk hardware cost and the segment free to leave at will. A genuine devotee tribe should retain members reasonably well regardless of whether they're locked to a $1,500 bike, because the pull is the community and the instructors, not the collateral. Peloton's data says the opposite: take away the hardware handcuff and the retention collapses to levels worse than typical fitness-app benchmarks (median 10–13% monthly churn per the threat register, but top-quartile apps hold 4–6% — Peloton's app-only tier is closer to the bottom than the top).

The second discriminator is how retention is being purchased, not earned. The dossier documents 200+ user reports (Jan–Feb 2026) of retention offers cutting the $44/month tier to $12.99 — saving subscribers $372+/year. A tribe defends its identity with belonging; a captor defends a base with price. When the mechanism keeping members from leaving shifts from "I don't want to lose this community" to "they cut my bill by 70% to keep me," the devotion has already left the relationship — what's left is a negotiated truce.

The third discriminator, and the most concerning for the brand specifically (as opposed to the hardware-lock business model): instructor departures. Kristin McGee, Kendall Toole, and Ross Rayburn — three star instructors — left in the same week (reported August 17, 2026). In a ritual-based fitness brand, the instructors are the tribal gatekeepers the persona framework asks about. Peloton's own marketing built individual instructor followings (the "Cody," "Robin," "Emma" fandoms of the pandemic era) rather than a faceless institutional brand. That is a double-edged cultural structure: it drove intense loyalty when the instructors were exclusive to the platform, but it means the loyalty was arguably always to the person, not the company — and instructors increasingly have their own YouTube/Instagram/app businesses to take that loyalty to. Peloton has not disclosed any member-attrition figure tied to instructor departures, which itself tells you the company either doesn't track it as a discrete driver or doesn't want to.

Verdict on the test: mostly Captive, with a shrinking Devotee core. The remaining paying base splits into (a) a genuine but shrinking hardware-locked cohort that stays partly out of habit/sunk cost and partly out of real affinity, now increasingly retained via discounting, and (b) an app-only cohort that behaves like any commodity subscription app and churns like one.

Peloton (PTON) — Explained Simply

Price: $5.39 | Verdict: WAIT | Buy zone $3.00–$4.35 | Position: 0%

1. What This Company Actually Does

Peloton sells a stationary bike or treadmill for one to three thousand dollars, and then charges about $44 a month, indefinitely, for the classes that make the machine worth using. The hardware is the doorway; the subscription is the house. About 2.7 million households currently pay that monthly bill.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

This looks like a clean analytical problem — every number is filed and checkable — but it contains a specific trap that has fooled better investors than any of us, and learning to see it is worth more than any conclusion about Peloton.

The trap: a company can improve every year while getting worse.

Peloton's improvement is genuine. Two years ago it lost $1.26 billion; this year it earned a profit, generated $378 million of free cash, and cut net debt by 80%. Every one of those is a real, audited fact.

But there are two completely different ways a company can raise its profits. It can sell more — more customers, more revenue, more gross profit. Or it can spend less — fewer staff, fewer stores, less marketing. Both flow to the same line on the income statement, and from the outside they look identical.

They are not identical, because they have different futures. Selling more can compound indefinitely. Spending less is bounded by arithmetic: there is a floor beneath which you cannot cut and still have a business. Cost-cutting is a finite resource that looks exactly like an infinite one while you are still spending it.

Peloton's own guidance settles which kind this is. For next year it forecasts gross profit — revenue minus the direct cost of delivering it — to be flat or slightly lower, while promising higher overall earnings. That combination has only one meaning: every remaining dollar of improvement comes from cutting, not from selling. Management is telling you, in its own numbers, that the engine driving the recovery is nearly out of fuel.

Meanwhile the thing that would need to improve — customers — is going the other way. Subscribers fell 7.6% and are guided lower. In 2025 the total number of workouts performed on Peloton equipment declined for the first time in the company's history.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

A framework for any company whose numbers are improving:

  1. Separate the two engines. Track gross profit (selling more) apart from operating expenses (spending less). If gross profit is flat while earnings rise, you are watching a company shrink profitably — a real achievement, and a temporary one.
  2. Ask how much cutting is left. Peloton beat a $100 million savings target. That target does not renew. Estimate the remaining runway explicitly rather than extrapolating the trend.
  3. Find the customer number and put it above the profit number. Costs are management's to control; customers are not. The uncontrollable variable is the one that determines the outcome.
  4. Check whether they fixed the leak or just bailed faster. This is the sharpest version of the question. Apple in 1996 cut costs hard under Amelio and kept losing share. Apple in 1997–98 made the same cuts and shipped the iMac, which fixed the reason people were not buying. Same company, same cuts, different outcome — the difference was whether anyone repaired the actual product problem.
  5. Watch for pay that does not look like a cost. See section 7.

4. How They Make Money

Sell hardware at a modest margin — around 16 cents of profit per dollar — to acquire a subscriber who then pays roughly $44 a month at about 69 cents of profit per dollar. The equipment is close to a customer-acquisition expense; the subscription is where the money actually is. This is a good model, which is why the second-hand market does not hurt Peloton as much as people assume: someone buying a used bike for $400 still needs the subscription.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

The brand defined the category. There are 5.8 million total members. And there is a real lock-in: you bought a $1,500 machine that is far less useful without the subscription, so leaving costs you something psychological even when it costs nothing financial.

Be honest about the limits, though. In January 2026 a competitor, iFIT, launched its classes on Amazon Prime Video — reaching 200 million members with no separate subscription. Peloton charges up to $44 a month for the same category of product. When a competitor's distribution is free and yours is not, brand strength is being tested rather than relied upon.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

The subscriber decline simply continues. Peloton has cut costs about as far as it can, so from here every lost subscriber comes almost straight out of cash flow. Counting stock-based pay as the real cost it is, spare cash is roughly $179 million a year — and revenue only has to fall about 10% for that to reach zero. At the current rate of subscriber loss, that is about two and a half years. This is not a bankruptcy story; the balance sheet is genuinely repaired. It is a story about running out of room.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Think of buying a laundromat. The owner shows you $378,000 of cash left over last year on a $2.35 million asking price — a 16% return. Extraordinary.

Then you notice she does not pay her staff in cash. She pays them in shares of the laundromat. Last year she handed over $198,600 worth — and the number of owners has gone from 322 to 436 over four years. Your slice shrinks every year, and that dilution never appears in the cash box you were shown.

Count the staff properly and the real return is about 6%, not 16%. Measured on ordinary accounting, the business trades at roughly 37 times its earnings.

That is the whole case. Peloton is a genuinely repaired company at a price that already assumes the repair worked — and the one number that would prove it worked, the customer count, is still going the wrong way. Fixing your costs and fixing your business are different achievements, and only the second one is worth paying for.

Well. I came here expecting to find the usual: a memo that took EBITDA at face value, waved at the stock compensation, and called a melting ice cube a bargain because the puddle is still growing. Instead I find a memo that did my job for me on the one issue that matters, and did it better than the machine that fed it.

Now, EBITDA. Well, Charlie's covered most of the ground and he's covered it with a shovel, so let me just add a few things gently.

The toll bridge. Now here's where I'd want to be careful, because Peloton did have something. When a fellow has a thousand-dollar machine bolted together in his spare room, he doesn't switch lightly. Capital preservation. Rule number one. Thirty-four point seven million shares last year. Twenty-four and a half the year before.
