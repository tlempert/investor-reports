Munger's Verdict

CHKP — Check Point Software Technologies: Munger Synthesis

Price $130.35, 3 October 2026. Twelve reports read in full. Every per-share figure divides by the pipeline's 102.1M shares [CALC] unless stated.

1. The Moat Tribunal

Moat Tribunal: 0/5 SEVERE flags. 5 MODERATE flags by: Buffett, Biologist, Historian, Anthropologist, Psychologist. 0 MINOR/NONE. Outside the Tribunal, Bezos, Burry, Cook, Jobs, Sherlock and the Futurist also flag MODERATE. Lynch alone says MINOR. The Historian is not on a Casualty trajectory. He calls it "Undetermined": Survivor ~35%, Casualty ~25%, "Citrix-like stuck middle" ~40%. So the 14x cap does not apply. The panel agrees on the facts. Switching costs are real across 100,000+ organizations [SEC]. Three pressures are working against them at once: a shift to cloud-delivered SASE, where Check Point is a Gartner Niche Player [MEDIA]; a self-inflicted sales reset; and a run of exploited flaws in the company's own gateways. Nobody claims the moat is gone. Nobody can show it is widening. Result: Uncertain, leaning intact.

2. What the twelve votes really are

The tally is 0 BUY, 6 WAIT, 5 PASS and 1 SELL. Jev's independence audit is right to warn about it, and when I read the trigger lines myself the picture gets narrower still. Eight experts (Bezos, Buffett, Burry, Cook, Jobs, Psychologist, Historian, Anthropologist) built their triggers on the dossier's re-run of a 1.3%-growth perpetuity at 11.58%: ~$84 on owner earnings and ~$106 on FCF [CALC]. Three (Sherlock, Futurist, Biologist) argued for ~3% growth on the same perpetuity. Lynch alone used a different method, a 3-year scenario grid. Jev filed him under "zero-growth yield". That is wrong, because his grid assumes 5–7% growth. So this is one division, done three ways, at one discount rate. The 11.6–12.1% local cost of equity (E002) is cited by all twelve. The tally is therefore one witness wearing twelve hats: owner earnings of $8.52 a share against a hurdle of 11.58%.

The real question is whether "not at $130" survives once that shared input is removed. Only two independent witnesses remain:

  • Lynch's own grid. He weights Bull/Base/Bear at 30/45/25 and gets ~$159.5 by October 2029, about 7.0% a year from $130.35. It works on exit multiples of per-share FCF, so it is algebraically independent of the perpetuity. It falls short even of a 10% hurdle: discounted at 10%, his own value is ~$120.
  • My perpetuity (§5), with income paired to the cash that earns it. At 11.58% it puts value at $113–124 on every owner-earnings base, below the price. At 10% it does not hold: value is $133–147, above the price.

Sherlock's "6.5% yield + 3% growth ≈ 9.5%", the ~3.25% growth implied at 10%, and the 4.7% implied at 11.58% [CALC] are all the same equation, y + g against r. They are the perpetuity witness restated, not extra witnesses.

So $130.35 offers no margin of safety at the top of the hurdle band. At the bottom (10%) the margin is thin and positive, 2–12% depending on which owner-earnings base is used. Only Lynch's grid says no at both ends. The hurdle therefore decides how much margin there is, and with a 10% margin of safety it moves the verdict only in one corner: r = 10% together with FY2025 owner earnings. §5 and §7 return to this.

Some yields must not be counted twice. The FCF yield (~8.3%) and the owner yield (6.5%) are the same cash before and after the $205.6M SBC bill. The 5.1-point gap between 6.5% and 11.58% and the 4.7% implied growth are one fact stated two ways. P/B of 4.90x on a 37.6% ROE is P/E again, and is not a second witness.

3. Bull–Bear balance

The bull lines, all twelve: - Bezos: subscriptions +11–12% persist and new products turn spending into leverage. - Buffett: subscriptions persist and the installed base adopts the newer products. - Burry: product sales recover and the $1.92B of financial assets prove liquid. - Cook: the expanded sales team becomes productive. - Jobs: an integrated platform plus AI-security adoption. - Psychologist: retention holds through the incidents. - Sherlock: the multiple has already gone from 29.2x to 14.5x, so a clean Q4 re-rates a 6.5% owner yield. - Futurist: the 300 reps become productive in 2027 and the stock re-rates from 12.5x forward earnings. - Biologist: a 92.6% subscription gross margin on 100,000+ hosts. - Historian: an Adobe / Palo Alto-style transition at 13.4x. - Anthropologist: "a franchise priced as a relic" if the installed base is a tribe rather than a prison. - Lynch: ~$14 of per-share FCF by 2029, which at 14x gives ~$200.

Eleven of the twelve bull cases are the same sentence: subscriptions keep compounding and Q3 is the trough.

Lynch, answered rather than dismissed.

He is right that the +1% headline hides double-digit growth. Q2 subscriptions were $333M, +12%, and 49.4% of revenue [MEDIA]. In Q1 they were $323.2M against $290.6M (+11.2%, 48.4%), at a flat ~92.6% gross margin [SEC/CALC]. But the other half of the company is shrinking. In Q1, maintenance was $234.4M (+0.6%) and products $110.8M against $114.1M (−2.9%). Q2 product revenue fell 14% [MEDIA], and the non-subscription half fell about 7–8% (Futurist's arithmetic). Product gross margin went from 79.8% to 75.3% [CALC]. The Futurist asks how much of the subscription line is attached to gateways. The record cannot say, and that is the decisive unknown. If subscriptions ride on gateways, the growing half follows the shrinking half.

He is right that cheaper buybacks retire more shares per dollar. But the bucket leaks. The company bought back $1.30B, $1.29B, $1.30B and $1.40B in 2022–2025. In 2025 that was ~120% of FCF and ~144% of owner earnings, and about 10% of market cap, yet the share count fell only from 108M to 106M (−1.9%) [CALC]. Over 2021–25 it fell from 129M to 106M (−17.8%). SBC rose from $0.12B to $0.21B over the same years. Gross repurchases are a poor guide to what owners actually keep.

He is right that the CRO sale is small. He also caught a dossier error. Seddik sold 10,272 shares at $123.59 and kept 24,675, so he kept ~71%, not 29%. It is the only insider sale in the record and it is not evidence.

Where he is wrong: his own grid returns ~7% a year against an 11.6% hurdle, and he admits it is "a fair-priced stalwart, not a Lynch bargain." I agree with his verdict and his reasoning.

Burry's SELL, rejected. He has a point: the printed anchors of $119.46 and $150.30 imply 8.5–8.6% discount rates [CALC]. But a short needs the price to be wrong by more than the dispute over the hurdle, and this one is not. Against it stand 40% FCF margins and $2.23B of reconciled net cash. There is a fresh $2B buyback authorization, about 15% of the ~$13.3B market cap. Short interest is already 6.12% (6.23 days to cover). Management has a guided Q4 rebound to aim at. Burry's own key risk, the $1.92B of financial assets, is worth ~$19 a share against him, and the reconciliation in §6 says it is liquidity. Overvaluation of ~11% against my base, which disappears at a 10% hurdle, is a reason not to buy. It is not a reason to short.

I weight the bull argument at 35% and the bear argument at 65%. That is a weight on the arguments, not a scenario probability.

The Private Buyer test. Lynch's figure is $150–172 a share, 14–16x FCF [JUDGMENT]. That is a real number, and my buy zone is far below it. The whole gap is the cost of capital. A buyer earning ~7% on FCF plus ~3% growth is underwriting about 9–10%, which is exactly what the pipeline's anchors and the market price assume. With the US 10-year at 5.28%, debt costs make a leveraged deal hard to finance, and the record contains no bidder and no founder ownership figure. Gil Shwed is Executive Chair [SEC], and a take-private is not in evidence. I do not treat private value as a floor under the stock. It does tell the reader that a long-term owner with ~9.5% money would find $130 acceptable. A minority holder demanding 11.6% would not.

5. Valuation

One method throughout: capitalize operating owner earnings, then add net cash at face. - Strip the interest. Owner earnings are OCF − PP&E depreciation − SBC. I remove the after-tax financial income: $114.0M in 2025 [SEC] at the clean 2024 tax rate of 13% ($126.4M on $972.1M pre-tax [SEC]), about $99M. That income was earned on ~$2.9B of debt-free 2025 liquidity. Today's net cash is $2.23B, smaller, so the strip slightly over-charges, by roughly $1 a share. - Add back the cash that earned it. Net cash is $4.20B of liquidity ($2.28B + $1.92B, now reconciled as liquidity in §6) less $1.97B of borrowings, which treats the convertible at face. That is $2.23B, or $21.8/share. - Choose the owner-earnings base. TTM ($0.87B) is the low point of the series. It is low because Q2 OCF fell 35.3% with no cause given, and the reset that caused it is already charged in g, which is anchored on the reset-year guide. 1. Dead analogues (the casualties)

Novell NetWare (c. 1990s-2000s) [RECALL]. Novell was the dominant PC network operating system, commonly cited with a majority share of the market in the early 1990s. The threat was a bundled platform (Windows NT) that was "good enough" and came with the platform the customer already bought. The decline took roughly 5-8 years from the share peak to irrelevance, not a cliff. The sign that matters: reported revenue stayed respectable for years while the installed base eroded, so the headline numbers lagged the franchise's real position. Management's posture during the slide emphasized the installed base and directory services as the durable asset. The matching pattern for CHKP: a franchise that is still large (100,000+ organizations [SEC per company release]) and profitable (FY2025 net income $1.06B [CALC]) can erode below the surface for years. The dossier's own gap, "no historical revenue decline" (stress test header [CALC]), means there is no cost-behavior evidence for a shrinking Check Point.

BlackBerry / RIM (2009-2014) [RECALL]. A "secure, trusted by enterprise" brand with a high-margin hardware-plus-service model and a loyal installed base. Roughly 20% global smartphone share at peak by most accounts, and revenue that fell by more than half in about two years after its fiscal-2012 peak. The killer was a new platform paradigm, not a better version of the same product. The fastest and most violent collapse of the three. What it shows for CHKP: a "trust and security" moat does not protect against a platform shift, and once the unit-sales line turns, the recurring-service line follows with a lag. CHKP's equivalent is the appliance/product line (-2.9% Q1 [SEC / CALC], -14% Q2 [MEDIA]) turning first while subscription (+11-12%) is still growing. That is the same ordering BlackBerry showed in its early stage, but it is also the ordering of every healthy subscription transition (see Adobe). The ordering alone does not discriminate.

Citrix (c. 2015-2022) [RECALL] - the "stuck middle" case, not a death. A dominant, high-margin infrastructure vendor that moved to subscription slowly under activist pressure, lost growth, and was eventually taken private (reported at roughly $16-17B). It did not die. It stopped compounding, and holders earned a poor return relative to the market for years. I include it because the most probable bad outcome for CHKP is probably not BlackBerry; it is Citrix: a still-profitable franchise growing at +1-6% [CALC / MEDIA] while the market rewards the 22-25% growers (CrowdStrike +22%, SentinelOne +22%, Zscaler +25% [MEDIA per "Cyber Security Market Size 2026" excerpt]). The 2-year total return of -31.72% [SEARCH per Yahoo-sourced analysis] is already paying holders for that outcome partially.

2. Survivor analogues (the adapters)

Adobe, Creative Suite to Creative Cloud (2011-2014) [RECALL]. Adobe moved from perpetual-license boxes to subscription. Adobe told the market what to measure. The dossier shows no equivalent for CHKP, so I cannot verify the transition is working.

Palo Alto Networks, "platformization" (announced Feb 2024) [RECALL]. A firewall franchise that chose to take a near-term billings and guidance hit to push customers onto a multi-product platform; the stock fell by roughly a quarter on the day, and the strategy was subsequently vindicated by results (I am not certain of the later numbers).
