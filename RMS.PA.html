Munger's Verdict

Munger Synthesis: Hermès International SCA (RMS.PA)

Price €1,286.00 [CALC: pipeline close], 2026-10-05. Valuation currency EUR. Hurdle: the dossier's LOCAL (EUR) cost of equity of 8.19% (EUR 10-year 3.18% + Damodaran total ERP 5.01%, i.e. 4.23% mature + 0.78% country, beta 1, no judgment premium). The US 10-year (5.35%) and the USD cost of equity (10.36%) are not the hurdle and I do not use them. Perpetual growth is capped at 3.18% in EUR. There is no 10-K or 8-K for this French IFRS issuer, so there is no [SEC] tag anywhere below. LATEST QUARTER and CASH CONVERSION blocks are absent from the raw dossier; H1 2026 figures reach us through press summaries of the 2026-07-29 release, and there is no QUARTERLY REVENUE TRAJECTORY block.

1. The Moat Tribunal

Moat Tribunal: 0/5 SEVERE flags. 2 MODERATE flags, from Buffett and the Psychologist. 3 MINOR, from the Biologist, the Historian and the Anthropologist. Outside the Tribunal, Bezos, Burry, Cook and Jobs flag MODERATE; Sherlock, the Futurist and Lynch flag MINOR. The Historian calls a Survivor Trajectory at about 80% confidence: Ferrari and Burberry, not Pierre Cardin or Coach. That rules out the Casualty cap. Every flag comes down to the same two exhibits. On one side, the average Birkin/Kelly resale premium fell from 2.2x retail in 2022 to 1.4x, with shorter wait lists. That comes from a single source, a Simply Wall St narrative [MEDIA]. On the other side, gross margin rose 40 bp to 71.1% in H1 2026 and the recurring operating margin held at 41% in FY2025 and 41.0% in H1 2026 [SEARCH per FT Markets; MEDIA per Quartr]. The resale data also cuts the other way: a regular-leather Birkin averages €17,750 and a Kelly €14,835 (July 2026 [MEDIA per Monitor]), and Birkin value retention is 122% [MEDIA per Rebag]. My reading is that the excess scarcity rent is thinning but has not been lost. Normal Quality Floor applies.

2. The Bull-Bear Balance

The twelve bull cases. Bezos: capacity grows without weakening exclusivity while the 41% margin holds. Buffett: scarce artisans and pricing power sustain growth. Burry: pricing power plus new workshops produce double-digit FCF growth. Cook: workshop capacity lifts sales at a 41% margin. Jobs: control of production and distribution sustains pricing power. Psychologist: capacity expands without diluting scarcity. Sherlock: a family-controlled maker-retailer at a 2-year low re-rates toward its old 50x peaks. Futurist: demand exceeds production, the margin holds, and the multiple is off its peak. Biologist: compounds 8–10% for a decade from a −39.29% two-year low. Historian: compounds FCF/share at 8%+ while the multiple only falls to the low 30s. Anthropologist: the Birkin ritual sustains 41% margins as the Americas (+15%) and Japan (+11%) carry growth. Lynch: the best business in luxury at the bottom of its range. Ten of the twelve cite one fact, that the 41% margin persists. Buffett's and Jobs's rest on pricing power and control of production without citing the figure. That makes them one witness, not ten.

Lynch, addressed and not dismissed. His central claim is right: the narrative broke and the business did not. Several of his counter-arguments hold on the numbers. The Q2 miss was €4.05B against €4.08B, about 0.7%, yet the shares fell 7.9% premarket, and Q2 constant-FX growth of 7% was an acceleration. The FX drag of €360M explains why H1 grew +2% reported against +6.1% constant FX. In the same half, adjusted FCF rose 18.1% to €2.18B and operating cash flow rose 16% to €2.7B. China was flat and the Middle East −4%, but the Americas grew +15%. The Simply Wall St "overvalued by 106.6%" has no stated assumptions, and I give it no weight either. Two of his own items remain open, as he concedes. The first is (d), resale compression. The second is (f): net margin fell 32.1% → 30.3% → 28.3% and ROIC 71.8% → 70.2% → 65.2% → 60.3%, with no decomposition in the dossier. Where Lynch and I part is the exit multiple. His probability-weighted three-year return of 7.9% a year against the 8.19% hurdle rests on a base case that exits at 30x on FY2028e EPS of €52. That is today's multiple held constant. His €1,180 trigger is the same exit-multiple machinery with a 2.8-point cushion. His scenario uses the same identity as my DCF, solved for price instead of value. What it adds is one independent input: the consensus EPS path (€44.16 for FY2026e, €49.50 for FY2027e). I do not accept his assumption that a 30x exit is the base case.

Independence (Jev). All twelve trigger prices come from a scenario grid or DCF, and eight of the twelve key metrics are the same division: a 3.1% FCF yield (3.4% on EV, owner yield 3.2%, earnings yield 3.3%) against 8.19%. The zero-BUY tally is therefore one calculation run twelve times, and the tally counts as one witness. Its size adds nothing.

On price, I can count one witness and one independent input, and no more. Lynch's exit-multiple scenario, my DCF and the required-growth table in §3 all solve the same identity, P·(1+h)^n = M·E₀·(1+g)^n, each for a different unknown. That makes them one witness in three hats. The table in particular is a restatement of the price, not evidence about it. The one input that does not come from price, hurdle and owner EPS is the consensus EPS path that Lynch brings in. It sits near the growth my central case assumes. The operating witnesses (Cook on inventory, Burry on forensics, the Anthropologist on culture) all speak to quality and not to price.

Weighting. I weight the bull argument at 45% and the bear argument at 55%. The bulls are right about the franchise. FY2025 constant-FX growth of +9% came in a luxury market Bain estimates fell 3% with about 50 million customers lost, so Hermès is taking share. The bears are right that the price leaves no margin. They overstate how far the growth it needs sits above the record, because the record depends on the window you pick. Shares are flat at 105M, so per-share and aggregate growth are the same.

3. Decision Logic: Ceiling and Floor

Ceiling: hidden value. Bezos finds no cash incinerator. Perfume and Beauty fell 4%, but no segment profit is disclosed, so core equals consolidated and core P/E is the headline 29.9x. Operating leverage is visible: SG&A rose from €3.57B to €3.70B (+3.6%) against +5.3% revenue growth, taking it from 23.5% to 23.1% of revenue. These are [UNVERIFIED]-tier figures from an OPEX block mislabelled "SEC XBRL"/"$". Burry finds no cost dumping and clean forensics. Receivables fell 12.5% to €0.42B (DSO 11.5 → 9.6 days). Inventory fell 7.9% to €2.58B and DIO went 237 → 226 → 203. SBC is €0.13B, 0.8% of revenue and about 2% of TTM operating cash flow of €5.74B. Borrowings are €0.01B; the €2.35B FORENSIC "debt" is almost entirely the €2.28B of lease liabilities. Recurring operating income of €6.6B plus D&A of €926M gives about €7.5B, against €4.52B of net income. The gap sits below the operating line: tax, the financial result and the French exceptional contribution.

Stress test. Burry's −20% revenue case takes adjusted FCF from €4.21B to €2.97B, a fall of about 29%, and the margin from 26.3% to 23.2%. At −10% FCF is €3.59B, and at −30% it is €2.36B. Adjusted FCF turns negative only at about a 70% revenue decline. The cost stickiness behind these figures is an industry default, not observed Hermès behaviour. The business survives a severe downturn. The valuation does not: at −20%, today's price buys a 2.2% FCF yield. Buffett confirms an intact moat but not a widening one. The Futurist's "Premium Justified" is narrow, at the hurdle with nothing to spare. The €12.30B of net cash (9.1% of market cap, against €12.31B of cash and short-term investments) is already in my model, so the hidden value is counted and the ceiling is not raised.

Floor: moat and quality. Buffett finds pricing power but says it fails the chaos test, because "a recession does not make a luxury handbag essential." Jobs grades the product "Insanely Great." The Biologist classes Hermès as a "Symbiont (Healthy)" with about 93% ten-year survival. The Anthropologist calls it a "Cultural Institution (Enduring)." That is a high-quality compounder, which earns the Quality Floor. On the Franchise Premium test, criterion 1 (no SEVERE flags) passes. Criterion 2 passes by the margin route: 41% in FY2025 (up 0.5 points on 2024 per the Q4 call) and 41.0% in H1 2026. Criterion 3 fails, because there is no contracted backlog (none disclosed, not applicable) and the revenue is about 100% individual, repeat but not contracted. Criterion 4 fails, because customers can walk out. No Franchise Floor.

Leases. The pipeline's FCF of €4.21B (€40.10 per share on 105M shares) and its owner-earnings figure of €4.32B are probably before IFRS 16 lease repayments. A boutique network renews its leases, so the right charge is a recurring one, not a one-time deduction of the €2.28B liability. The dossier's H1 2026 bridge sizes that charge. Operating cash flow of €2.7B, less operating investments of €0.3B, less adjusted FCF "after repayment of lease" of €2.18B, leaves about €0.22B for the half. Rounding on the inputs (±€0.05B on each of the first two) gives €0.12–0.32B. I annualise that to about €0.44B (range €0.23–0.65B). That assumes the two halves are similar; I have no seasonality data. There is one more caveat, E036. The Flash report puts capex at about €1.0B a year, which would be roughly €0.5B for a half. If that figure is right, the bridge residual nearly vanishes. The dossier gives no FY2025 operating cash flow, so I cannot tell which capex figure the pipeline FCF embeds. I keep the company's own €0.3B line, because it sits in the same bridge as the lease figure. I charge €0.44B a year in perpetuity, growing with the business, and do not also deduct the liability. Owner earnings after leases are €3.77B, or €35.90 per share.

Valuation date. The date is 2026-06-30, the balance-sheet date. The €12.30B of net cash already contains H1 2026 cash flow, so I count only the H2 half of FY2026, at half a year, and roll the result forward to 5 October at the hurdle.

Model. The model is two-stage at 8.19%: an above-cap stage of N years, then a 3.0% terminal (JUDGMENT, under the 3.18% cap). The growth grid is re-sourced against the record range in §2. 5% sits below every smoothed cash record and near consensus FY2026 revenue growth of +4.5%. 7% is about the consensus two-year EPS path (€43.07 → €49.50, 7.2% a year), haircut from the smoothed 7.7% FCF/share record. 9% is the smoothed net-income record and FY2025's +9% constant-FX growth. Roughly 3 points of any path are volume (24 → 28 workshops by 2030, about 3% a year, given an 18-month artisan qualification path); the rest is price and mix.

Stage length. I take N = 15 years (JUDGMENT). The workshop plan is visible only to 2030. The moat half-life I assign is 25+ years. Above-cap growth should last less than the moat itself, and the resale-premium gauge argues against the full half-life. 10 and 20 years are the sweep.

4. Final Decision

Decision: WAIT. Moat Tribunal Result: Strong. All twelve experts agree the franchise is intact. None flags SEVERE. Buffett and the Psychologist dissent only to MODERATE, both on the resale premium. The disagreement on this council is not about the business. It is about whether the price leaves anything for the buyer. On that, there is one valuation witness, the identity in §3, plus the consensus EPS path, and they read the same: the price is about fair, with no margin. Burry's SELL (a 1% short) is the valuation argument taken to its end, and he concedes that he finds "no demonstrated operating fracture." Of the two ends, the Futurist's and Anthropologist's 2% HOLDs now sit closer to my arithmetic than Burry's short. At €1,286 my base path (7% for 15 years) earns about 8.1% a year, essentially the 8.19% hurdle. That is a fair price for a great business. It is not a price with a margin of safety in it, and on a long-duration asset a 10% margin buys only about 0.6 points of return.

Flip condition. The ceiling reaches today's price when the central reaches about €1,429. On a uniform path, that takes about 8.2% growth sustained for 15 years (10.2% if only 10 years are credited, 7.2% if 20). Evidence that FY2026–27 owner earnings after leases are compounding above 8%, together with a resale premium holding at 1.4x, would flip this to BUY at today's price. The 8% path alone does not quite get there: it is worth €1,402, with a ceiling of €1,262.

Buy Zone: €888–€1,153

The "Munger Buy Zone": €888 (absurdly cheap) to €1,153 (fair-value limit). The ceiling is the €1,281 central value less a 10% margin of safety. At €1,153 the base path earns about 8.7% a year. That is thin, which is why the size steps up only toward the floor.

The floor now uses the same cash-flow arithmetic as the ceiling. It is the bear path (5% for only 10 years) worth €987, less the same 10%. At €888 the bear path earns about 8.8% and the base path about 10.4%. Printed the other way for comparison, the band method (18x post-lease owner EPS of €35.90, plus €117.14 of net cash) gives €763, and the growth-free value (3% from today) is €858. I do not use the band for the floor, for the same reason I do not use it for the ceiling. I raised the floor for quality, though not to a Franchise Floor, and I did not raise the ceiling for hidden value.

Why could it get there? The cause would be fear and boredom, not complexity. The stock has already fallen 39.29% from a €2,781.45 high close to a low close of today's €1,286. It is −26.31% year to date and +21.44% over five years. Consensus is being cut. China is flat, the Middle East went from double-digit growth in FY2025 to −4%, and the Americas (+15%) are carrying the group just as tariffs are named but unsized. A further 10% fall is well within the volatility of the past two years. A second Q2-style print, a 0.7% miss met with a 7.9% drop, would do it.

Moat Half-Life: 25+ years for the Birkin/Kelly franchise. The constraint is artisan labour: 27,107 employees, 61% in France, and 600 jobs added in Q2 2026. More than 92% of sales go through owned stores. These are structural, and the regulatory items (EUDR leather traceability, CSRD, Digital Product Passports, the dismissed Cavalleri tying case) favour a vertically integrated house. The leading gauge is the resale premium. If it falls through 1.0x, the scarcity signal is gone and the half-life shortens sharply.

TOO UNCERTAIN check: 0 of 5 tripwires. There are no SEVERE flags. The load-bearing variable is a growth rate, not a regime. No PASS cites unknowability. The margin of safety is ordinary. Nothing here is "too hard." It is too expensive.

Position Size: 0% at €1,286. Inside the zone the size is 2% at ≤ €1,153, rising to the Moderate cap of 3% at ≤ €888. The conviction is Moderate, held there by named unresolved inputs: - the growth path (the 5–9% paths span €1,038–€1,555, more than 20% of central); - the length of the above-cap stage (10–20 years spans €1,144–€1,416, 21% of central); - the annual lease charge (€0.23–0.65B from the H1 bridge, with the capex conflict); - whether FCF already includes interest on the cash; - the end date of the French contribution; - the resale premium's direction.

The verdict holds under every stage length swept: even at 20 years, the €1,274 ceiling sits below the €1,286 price.

Historian of Disruption: RMS.PA (Hermès International SCA)

0. Framing: which disruption is the right question?

Section H and the Biologist/Historian facts say it plainly: "no AI or platform disruptor appears; the competitor set in the raw is other luxury houses" [MEDIA]. The Christensen threat (a cheaper, good-enough entrant eating the bottom of the market) is not visible. The relevant historical failure modes for a luxury house are different: 1. Brand dilution and over-extension (licensing, outlets, discounting, entry-price creep). 2. Cycle and tourist-flow shocks (China, US tariffs). 3. Valuation compression of an excellent business bought at a peak multiple (the Nifty Fifty problem).

I score Hermès on all three. The first two are the "moat decay" questions. The third is why the verdict is WAIT, not BUY.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (the casualties)

1a. Pierre Cardin, 1970s-80s [HISTORY]. A prestige couture name that licensed itself into hundreds of product categories and hundreds of licensees worldwide. Revenue rose while the brand's scarcity was destroyed. The decline took about a decade and was invisible in the P&L until it was irreversible. Management language at the time was about "reach" and "democratization of luxury." - Hermès contrast: >92% of 2025 sales are through own stores [MEDIA per Find My Moat]. Production is in-house in France (24 workshops) [MEDIA]. There is no licensing leak visible in the raw. This is the opposite structure.

1b. Coach, 2013-2016 [HISTORY]. The closest handbag-economics analogue, and Tapestry (TPR) is already in the dossier's peer table at 16.1x P/E [CALC]. Coach had very high margins (roughly 30% operating, from memory), strong brand heat, and a premium multiple. It chased volume via outlets and promotions. The affordable-luxury entrant (Michael Kors) took the heat. Sales stalled, the multiple compressed from the 20s to the low teens, and a turnaround took about three years under new management. What killed it was brand-heat loss through discount-channel leakage. The signature was inventory building in discount channels while headline margins stayed high. - Hermès contrast: inventory fell 7.9% in 2025 (€2.80B → €2.58B) while revenue grew ~5%; DIO fell 237 → 226 → 203 days [CALC: WORKING CAPITAL block]. Receivables fell 12.5% [CALC]. These are the opposite of the Coach signature. The only DIO caveat is that inventory is still 203 days of COGS, but that is a function of craft production and raw hides, and composition is not in the raw.

1c. Coca-Cola / Nifty Fifty franchises, 1972-74 and 1998-2008 [HISTORY]. These are the right analogue for the valuation problem, not the moat problem. Gillette, Coca-Cola, Xerox and Polaroid were bought at 40-50x earnings in 1972 as "one-decision" stocks. The superior ones (Coke) survived fully, yet shareholders earned poor real returns for 10+ years because earnings growth in the high single digits could not offset multiple compression from ~45x to the teens. Coke repeated this in 1998-2008. The Flash report cites Hermès "historical peaks near 50x" trailing P/E vs 29.8x-34.3x now [MEDIA, undated source]. The stock peaked at €2,781.45 and is at €1,286.00 [CALC]. - Time in the casualty pattern: 6-12 years of flat or negative real returns while business results stayed fine.

Scorecard for the dead analogues: Hermès shares none of the operating signatures of 1a and 1b. It does share the price-structure signature of 1c, partly unwound.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (the adapters)

2a. Burberry, 2006-2013 [HISTORY]. It had been over-licensed and over-exposed (the check pattern on everything). It bought back licences, cut distribution, vertically integrated and went direct-to-consumer. Margins rose and the stock re-rated. The lever was control of the channel. - Hermès: already at the end state, with >92% direct [MEDIA per Find My Moat]. Hermès did not need this turnaround because it never left the model.

2b. Ferrari (RACE, also in the dossier's peer table at 35.7x P/E [CALC]), 2015-present [HISTORY]. It deliberately builds fewer cars than demand, runs an order book and a personalization ladder, and keeps scarcity as the policy. It was priced as a luxury house and not as an automaker. It survived and thrived because supply discipline is a corporate decision rather than a circumstance. - Hermès parallel: "demand exceeds physical production" [MEDIA per Flash report]; the artisan constraint (18-month qualification) is the capacity governor [MEDIA per Find My Moat]; capacity is added slowly (23 → 24 workshops in Sept 2025, four more 2026-2030) [MEDIA per Investing.com slides].

2c. Loro Piana / Brunello Cucinelli, "quiet luxury" [MEDIA per Business Insider]. The dossier says quiet luxury benefited these houses and Hermès more than some LVMH houses. This is a within-industry survivor cohort. It is a tailwind and also a crowding risk (see section 4).

2d. Visa/Mastercard-style survivor pattern (invest through cycles, keep the toll). For Hermès it maps onto the capital-allocation record: capex ≈ €1.0B/yr vs total D&A €926M = 1.1x [MEDIA per Flash report; CALC]. The investment is modest, not heroic.

The Real Charlie Munger's Take

Here is a wonderful business: 41% margins, no debt, the artisans in France and the customers on a waiting list. The memo even counts its cash after the leases, which is more honesty than most of Wall Street manages in a career. Then it does a foolish thing. It runs one calculation three times and calls the agreement corroboration. A required-growth table that takes the price, the hurdle and the earnings and hands them back to you as a growth rate is not a witness. It is the price talking to itself. I have seen this Lollapalooza before: three hats, one head.

The memo is also stingier than it knows. It says the moat lasts 25 years, then lets the business grow above inflation for ten. It calls 4.7% "the record" when it is the one start year in four that flatters the bear. And it hands the reader a flip condition that flips nothing. On leases it is too kind, because shops get re-leased forever and a one-time deduction pretends they don't. On SBC there is no rat poison worth the name, at 0.8% of revenue, and nobody here is peddling EBITDA. Pricing power is real: gross margin rose while the resale premium fell, which tells you the excess scarcity rent is thinning but the rent itself is intact. Is it too hard? No. Is it cheap? No. Sit on your ass. But fix the arithmetic first, so that when the price comes in you know which number you are waiting for.

The Real Warren Buffett's Take

I'd be happy to own this business forever, and I wouldn't be happy to pay 32 times owner earnings for it with fresh money. The memo gets that much right, and gets it right for the right reason: a 3.1% cash yield against an 8% cost of capital has to be made up by growth, and you'd better be sure of the growth. Where I'd push back is on consistency. If you tell me the franchise has a quarter-century half-life, show me what a quarter-century runway is worth before you tell me the price is "less than its cost of capital." And if you tell me the record is 4.7%, show me the record from the other end of the table too.

Rule No. 1 is not to lose money, and nothing in this file suggests a buyer at today's price loses money on the business. The risk is a decade of so-so returns while the multiple settles. That is the Coca-Cola lesson of 1998. The toll bridge is real, and the moat is wide. The family are owners, not promoters: they disclose the tax hit by name, they don't issue stock, and they don't play games with buybacks. The only thing wrong here is the price, and a fair price on a great business is a fine reason to keep a list and wait.
