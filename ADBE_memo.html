Adobe as an investment

An exceptional cash-generating franchise is priced as though artificial intelligence will weaken its toll bridge faster than Adobe can rebuild it.

I would buy Adobe with moderate conviction. The council’s gated verdict is BUY, supported by six BUY votes, four HOLD votes, two PASS votes and no SELL votes [11; calculation]. The central risk is not whether Adobe can add artificial intelligence to its products; it is whether inexpensive, AI-native alternatives would weaken pricing power and customer retention before Adobe converts its expanding audience into paying customers.

Adobe remains unusually profitable and deeply embedded in professional workflows, yet the prosumer edge is already contested and management does not disclose net revenue retention, churn or customer-satisfaction scores. My interpretation: the market’s concern is directionally reasonable, but the current price assumes more franchise deterioration than the filed cash flows presently demonstrate.

My decision is to buy at the ledger price of $252.23 [6; calculation], against a central value of $314.97 [6; calculation], a ceiling of $267.72 after a 15% margin of safety [6; calculation; 7; judgment], and a no-growth floor of $180.86 [6; calculation], while limiting the position to 4% [7; judgment].

What you would own

Adobe reports three units. Digital Media contains the creative and document businesses through which individuals and professionals create, edit and distribute visual and written work. Digital Experience sells marketing and customer-experience tools to organizations. Publishing and Advertising is the much smaller collection of publishing and advertising products. In fiscal 2025, these units produced revenue of $17.649 billion, $5.864 billion and $256 million respectively [1; filing].

The moat is experienced less as possession of a particular feature than as dependence on a working system: familiar tools, trained employees, established file formats, internal processes and contracted enterprise deployments. Adobe ended its latest reported quarter with $22.16 billion of remaining performance obligations, 67% classified as current [4; filing]. Subscription revenue represented 96.4% of fiscal 2025 revenue [1; calculation]. That evidence supports organizational switching costs, although cancellation penalties under regulatory scrutiny mean the contracted balance should not be treated as pure customer preference [13; search].

My interpretation: the reader would own a wide professional franchise with an eroding low-end perimeter—not an obsolete software vendor, but not an impregnable platform either [12; judgment].

The two measures differ because the council’s owner-earnings convention subtracts maintenance capital expenditure and the full economic cost of stock-based compensation, whereas conventional free cash flow does not deduct stock compensation. Fiscal 2025 stock-based compensation was $1.942 billion [2; filing]. My interpretation: owner earnings are the more conservative measure for shareholders because issuing employees shares is an economic expense even when it is added back in the cash-flow statement.

The bull case and the counterargument

The strongest bull case is that Adobe can use its existing professional position to absorb generative AI rather than be displaced by it. Its advantages include commercial safety, licensed training inputs, content credentials and indemnification, alongside a professional base whose workflows cannot be replaced as casually as a consumer design application [12; judgment]. More than one billion monthly users and AI-first ARR growth above 150% show that distribution and adoption are real [4; filing]. The professional core is reinforced by $22.16 billion of contracted obligations and 96.4% subscription revenue [4; filing; 1; calculation].

The counterargument is economic. Adobe may attract enormous usage while monetizing too little of it, incur higher inference costs, and lose pricing power at the edge. Management’s freemium strategy was associated with approximately $500 million of near-term ARR suppression, while stock-photo defection reduced net-new ARR by approximately $70 million; the conversion rate is not disclosed [10; media]. Cost of subscription revenue was growing 13% against subscription revenue growth of 11.6% [1; filing].

A simplified example is the disclosed scale contrast: AI-first ARR was approximately 1.8%–2% of total ARR even while growing more than 150% [4; filing; 5; calculation]. That is evidence of rapid adoption, but not yet evidence that the new stream could replace pressure on the larger base. This example is illustrative, not an incremental valuation or forecast.

My interpretation: the bull case strengthens if new usage becomes paid recurring revenue before delivery costs finish compressing margins. My interpretation: the bear case strengthens if low-end substitution migrates into professional teams or if regulatory limits make cancellation and switching materially easier. The EU Data Act’s zero-exit-fee requirement expected in 2027 falls within the council’s five-year horizon, although the dossier’s source for that date is a single vendor compliance publication [13; search].

Conditions that would support buying

  • Organic ARR remains healthy after excluding acquired revenue.
  • The freemium audience begins converting into disclosed paid usage or recurring revenue.
  • Professional adoption and contracted obligations remain resilient.
  • Cash conversion stays aligned with reported revenue growth.
  • Repurchases continue to reduce shares after employee issuance.

Conditions that would support waiting

  • Management continues reporting headline ARR without reconciling organic growth.
  • The new leadership team has not yet explained its capital-allocation priorities.
  • AI adoption expands while its contribution to margins remains unclear.
  • Cancellation regulation is unresolved and retention metrics remain undisclosed.
  • The quotation sits near the upper boundary of an estimate whose honest resolution is roughly plus or minus $10 [12; judgment].

Conditions that would invalidate the thesis

  • Professional customers, rather than only prosumers, begin switching away.
  • Organic ARR falls into a pattern consistent with core erosion.
  • AI delivery costs produce persistent deterioration in unit economics.
  • Acquisitions displace disciplined repurchases without adequate returns.
  • Management’s continued silence makes retention impossible to underwrite.

My interpretation: repurchases create value only when they outrun dilution and occur below intrinsic value; the same management cannot defend expensive historic purchases while presenting cheaper current purchases as proof of discipline.

The next review should answer five questions

  1. What is organic ending-ARR growth excluding acquired revenue, disclosed in the fiscal-year results or earnings materials?
  2. What percentage of freemium users convert to paid subscriptions or purchased generative credits, disclosed through product metrics?
  3. What are net revenue retention and gross churn by major customer group, disclosed in investor materials or regulatory filings?
  4. What is operating income by reported segment, disclosed in the annual report?
  5. What are the new chief executive’s priorities for repurchases, employee issuance and acquisitions, stated in the first full capital-allocation discussion?

The answers should be evaluated together: growth without conversion, retention without margin economics, or repurchases without net share reduction would not resolve the thesis.
