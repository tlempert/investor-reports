Munger's Verdict

LVMH Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton (MC.PA) — Munger Synthesis

Analysis date 2026-10-04 · price €378.55 (2-year low close) · Euronext Paris · IFRS filer, EUR

This is a wonderful business at a price that already pays for its recovery. The brands are not breaking; the arithmetic is. Cycle-normalized owner earnings are about €11.5B. Even at the bottom of the dossier's 10.3–11.3% cost of equity, €378.55 needs them to grow about 4.1% a year forever. Put differently, the price is fair only if the 2023 peak cash earning power comes back and then compounds at 3%. I would own LVMH happily at a price that asks for 3% from mid-cycle. That price is about €280.

"Company" below means LVMH's own published figures seen through secondary pages ([SEARCH per ...]): FY2025 revenue €80.8B, profit from recurring operations (PRO) €17.8B, free cash flow €11.3B, Group-share net profit €10.9B. Over the same year the net margin moved 14.8% → 13.5%, so the two lines fell by similar amounts. A brand that loses customers but keeps its gross margin is still a brand. The Biologist's ~92% ten-year franchise survival is a fair statement of it. The question here is the price of growth. Survival is not in doubt.

3. Cash truth

Lines [CALC], 2022→2025: net income €14.08B, €15.17B, €12.55B, €10.88B (CAGR −8.2%); FCF €12.75B, €10.60B, €13.37B, €14.21B; owner earnings €11.61B → €10.87B. Revenue went €86.2B → €84.7B → €80.8B (−3.2%/yr 2023–25). Net income fell 28% on revenue down 6.3%. The net margin went 17.6% → 13.5% over 2023–25. The record has no 2023 operating margin; for 2024→2025 the operating margin went 23.1% → 22.0% and the net margin 14.8% → 13.5%.

The €2.91B FCF gap (Burry's key metric). Under IFRS 16, lease principal sits in financing cash flow. The pipeline's €14.21B (operating cash flow minus capex) therefore excludes lease cash. The company's €11.3B is, from my background knowledge of LVMH's "operating free cash flow" definition, struck after lease repayments. The record does not verify that. Owner earnings take a different route: operating cash flow minus depreciation. Yahoo's ttm operating cash flow of €18.64B [MEDIA] minus €10.87B owner earnings [CALC] is €7.77B, about the size of total D&A of €8.0B [CALC]. The dossier says that D&A likely includes right-of-use (lease) depreciation. If that holds, owner earnings already carry roughly €8B of depreciation, the leased stores included. Two different subtractions, the company's FCF after lease payments and owner earnings after total depreciation, land within 4% of each other (€11.3B vs €10.87B). This is an inference across mixed periods (ttm vs FY), not a reconciliation. The dossier's own words are weaker than a finding: the IFRS 16 cause is "one candidate, unverified", D&A "likely" includes right-of-use depreciation, and forensic debt "appears to" include leases. So I treat the lease reading as likely, not settled, and give each branch exactly one lease charge.

  • Branch A (likely): owner earnings already bear the lease cost as a flow, through right-of-use depreciation. The liability is not deducted. On this branch, subtracting the €16.63B liability as well counts the stores twice, about €34 a share. Burry made his own trigger conditional on this. Bezos, Buffett, Jobs and Cook subtracted net debt and the liability. The Psychologist subtracted the liability but no net debt. Re-run on each author's own inputs, with only the lease deduction removed and net debt deducted where it was omitted, the five PASS triggers become €225–254. Burry's becomes €235–265.
  • Branch B (adverse): owner earnings do not bear lease cash. Then the ~€2.9B a year is charged as a perpetual flow, because the stores are renewed rather than run off, and the liability is again not deducted. That is one charge, and the harsher one. Deducting the liability instead gives the milder variant, about €34 a share below Branch A.

Both branches are priced in §4.

The working-capital swings, and what the cash line really did. Trade working capital (inventory + receivables − payables, balance-sheet deltas [CALC]; the record has no cash-flow working-capital line) went €15.79B (2022) → €18.63B → €19.77B → €18.77B (2025). That is an absorption of €2.84B in 2023 and €1.14B in 2024, and a release of €1.00B in 2025. Adding it back gives owner earnings before working capital: €14.07B (2023), €12.27B (2024), €9.87B (2025). The 2022 figure cannot be split because the 2021 row is NaN. One caveat runs one way: balance-sheet deltas include currency translation, and a stronger euro shrinks EUR inventory without cash (FY2025 revenue carried −3% of currency [SEARCH]), so part of the 2025 "release" may not be cash. On this line, cash is not flat. It fell ~30% from 2023 to 2025, in step with net income (−28%). The reported owner-earnings series looked flat because inventory was built at the peak and run down at the trough. As the Historian said, that run-down cannot be repeated. FY2025's €10.87B (owner EPS €22.05) is flattered by the release. The pre-working-capital trough is €9.87B (€20.02 a share).

So there is a trough cushion, and the base must be cycle-normalized. The 2023–25 compression is partly demand cycle and partly structural. On the cycle side: margins held, organic growth turned positive in Q2 2026, and the inventory is being worked down. On the structural side: the Bain pool is down ~15% and Fashion & Leather is still negative. I therefore weight peak and trough equally and do not treat the 2023 peak as normal. The base is the 2023–25 pre-working-capital average of €12.07B, less recurring working-capital investment at my 3% growth on €18.77B of working capital (€0.56B a year). That gives €11.51B, about €23.35 a share. This base charges the 2023–24 build as non-recurring and charges only the growth-proportional slice as recurring.

Forensics are clean but not spotless. Inventory fell 4.3% against revenue down 4.6%, receivables fell 8.5%, and DIO sits in a 297–312 band (303 now). DPO fell 128 → 110 days. ROIC fell from 19.5% to 14.8%. Goodwill fell €24.8B → €18.3B, cause not attributed. Buybacks of $5.15B (label unverified) cut the share count only 1.0% (501M → 496M). The €13.00 dividend is a 3.43% yield and costs ~€6.4B: 59% of net income, 57% of company FCF. Net debt is €8.28B excluding leases and €24.91B including them; the forensic DEBT line of €36.73B appears to include leases. Cash of €12.20B sits against €20.48B of borrowings.

Stress test (dossier, anchored on €14.21B): adjusted FCF goes €14.21B → €10.82B (−10% revenue) → €7.44B (−20%) → €4.06B (−30%), and turns negative at approximately −45% revenue. On the company's €11.3B base, −20% gives ~€5.9B, below the €6.4B dividend. Burry's 20% test therefore passes on survival and fails on the dividend. It is a valuation risk, not a solvency risk.

Cultural Anthropologist: LVMH (MC.PA)

Louis Vuitton is a status symbol, and for part of its base it is also an identity ritual: the first LV bag as a rite of passage into a social tier. LVMH (MC.PA): Ecosystem Biology Analysis

Scope: only the dossier is used. Source tags are preserved. Reporting currency is EUR. Hurdle used throughout: the dossier's LOCAL COST OF EQUITY of 10.3-11.3% (USD 10-year 5.28% + Damodaran total ERP 5.01% = 10.29%, plus a 0-1.0% unsourced JUDGMENT governance/China premium). I do not use a Treasury yield as a stand-alone hurdle.

1. Species classification

LVMH is not one species. It is a portfolio of habitats under one roof, and the classification differs by habitat.

  • Louis Vuitton / Fashion & Leather (47% of revenue, ~74% of group PRO) [MEDIA per "LVMH Statistics 2026", ratios CALC]: a symbiont with a status-signaling host, not a parasite. The test for a parasite is "would the host leave if it could?" Here the host can leave at zero switching cost. Nobody is contractually or technically locked in: CONTRACTED BACKLOG is none, and the customer base is ~100% individual consumers (dossier ESTIMATE). That cuts both ways. Revenue is voluntary, so it reflects real desire. It is also not defended by any lock-in, so when desire weakens there is nothing underneath. The record shows the host leaving: Bain puts the global luxury customer pool at ~400M (2022) -> 340M (2025), with a further 20-30M loss likely, concentrated among aspirational buyers [MEDIA per China luxury outlook citing Bain].
  • Sephora / Selective Retailing (EUR 18.3B, organic +4%, ~9.8% margin) [MEDIA]: a conventional retail symbiont, a niche that is growing (~100 openings; Sephora profit +28% to EUR 1.8B [MEDIA]) and is not exposed to the aspirational-handbag cycle in the same way.
  • Wines & Spirits (~EUR 5.4B, +5% organic, PRO +11% H1 2026, margin 22.4% vs 20.3%) [MEDIA]: a small, recovering niche, about 6.7% of revenue [CALC]. Too small to rescue the group.

Honest label for the whole organism: a symbiont at the top of the food chain whose rent extraction ran ahead of its host's income. Prices rose 50-60% over a decade, 5-10% annually, with volumes allegedly rising [MEDIA per an unsourced marketing case study, not usable as evidence]. Against that, other pages say price rises frustrated loyal customers and the CFO said affordability "is not the answer" [MEDIA]. I have no volume data to arbitrate. What I can say is that the customer pool shrank ~15% over the same window in which price rose ~5-10% annually. In ecology, raising the toll while the traffic falls is a sign of a species harvesting a host population faster than it replenishes. It does not prove overharvesting; the data do not allow that. It is the pattern to watch.

2. Host health

  • Host population: shrinking in the aspirational tier (Bain, above). China was an estimated 35-40% of global luxury spend at the post-COVID peak, and J.P. Morgan expects flattish China luxury in 2026 [MEDIA]. China's share of LVMH's own revenue is not in the record. I will not guess it. The ecosystem-level China exposure is large; the company-level exposure is an open number that should be one of the first things resolved. The supporting data points are LVMH FY24 Asia ex-Japan -12.5%, Q4 China -10% Y/Y, Japan +18% [MEDIA via LinkedIn summary], and Bloomberg 2025-07-24 on shoppers reining in Louis Vuitton bag and Dior jacket purchases.
  • Host consolidation: the top-tier customer is not consolidating in any way the record shows. The segmentation does not exist (no aspirational-vs-top-tier revenue split). That is the single most valuable missing datum for my lens, because the survival question is not "is luxury growing" but "which tier carries the profit pool."
  • Can the host evolve to not need this species? Yes, in three ways the record names: (a) dupes: Louis Vuitton was the brand most discussed for dupes by both millennials and Gen Z, ahead of Hermes [MEDIA per Business Insider; STATUS: Speculative]; (b) resale: secondhand luxury ~11% CAGR 2023-2028 [MEDIA], which lets the host consume the brand without paying LVMH; (c) Gen Z: price-sensitive and brand-aware at once, drawn to micro-luxuries, resale and social commerce [MEDIA]. No LVMH-specific demographic data exists in the record.
  • Rent extraction rate: the clearest figure is on the supplier side, not the customer side. The Italian labour case cites a Dior handbag supplied for as little as EUR 53 and retailed at EUR 2,600 [MEDIA per Reuters]. That is a supplier cost of ~2.0% of retail price (53/2,600) [CALC]. It is one anecdote and it is not LVMH's blended cost of goods (the group's COGS is EUR 27.28B on revenue EUR 80.81B, 33.8% [CALC]). But it shows where the extraction works: the brand captures the value, and the biology below it (subcontractors) is the weakest link.

3. Invasive species threat

Ranked by how fast they colonise:

  1. Dupes and resale (behavioural, slow colonisers, already established). Speculative status in the record, so I do not size the revenue loss. The mechanism is credible because it attacks exactly the aspirational tier that Bain says is leaving.
  2. Regulatory species (fast, but mostly cost rather than demand). EU ESPR destruction ban from 19 July 2026 for medium and large companies (STATUS: Proposed in the record, though the same source says delegated regulations were adopted 9 February 2026); Digital Product Passport (Enacted; textile delegated acts expected 2027). LVMH carries 303 days of inventory (DIO) [CALC], so a ban on destroying unsold goods bites a business with a lot of slow-moving stock. No company response is recorded. Cost is not in the record; I do not size it.
  3. Antitrust. The Commission fined Gucci, Chloe and Loewe a combined EUR 157M for resale price coordination under TFEU Article 101 [MEDIA]. One source headline names LVMH among the fined; the body text names the Kering brands. This is a conflict in the record that I cannot resolve. If the headline is right, it is a precedent in LVMH's own habitat. I treat it as an unresolved flag, not a fact. Note that wholesale/travel-retail price discipline is exactly the sort of practice Article 101 reaches.
  4. Labour/supplier due diligence. A Dior-handbag subsidiary was placed under court administration on June 11 over alleged subcontracting to Chinese-owned firms that mistreated workers [MEDIA per Reuters; STATUS: Enacted]. Dior said it condemned the practices, will place no new orders with the two suppliers, and said the suppliers had concealed practices despite regular audits. Together with the Dutch AML case (EUR 500,000 settlement, ~0.005% of FY2025 net income [CALC]) and the Italian AGCM probe (opened 27 March 2026, outcome and fine not in the record), the pattern is a control-environment pattern, not a size problem. Individually immaterial; collectively they say the ecosystem's enforcement layer is attending to the species.
  5. US tariffs. A Bloomberg headline ties the tariff threat to weak US and China demand and a first impact on US beauty and cognac demand; no specific measures or dates are in the record. Affects Wines & Spirits and P&C, the two small segments.

How fast do invaders colonise this ecosystem type? The record gives no history (no Historian analogues supplied). I will not invent a rate. Gucci's fall (-22% across 2025, -14% Q1 2026 [MEDIA]) is the closest in-record specimen of what a brand-fashion contraction looks like once it starts, and Louis Vuitton is a much larger organism than Gucci, but it is the same mechanism.

4. Resilience vs brittleness

  • Concentration of the organism. ~74% of group PRO from one division, 47% of revenue [MEDIA/CALC]. That is the single point of failure. Operating margin only fell 1.1 points (23.1% -> 22.0%) in 2025 on a profit-from-recurring-operations decline of 9% to EUR 17.8B [SEARCH per Investing.com], which says the cost base flexes reasonably. The stress test's own adjusted FCF is EUR 10.82B at -10% revenue, EUR 7.44B at -20%, EUR 4.06B at -30%, and turns negative at about -45% [CALC]. So the species survives a deep revenue shock: that is real resilience. It does not survive it at today's price, which is a different question.
  • Balance sheet. Net debt EUR 8.28B excluding leases; EUR 24.91B including EUR 16.63B of leases [CALC]. Borrowings EUR 20.48B against cash plus short-term investments EUR 12.20B. Not a fragile habitat. The lease treatment is a valuation issue, not a survival issue (see section 6).
  • Is the ecosystem diversifying or concentrating? Mixed. LVMH itself diversifies (75 brands [SEARCH per Statista], Sephora, spirits), but the luxury ecosystem is concentrating in the sense that the profit pool sits with fewer, richer, more China-sensitive customers if aspirational buyers keep leaving. A species that depends on a narrowing host base becomes more brittle even while its revenue looks stable.
  • Dominance structure. The family holds 50.01% of capital and 65.94% of votes (19 Feb 2026, up from 49.77% at end-2025) [MEDIA per Luxury Tribune citing AMF filings]. In biological terms: there is no apex predator. Hostile takeover and activist pressure are structurally absent, so the organism can stay patient through a downturn. The risk is the opposite: succession. Reuters reports investors demanding clarity; Agache Commandite SAS with five children at 20% each would head Agache SCA "as soon as Arnault leaves" [MEDIA]. The dossier flags a governance discount. A single dominant individual who has not named a successor is a keystone dependency inside the organism.
  • Balance of evidence on the downturn: Q2 2026 organic +3% after +1% in Q1 is an accelerating signal, but H1 2026 reported revenue was EUR 38.6B, -3% reported, +2% organic; F&L H1 EUR 18.1B, -1% organic, -5% reported (currency -4%) [MEDIA]. Currency took ~EUR 700M (~8%) of H1 PRO [MEDIA, ratio CALC]. The host is not dead; the organism is still bleeding at the reported line.

5. Evolutionary trajectory

Is it co-evolving or extracting from a static position? The record supports both stories and I will not pick a winner.

  • Adaptation signals: Sephora expansion (~100 openings), Wines & Spirits margin recovery, operating investments of EUR 2.1B in H1 2026, Marc Jacobs IP disposal for ~USD 850M (completed 1 Sep 2026 per one source; the buyer is G-III in one source and WHP Global in another, unresolved) [MEDIA]. Pruning a non-core brand while the top brand compounds is what an adapting species does.
  • Extraction signals: the core F&L division kept raising prices into a falling customer pool; DPO fell from 128 to 110 days over three years (suppliers paid faster) [CALC], which is a small sign the organism is not squeezing its suppliers harder, but it does not outweigh the labour findings.
  • Return on capital trajectory: ROIC 19.5% (2022) -> 19.4% -> 15.4% -> 14.8% (2025) [CALC]. Net margin 17.8% -> 13.5%. Goodwill EUR 24.8B -> 18.3B [CALC], cause unattributed (impairment, currency or disposal; the record does not say). A falling ROIC on a roughly stable asset base says the organism is earning less per unit of habitat. That is the quantitative footprint of the shrinking host pool.
  • 10-year arc: The brand species is very likely to exist in ten years; Louis Vuitton is a century-old organism with ~67% gross margin [MEDIA]. The harder question is whether the ecosystem in ten years carries as much rent. I put a 10-year survival probability of ~92% as a dominant luxury franchise (franchise survival, not investor-return survival). The residual 8% is for a combination of a succession failure, a permanent loss of the aspirational tier, and a regulatory/ESG shock to the supply chain. That number is a judgment, not a computation.

MC.PA (LVMH) - Historian of Disruption

0. Scope and source discipline

The dossier states "Historian: no analogues supplied in the record." Every analogue below (dates, share figures, outcomes) therefore comes from my own background knowledge, not from the dossier. Treat the figures as approximate and unverified. Every number about LVMH is from the dossier and keeps its tag: [CALC] for pipeline-computed figures from yfinance, [SEARCH per X] for company figures seen through secondary pages, [MEDIA per X] for web summaries.

The most important framing point is that LVMH is not facing a technological disruptor of the Kodak or Blockbuster type. The threat register shows no technology that replaces a Louis Vuitton bag. Dupes are rated "Speculative" and resale is a side channel (secondhand luxury ~11% CAGR 2023-2028 [MEDIA]). The right analogues are therefore companies that lost their customer base through overextension, aspirational-customer fatigue and brand dilution. They are not companies a new technology killed. The historian's question becomes: when a prestige brand loses its marginal buyer, how does it end, and how long does it take?

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (the casualties)

Pure death is the wrong frame for a brand with a EUR 187B market cap [CALC] and 75 brands [SEARCH per Statista]. The honest casualty analogues are brands that kept existing but lost their moat and their earning power.

  1. Pierre Cardin (1960s to 1990s). Cardin licensed his name across roughly 800 products in about 140 countries. Revenue rose while pricing power fell, because the name stopped signalling anything. The brand survives as a licensing shell worth a small fraction of its peak significance. Decline ran over two decades, and the licensing revenue masked the brand erosion for most of that time. The management signal was optimising the royalty stream while the scarcity that gave the royalty its value ran down.
  2. Gucci before 1994. Family infighting, product proliferation and heavy licensing left the house near insolvency by the early 1990s. The collapse took about a decade from the 1980s expansion. Brand equity looked intact on the outside until the numbers broke.
  3. Abercrombie & Fitch (2013-2016). An aspirational brand tied to a specific buyer cohort. Comparable sales fell for years once the cohort aged out and discounting replaced scarcity. It stabilised at a much lower earnings level, and it is not a growth franchise. This is the closest warning for the "aspirational buyer" tier.

What these have in common is that none was killed by a competitor. Each lost control of scarcity (licensing, discounting, over-distribution) at the moment growth was pressing management to keep volume up.

Read-across to LVMH. The dossier supports some casualty signals and contradicts others. - The Bain customer-pool decline (400M to 340M, 20-30M more likely, concentrated in aspirational buyers [MEDIA per China luxury outlook citing Bain]) is the Abercrombie-type signal. - Price rises of 50-60% over a decade, 5-10% a year [MEDIA per an unsourced marketing case study], are the classic stretch that precedes aspirational flight. The dossier has no volume data to arbitrate, and a CFO remark that affordability "is not the answer" [MEDIA per Louis Vuitton pricing article] points the other way. This is the single most important unknown. - Against the casualty reading: LVMH does not license its core name at scale in the dossier, and nothing in the record shows discounting of Louis Vuitton.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (the adapters)

  1. Swiss watch industry after the quartz shock (1970s-1980s). Swiss share of world watch unit output fell from roughly 40%+ in the mid-1970s to roughly 15% by the early 1980s, with employment collapsing by about two thirds. Survival came from repositioning the top of the range as status objects and from Swatch at the bottom. It took about 10-12 years of pain, and the survivors were the ones who defended the top tier and let the middle go. That is directly relevant: the aspirational middle is the part at risk, and it is the part least defensible.
  2. Gucci 1994-1999 (De Sole and Ford). They cut licences, centralised design and retail control and took the brand upmarket. A turnaround from near-failure to leading growth took about 3-4 years. Management acted before the market forced it to.
  3. Burberry 2006-2014 (Ahrendts). It bought back licences and removed the over-ubiquitous check, then rebuilt around owned retail. Revenue grew several-fold over the following years. The reset cost short-term margin and sales, and management accepted that.
  4. Hermès (late 1990s onward). It limited production and used scarcity as policy. The dossier describes Hermès as sustaining above-market growth through controlled production and scarcity, qualitatively and without figures [MEDIA per "Top Luxury Goods Companies"]. Hermès is the control case showing the category itself is not dying: the weakness is specific to the exposed tier, and LVMH owns more of that tier than Hermès does.
  5. Coach 2013-2016. After heavy outlet discounting it cut wholesale and discounting, accepted lower revenue and rebuilt the brand. It recovered at a lower scale, and the recovery took roughly three years.

4. DISRUPTION TIMELINE

Where are we in the arc? These analogues show a consistent pattern. The volume and the aspirational cohort leave first, the headline numbers (operating margin, FCF) hold up for a time because cost cuts and working-capital release cover the gap, and the break comes when the brand itself loses pricing power.

  • LVMH sits at mid-transition (2-5 years) on the aspirational-customer cycle, if the cycle is structural. Revenue fell from EUR 86.2B to EUR 80.8B (-6.3%) while net income fell -28% (EUR 15.17B to EUR 10.88B) [CALC]. That 4.4x operating leverage on the way down is the same shape Swiss watchmakers and Coach showed early in their declines.
  • Operating margin fell only -1.1pt in 2025 (22.0%) [SEARCH per Investing.com]. FCF rose 34% from 2023 to 2025 (EUR 10.60B to EUR 14.21B [CALC]) while Owner Earnings drifted down (EUR 11.61B to EUR 10.87B). The FCF rise is a warning, not comfort: it comes with a EUR 2.9B unexplained gap to the company's own EUR 11.3B figure [SEARCH per LVMH press release], and with inventory and receivables being run down in line with sales (inventory -4.3%, receivables -8.5% [CALC]), which cannot be repeated. DPO fell 128 to 110 days, so suppliers are being paid faster.
  • DIO is 303 days [CALC], so the brand is carrying about 10 months of stock. Heavy stock is where discounting pressure starts. This is the first thing to watch.
  • Early signs of stabilisation: Q2 2026 organic +3% after Q1 +1%; H1 2026 +2% organic, reported -3%; Wines & Spirits and Selective Retailing growing [MEDIA]. But Fashion & Leather, ~47% of revenue and ~74% of group PRO, was -5% organic in 2025 and -1% organic in H1 2026 [MEDIA]. Gucci (a peer) lost 22% across 2025 and a further 14% in Q1 [threat register]. Gucci is the closest data point for what "pricing power lost" looks like inside the category. LVMH's -5% in Fashion & Leather is mild by comparison.
  • The reference arcs: Gucci 1994-1998 (3-4 years from trough), Coach 2013-2016 (~3 years), Burberry 2006-2010 (~4 years), Swiss watches (10+ years). LVMH is roughly 2 years into its decline (revenue peak 2023), so a normal recovery arc puts resolution 1-3 years out and the Swiss arc puts it much later.

Point-of-no-return signals. Any one of these should be treated as casualty confirmation. 1. Louis Vuitton or Dior discounting, outlet expansion or visible volume-for-price trades, which would show up first as inventory growing faster than sales (DIO well above 303 days). 2. Fashion & Leather organic growth negative for a third year, with the operating margin below ~20% (it was 22.0% in 2025 [SEARCH per Investing.com]). 3. A forced price cut or "accessible" product line launched by Louis Vuitton. 4. Gross margin falling below ~65% (it is 67.1% in H1 2026 [MEDIA], +30bp), which would show the price premium eroding.

The analogue arcs say the bottom comes 2-4 years after the trough in organic growth. What matters for the verdict is whether the price pays for the wait.

Yields against the hurdle. - FCF yield 7.6% on the pipeline's EUR 14.21B [CALC] and 6.1% on the company's EUR 11.3B [SEARCH per LVMH press release]. I do not accept the 6.8%. - P/E ~17.2x on FY2025 EPS (~EUR 21.9); 14.2x on the 4-year average net income (EUR 13.17B); 12.3x on 2023 peak (EUR 15.17B) [CALC]. What growth the price demands. Using owner earnings of EUR 10.87B [CALC], or ~EUR 21.9 per share on 496M shares, and a one-stage perpetuity, price = EPS x (1+g) / (r - g). - At 10.8% and EUR 378.55, implied g is ~4.7%. At 10.3% it is ~4.3%. - Using the pipeline FCF (EUR 28.65 per share) the implied g is ~3.0% at 10.8%. Using the company's EUR 11.3B FCF (about EUR 22.8 per share on 496M shares) it is again ~4.6%. - The dossier's own anchors use g = 2.0% and back-solve to discount rates of ~9.2-9.6% [CALC, Chief-of-Staff back-solve], below the stated hurdle. The dossier does not size this, and I do not either.

So the price assumes the survivor outcome, in full, now. A price of EUR 378.55 needs ~3-4.7% perpetual growth from a business that has shrunk for two years. That is the Gucci-1996 outcome, priced before the evidence arrives, with no margin for the casualty outcome. The analogues say the best entries were made after the first confirmed year of growth, not before (Gucci after the 1995 IPO, Burberry after the licence buy-ins, Coach after the 2016 reset), and the cost of waiting was 10-20% of the eventual gain, not the whole thing.

Stress test. Adjusted FCF falls to EUR 10.82B at -10% revenue, EUR 7.44B at -20% and EUR 4.06B at -30%, turning negative at about -45% [CALC, stress model]. At -20% revenue, owner yield at today's price would be roughly 4%. The Swiss watch arc (a two-thirds fall in employment) shows -20% is not a tail event for a category in structural shift. For LVMH it is a bear scenario, not a base case.

  • Governance and succession. Gucci's and Cardin's decline both ran through founder-era decision-making. Arnault holds 50.01% of capital and 65.94% of votes [MEDIA per Luxury Tribune citing AMF], up from 49.77% at end-2025.   • Dior supplier scandal. The EUR 53 sourcing price against the EUR 2,600 retail price is the kind of data point that undermines a scarcity story with the customer who pays for it. It is a small legal exposure but a larger brand-narrative one. It is not by itself a casualty signal.
  • Regulation. The EU destruction ban (19 July 2026, status "Proposed" in the register though the text says the date has passed) and the Digital Product Passport remove a tool that luxury houses used to protect scarcity: burning unsold stock. With DIO at 303 days, this makes the inventory problem a markdown problem sooner. The dossier does not size it.
  • Resale-price enforcement. The EU fine of EUR 157M on Gucci, Chloe and Loewe for resale price control (Article 101 TFEU) shows the regulator is watching scarcity-by-control. LVMH is not named in the fines in the text, though the headline lists it. The risk is that controlling resale pricing, which is part of how these brands protect scarcity, becomes harder.

7. THE VERDICT

Trajectory: Undetermined, leaning Survivor. Survivor 55%, Casualty 20%, 25% the Swiss-watch outcome (survives at a lower, narrower tier with the middle gone). Confidence in the call itself: 45%.

Reasoning. The core brand franchise (Louis Vuitton, Dior, Sephora, Wines & Spirits) is not showing the casualty signature, which would be discounting, licensing and a collapse in gross margin. Gross margin is up 30bp to 67.1% in H1 2026 [MEDIA]. Group structure (owned retail, pruning, a ~EUR 12B cash buffer [CALC]) matches survivors. But the analogues say disruption of this type is slow and shows first in volume and in the customer pool, which is where the evidence is worst (Bain: 400M to 340M). The price rise versus volume question cannot be answered from the dossier, and that unknown is exactly the one that separates Burberry from Pierre Cardin.

Why WAIT and not BUY or HOLD. There is no position to hold. BUY fails on price because the market already pays for a full survivor outcome (implied g ~3-4.7% against a -3.2% revenue CAGR) at a 10.3-11.3% hurdle. SELL fails because the structural behaviour is survivor-like and the analogues show a 3-4 year recovery arc for the survivors, so the stock at a 2-year low of EUR 378.55 (-40.5% over two years [CALC]) is not obviously overpriced for a 3-5 year holder. WAIT is the call.

Position size: ZERO at EUR 378.55. This is a verdict about price and evidence, not about the brand. If the evidence below arrives, a small starter is justified before the full-recovery price shows up.
