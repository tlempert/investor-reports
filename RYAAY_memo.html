The bull case and the counterargument

The council’s strongest bull case is that low costs, direct distribution and financial strength could let Ryanair gain share while weaker carriers retreat. Filed unit cost rose only 1% despite traffic growth of 4%.3 Competitor financials are absent, however; management’s claimed financing advantage is not independent proof of rivals’ weakness.

The economic counterargument is straightforward: fuel protection expires faster than customers’ willingness to pay changes. Reported hedge coverage falls from 80% at $67/barrel to 15% at $85/barrel.5 The council’s simplified constant-spot example raises blended fuel from $83.6 to about $140/barrel if unhedged fuel remains $150/barrel; this is arithmetic, not a forecast.2

My interpretation: falling fares during the fuel spike weaken the pass-through case. AI intermediaries, agent-access remedies and ancillary-fee restrictions could also erode distribution economics; aircraft delivery, airport and air-traffic constraints could limit expansion.

How I would make the decision

My interpretation: I would purchase a continuing cost advantage when the price compensates for uncertain fuel recovery and distribution economics.

Conditions that would support buying

  • Fares recover alongside fuel costs.
  • Hedge disclosures reduce exposure without expensive protection.
  • Ex-fuel unit costs and direct bookings remain resilient.

Conditions that would support waiting

  • Hedge coverage remains thin and fares weak.
  • Certification or regulatory remedies remain unresolved.
  • Updated evidence improves central value without supporting the purchase ceiling.

Conditions that would invalidate the thesis

  • Persistent relative cost deterioration.
  • Lasting inability to recover industry-wide fuel costs.
  • Distribution tolls structurally impair ancillary profitability.
