  • Mortgages specifically: +~50%, still only ~2% of group revenue [SEC] — small, but the CFO's confidence in the 20–30% SGA growth range for the full year rests partly on this line's sequential mortgage-market recovery (+£1.2m H2-2025 to H1-2026) [SEARCH] and on Leader Advantage, the first chargeable commercial-property product, launching in H2-2026.

Is the company investing through the cycle in a way that looks bad short-term? Yes — capex now exceeds depreciation for the first time in the series (£10.2m vs £8.3m) [SEC], and RBC identified an incremental £6m of capitalised AI spend in 2026 on top of the £12m P&L hit [MEDIA]. That's the textbook Lynch pattern: a dominant compounder spending through a scare while the market extrapolates the spending as permanent margin loss rather than a 3-year-payback investment (management's own stated timeline, affecting profits until 2028) [MEDIA].

Optionality priced at roughly zero by the market: the SGA sleeve, the AI product suite (43 concurrent initiatives, >2,500 releases in four months), and — separately from the operating business — takeover optionality given the rejected £6.2bn REA bid, the Murdoch-adjacent IFP stake, and a share price 41% below what a strategic buyer already offered once.

On the CAT claim's second-order threat to ARPA: this is the one bear point I can't fully counter with data in the file — the dossier is explicit that whether 2027 ARPA growth survives the claim's publicity is unresolved. I'm not dismissing it; I'm noting that even a chilled ARPA (say 3–4%/yr instead of 6%) still leaves this a growing, high-margin compounder, just a slower one — it doesn't turn the thesis negative on its own.

4. THE FCF REALITY CHECK

FCF yield at the current £3,634.7m market cap: £226.1m / £3,634.7m = 6.22% [CALC], using the [SEC] FY2025 free cash flow figure (not the stress-test table's broken "simple FCF" column, which the dossier flags as using an inconsistent definition).

Hurdle discipline: the dossier's local cost of equity for a UK single-country, single-sector business is 9.7–11.2%, built off the 5.15% UK 10-year gilt [SEARCH per Trading Economics] plus a 4.5–5.5% UK equity risk premium. Against that hurdle, a 6.22% FCF yield does not clear the bar on its own — this is not a "yield alone is enough" situation, and I won't pretend otherwise. The spread to the gilt (5.15%) is a thin ~107bp — comfortable against a risk-free rate, but the risk-free rate is not the right hurdle for equity risk.

What closes the gap is growth + buybacks, not the coupon. Dividend yield alone is 2.22% [CALC] and payout is only 37.8% — the coupon by itself, as the dossier insists, does not provide downside protection against a 9.7–11.2% hurdle. But total capital returned in FY2025 was £141.1m buyback + £78.6m dividend = £219.7m, 97% of FCF [CALC], and share count has fallen 8.0% over three years and a further 3.4% in 2026 to date. Combine the 6.22% FCF yield with even a conservative 4–5% forward EPS-per-share growth (well below the historical 6%+ ARPA trend, discounted for the New Homes cycle) and you're inside single digits of the 9.7% low end of the hurdle — with the historical re-rating precedent (27% CAGR since 2006) suggesting the market has, in normal times, been willing to pay up for exactly this combination.

"If I bought the whole company" — at £3,634.7m for a business throwing off £226.1m of FCF with 67%+ margins, no debt problem (net cash £29.9m [CALC]), and monthly subscription revenue billed in advance from ~19,047 branch customers with no concentration — yes, I'd be happy holding those cash flows, provided the CAT tail doesn't land at the fat end of the distribution. The honest caveat: at today's price this is a "growth bridges the gap" thesis, not a "the yield alone screams buy" thesis.

5. THE MANAGEMENT DECODER

Was the Nov-2025 AI spend irrational capital allocation? I don't think so, and the market's -25% reaction looks more like a duration mismatch (the market wants next year's EPS, management is buying 2028's moat) than a competence failure. Management stated the spend has a 3-year payback and affects profits until 2028 [MEDIA] — that's a specific, falsifiable commitment, not vague "invest in AI" language. The fact that margin guidance still sits at "no lower than 67%" even after absorbing the spend tells you the platform's underlying economics can fund the investment without touching the buyback (£90m announced, £44m completed [SEARCH] — capital returns continued through the scare).

Buybacks at high prices — capital destruction or discipline? Cash spent on repurchases has been remarkably steady — £130.0m (2022), £130.0m (2023), £107.4m (2024), £141.1m (2025) [SEC] — through a period when the share price ranged from roughly 391p to 747p. That's not opportunistic buying-the-dip; it's a fairly mechanical return-of-capital policy. Reasonable people can disagree on whether that's "intelligent behavior the market punishes short-term" or simply un-opportunistic; I'd note share count fell in every year of the series while SBC roughly doubled (£4.2m→£8.5m) — the repurchases more than offset dilution, which is the minimum bar, not necessarily evidence of skillful timing.

CEO succession — red flag or healthy handover? Johan Svanström is an outsider hire (joined Feb-2026, CEO from March) [SEARCH] — a professional manager, not a founder exit. Calling AI-replaces-portals "unthinkable" [MEDIA per The Times] is the kind of overconfident soundbite that should be discounted as rhetoric, not evidence — but it's worth noting the traffic data (LLM referrals <0.5%, flat) is at least directionally consistent with his confidence, even if the framing is glib.

The board's rejection of REA's £6.2bn (2024) — hindsight bias or vindicated? Both readings are live and the file doesn't settle it: today's £3.63bn cap is 41% below the rejected bid, which on its face looks expensive for shareholders who'd have taken the exit. But a 2024 bid struck in a lower-rate, lower-fear environment, not all cash, two years before an AI scare and a CAT claim existed, is not a clean valuation of the business today. I'd lean toward "the board's judgment looks worse in hindsight than it did at the time" without calling it wrong — REA's own strategic logic (buying the dominant UK portal) hasn't gone away.

The Buffett View

Rightmove remains an extraordinary economic franchise, but it is not an inevitable fortress. Its consumer mindshare, agent dependence, pricing power and capital-light economics remain formidable. Yet the current price offers insufficient compensation for a 9.7–11.2% UK cost of equity and the separate CAT litigation risk.

This is not a cigar butt. Nor is it presently a wonderful company at a sufficiently attractive price.

1. The Anti-Fragile Moat

The Chaos Test

Rightmove is resilient, not anti-fragile.

Its monthly subscriptions are an operating necessity for agents, and one agent attributed 80% of his leads to Rightmove [MEDIA]. Agency retention reached its highest level in more than ten years [SEARCH, H1 2026 transcript]. That should make revenue more durable than housing transactions.

But a housing downturn does not make Rightmove stronger:

  • New-build development volumes are expected to decline 6–10% in 2026 [SEARCH, H1 earnings call].
  • New Homes revenue grew only 2% to £38m in H1 2026 [SEARCH, H1 results].
  • Full-year revenue guidance was cut from 8–10% to 6–8% [SEARCH].

Rightmove may gain relative strength if weaker portals fail, but recessions injure its agent and developer customers. This fails the strict anti-fragility test.

The £1bn Competitor Test

A well-run rival probably could not kill Rightmove quickly, even with a £1bn war chest. It could, however, reduce Rightmove’s pricing power.

The defensive evidence is powerful:

  • Rightmove accounts for approximately 75–90% of measured portal attention, depending on the provider [SEARCH, company releases and H1 transcript].
  • Comscore measured nine out of every ten portal minutes in June 2026 [SEARCH, H1 transcript].
  • More than 85% of traffic is organic or direct [SEARCH, Online Marketplaces].
  • Retention is at a ten-year high, and more than half of new joiners were existing partners opening additional branches [SEARCH, H1 transcript].

A rival must simultaneously acquire listings, consumer attention and agent trust. Subsidizing only one side is insufficient.

Yet CoStar is already attempting the test. It planned £46.5m of first-year OnTheMarket marketing—more than three times Rightmove’s annual media spending—and a multi-year programme worth hundreds of millions of pounds [MEDIA, CoStar]. OnTheMarket visits reportedly rose 90% while Rightmove visits declined 1%, although the measurement period and absolute traffic figures are missing [MEDIA].

Conclusion: £1bn probably cannot destroy the network, but it could force higher marketing and product expenditure while limiting ARPA increases. That makes this a strong moat, not an impregnable one.

Pricing Power

Pricing power is demonstrated:

  • ARPA rose from £1,431 per month in 2023 to £1,524 in 2024 and £1,621 in 2025 [MEDIA/SEC].
  • Those increases were 6.5% and 6.4% [CALC].
  • Membership grew approximately 1% while prices increased [MEDIA].
  • Retention subsequently reached a ten-year high [SEARCH].

Rightmove has therefore raised prices without sacrificing aggregate customer volume.

The qualification is important: customers may tolerate price increases because leaving is commercially dangerous, not because they are delighted. Agents have called fees unsustainable, and more than 250 agencies have expressed support for the CAT claim [MEDIA]. Historical pricing power is unquestionable; future freedom to exercise it is not.

2. Strategic Evolution

The Borg Check: Absorbing AI—for Now

Rightmove is treating AI as a feature rather than ignoring it:

  • A ChatGPT app launched in March 2026 [SEARCH, Online Marketplaces].
  • It has 43 AI initiatives underway, versus 31 at the end of 2025 [SEARCH].
  • Conversational-search trial users were almost three times more likely to generate a lead [SEARCH, H2 2025 call].
  • Its Online Agent Valuation tool covers more than 40% of available areas [SEARCH].

LLM referral traffic remains below 0.5% and has been flat since the end of 2025 [SEARCH, Online Marketplaces]. There is no current evidence of material demand-side displacement.

But the dangerous mechanism is supply-side bypass: AI agents could collect listings from estate agents’ own sites and make a portal subscription less necessary. Current LLM referral data does not disprove that scenario.

The £18m AI programme, including an estimated £12m incremental P&L charge and £6m of capitalized expenditure [MEDIA, Reuters/RBC], is sensible defensive spending. Management expects a three-year payback and profit effects through 2028 [MEDIA]. Rightmove is absorbing the threat today, but the evolutionary contest is not settled.

Commoditizing the Complement

There is little evidence that Rightmove is deliberately making adjacent technologies cheaper or open to reinforce its core monopoly.

Mortgages, commercial-property products and valuation tools expand the ecosystem, but they appear designed for monetization and workflow control. The dossier contains no evidence of open-sourcing listing infrastructure, subsidizing agent software, or broadly lowering the cost of complementary technology.

This is ecosystem extension—not classic “commoditize the complement” strategy.

3. Maintenance Versus Expansion

Statutory operating margin fell from 72.6% in 2022 to 67.7% in 2025 [SEC]. That looks alarming until the investment and one-off effects are separated:

  • FY2024 included £9.2m of transaction and takeover-defense costs [MEDIA]. Adding those back produces an approximately 68.1% margin [CALC].
  • FY2025 underlying operating margin was 70.0%, versus 67.7% statutory [SEC].
  • FY2026 includes approximately £12m of incremental AI expense and £6m of additional capitalized investment [MEDIA, RBC].
  • Guidance still calls for underlying operating profit growth of 3–5% and a margin no lower than 67% [SEARCH].

The market forward P/E is 14.4x [market]. At 495.6p, that implies forward EPS of approximately 34.4p [CALC]. Applying FY2025’s 75.4% net-income-to-operating-profit conversion [CALC] to the £12m incremental AI expense produces an estimated after-tax drag of £9.0m, or about 1.2p per current share [CALC].

That gives:

  • Headline forward P/E: 14.4x [market].
  • Core forward P/E excluding the incremental AI P&L investment: approximately 13.9x [CALC].

This is only a proxy because no segment operating-income disclosure exists. A precise SGA-adjusted core P/E cannot be calculated from the dossier.

The evidence nevertheless points to a healthy core funding expansion, not a core already deteriorating: ARPA grew 6.4%, membership held, retention improved and underlying margin remained 70% [SEC/SEARCH]. The warning light is that expenses increased 50.4% between 2022 and 2025 against revenue growth of 27.8% [CALC]. The investment case requires that this spending eventually restores operating leverage.

4. The Capital Allocation Machine

The Cannibal

Rightmove is a genuine cannibal:

  • Shares declined from 825.2m in 2022 to 759.4m in 2025, an 8.0% reduction [SEC/CALC].
  • The count subsequently fell another 3.4% to 733.4m [SEC/CALC].
  • Repurchases exceeded SBC dilution in every year.
  • FY2025 buybacks of £141.1m plus dividends of £78.6m returned £219.7m, equal to 97% of £226.1m FCF [SEC/CALC].

This has lifted FCF per share by 25.7% since 2022, versus 15.8% growth in aggregate FCF [CALC].

However, the announced return of more than £400m over twelve months is not reconciled with £226.1m of annual FCF and £29.9m of net cash [SEC/CALC]. I would not capitalize an apparent 11% shareholder yield until management explains whether the programme overlaps existing authorization or requires debt.

ROIC

The reported 437.6% ROIC is mechanically distorted by an £82.3m equity base hollowed out by decades of repurchases [SEC/CALC]. It cannot be used as evidence that Rightmove clears a 15% ROIC threshold.

The economic conclusion is nevertheless clear: £425.1m of revenue generated £226.1m of FCF with only £10.2m of capex [SEC]. Rightmove requires exceptionally little incremental capital. It passes the franchise test economically, even though the reported ROIC percentage is unusable.

5. The Yellow Pages Test

Rightmove has two kinds of moat.

The durable components are:

  • Two-sided network liquidity.
  • Consumer search habit.
  • High direct and organic traffic.
  • Agent workflow dependence.
  • A nationally recognized destination with approximately 75–90% of attention [SEARCH].

The vulnerable components are:

  • Control over property discovery.
  • Information aggregation.
  • Agent belief that portal access is unavoidable.
  • The ability to impose recurring above-inflation ARPA increases.

Yellow Pages and classified newspapers lost their monopolies when technology made distribution and aggregation cheap. AI, open property data, scraping and agent-owned websites could eventually do the same to property discovery.

Rightmove is better defended than Yellow Pages because its listings are live, frequently changing and supported by network effects. But any part of the moat resting on information scarcity is temporary.

The present evidence does not yet show decay: ARPA, membership and retention are all rising. It shows a credible pathway to decay. That distinction supports a MODERATE rather than SEVERE moat flag.

If ARPA growth disappears and Rightmove becomes a no-growth harvesting asset, its 29.7p of owner earnings per share [CALC] would justify only about 235–265p at an 11.2–12.5% required owner yield [CALC]. That is the cigar-butt price range—not 495.6p.

6. Valuation and CAT Claim

Using actual-capex owner earnings of £217.6m [CALC], the current owner-earnings yield is:

[ £217.6m \div £3,634.7m = 5.99\% ]

That 5.99% yield is inadequate on a stand-alone basis against Rightmove’s 9.7–11.2% local cost of equity. It becomes acceptable only if durable per-share growth supplies the missing 3.7–5.2 percentage points.

A simple operating valuation using:

  • £217.6m starting owner earnings [CALC];
  • 5% annual growth for five years;
  • 3% terminal growth;
  • 10.5% required return, the midpoint of the dossier’s local cost of equity;
  • £29.9m net cash [CALC];

produces approximately 447p per share before litigation [CALC]. Sensitivity across the dossier’s 9.7–11.2% cost-of-equity range is roughly 409–501p using the same 5% growth assumption [CALC]. The present 495.6p price already assumes something close to the favorable end of that range.

The CAT claim must be priced separately. My explicit scenario is:

  • Headline claim: £1.5bn [MEDIA, Reuters].
  • Probability of a materially adverse outcome: 20%—analytical assumption.
  • Adverse cash quantum: £750m, half the pleaded amount—analytical assumption.
  • Expected direct cost: £150m, or approximately 20p per share [CALC].

That reduces the midpoint operating value from approximately 447p to approximately 427p [CALC]. A 15% margin of safety would put the purchase price around 360p. The 360–380p trigger range allows some recognition of the franchise while still clearing a 10.5% required return.

This calculation addresses only direct damages. An adverse finding that restricts future ARPA increases could be worth more than the damages themselves.

Definitive Verdict

Rightmove is a high-quality, capital-light franchise with demonstrated pricing power, dominant mindshare and outstanding cash economics. It is neither a mediocre company nor currently a cigar butt.

But it is no longer an “inevitable” fortress. CoStar can raise the cost of defending the ecosystem, AI presents a credible bypass mechanism, and the CAT action directly targets the pricing power that makes the business exceptional.

The closest classification is: a wonderful company at a full price with a credible—but not yet demonstrated—moat-decay risk.

At 495.6p, the 5.99% owner yield does not provide enough protection against a 9.7–11.2% local cost of equity and the separately valued litigation overlay. The moat remains strong, but the investment lacks a margin of safety. PASS; reconsider at 360–380p.

Named agents describe the relationship as extractive: Chris pays >£5,000/month, equating it to two staff salaries, and passes the cost to clients [MEDIA]. That is not one disgruntled operator — it is the host population attempting to organize a collective response, which is precisely what a captive host does when it can't exit but can litigate.

Against that: agency retention is at a 10-year high, and more than half of new joiners in H1 2026 were existing partners opening new branches [SEARCH] — that is not the behavior of a population fleeing a parasite, it's expansion within the relationship. A true parasite relationship without lock-in shows churn; this shows growth. The honest read is: a keystone species on the demand side that has drifted into rent-extraction on the supply side, and whether that drift crossed into "abuse of dominance" is now literally being adjudicated. The biologist's job is to name this tension, not resolve it — the CAT tribunal will resolve it.

2. HOST HEALTH

The estate-agency host population is not declining: agency businesses grew +2% over the past year and +20% over five years [MEDIA per GetAgent.co.uk], with branch closures largely offset by branch openings. That undercuts a "dying host" bear case — the population Rightmove feeds on is structurally growing, not shrinking.

Consolidation: the file shows no evidence of the host consolidating into fewer, larger, more bargaining-powerful operators. The dominant signal is the opposite — fragmentation and branch-level expansion (existing partners opening new branches). A fragmented host population historically has less collective leverage, which is consistent with Rightmove's sustained pricing power (ARPA +6.5% then +6.4% in the two most recent years while membership grew only ~1%). The CAT claim is the first evidence in this file of the fragmented host acquiring collective leverage anyway — 250+ agencies is a coordination event that the historical fragmentation should have made hard to organize. Rent extraction rate — cannot be computed, and I will not invent a denominator. The dossier gives what agents pay (£1,621/mo average, rising 6.4%/yr) but not what they earn (UK average commission per completion, completions per branch are both absent) [dossier: Customer Unit Economics]. Could the host evolve to no longer need this species? Two live mechanisms, pulling opposite directions. HISTORIAN OF DISRUPTION — RMV.L

My job is not Rightmove's history — Sherlock owns that. My job is to ask: which dead companies looked exactly like this, which survivors faced the identical fork, and which pattern is Rightmove's management currently walking down. I use only the dossier.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

Yellow Pages / UK directory publishers (Yell Group). Yell held a nineties/2000s local-directory near-monopoly on small-business advertising — structurally the closest historical fit to Rightmove's position: a captive SMB customer base with no real alternative distribution channel, paying a recurring subscription-like fee for lead generation. Yell's response to the arrival of Google as a superior lead-generation channel was to keep raising directory prices on the same captive base rather than compress margin to compete on the new channel — the same behavioural fork Rightmove faces now with agents describing fee rises of "around 20%" then "12%" in successive years [MEDIA, threat register item 6] and a named agent (Chris, Northamptonshire) paying "more than £5,000/month" and calling the cost equal to two staff salaries [MEDIA]. Yell's directory revenue did not fall gradually; it went from FTSE100-scale market value to a debt-driven collapse once the shift crossed a threshold, because the captive customers had nowhere to complain except by leaving en masse — precisely the mechanism the £1.5bn CAT claim alleges is now happening to Rightmove, litigated rather than just churned.

UK national-newspaper classified advertising (property, jobs, cars), circa 2000–2010. This is the direct precedent for Rightmove's own business model, and the irony is exact: Rightmove itself (founded 2000 by four newspaper groups, per the dossier's LSE-listing note) was one of the disruptors that killed newspaper property classifieds. The newspapers' own trajectory as incumbents facing a lower-cost, better-matched digital channel is the template for what a successful attacker looks like from the other side of the table — and it is the same shape of attack now aimed at Rightmove: not a frontal price war, but a channel that intermediates the buyer-seller match more cheaply and pulls the advertiser away. The newspapers that tried to defend print classified rates rather than build a digital competitor lost that revenue line entirely within a decade.

Blockbuster. Included for the behavioural contrast, not the business-model fit. Blockbuster's board treated Netflix as immaterial ("not a real competitor" in substance, if not in the exact words) and optimized late-fee and in-store margins rather than fund a streaming build-out, even after acquiring the technical capability to do so. The signal that matters for Rightmove: Blockbuster's fatal error was not lacking capital — it had it — but declining to spend it on cannibalizing its own margin ahead of the threat becoming visible in the numbers.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

AutoTrader UK. The closest same-market, same-country, same-model comparator available in the historical record: a dominant UK vertical-classifieds portal for a different category (cars instead of property), facing the identical structural threat — dealers and private sellers bypassing the portal via their own websites or manufacturer channels. AutoTrader survived by continuously reinvesting in richer buyer tools (valuation, financing, video) that raised switching costs for dealers rather than defending price. It never treated its dominance as a reason to stop building.

Booking.com. Faced disintermediation risk from Google's own travel-search products and now, most recently, from AI travel agents doing the same "why do I need the portal" job threatened against Rightmove. Booking.com's response was heavy, continuous reinvestment in mobile UX, personalization, and (more recently) generative-AI trip-planning tools built by the incumbent itself, funded from a still-dominant cash-generative base, rather than harvesting margin while the product stagnated.

Bloomberg (Terminal). Faced decades of "why do we need this workflow lock-in" pressure from cheaper data providers and never lost material share, because the switching cost was built from workflow embedding (analysts' fingers know the keystrokes) rather than from information scarcity alone. Rightmove's agent-side lock-in argument — "clients asked why the property was not marketed on Zoopla" after one agent cancelled [threat register item 6] — is the same mechanism in miniature: the moat is habit and completeness of coverage on both sides of the market, not just data.

4. DISRUPTION TIMELINE

Newspaper classifieds took roughly a decade (2000–2010) to go from "digital is a curiosity" to "the revenue line is gone," once a dedicated, better-matched vertical competitor (Rightmove, AutoTrader, Monster) existed and captured supply-side listings. Point of no return signal to watch for, based on both analogues: in each case, the terminal move was not rising consumer traffic to the new channel — it was the supply side deciding to stop paying for the old one, because the new channel could reach buyers without it. 1. What This Company Actually Does

Rightmove is the UK's digital shop window for houses. Every estate agent in Britain pays a monthly rent — about £1,621 — to put its "For Sale" signs in the one window that almost every house-hunter in the country already walks past. The homebuyer browses for free, scrolling photos of kitchens and gardens. The agent pays, because that's where the customers are already standing. It's less a website than a toll bridge that both sides of the property market have to cross.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

The dossier's first-pass label was "clean analytical with a binary/event-driven overlay" — clean analytical because revenue, margins and pricing all move in narrow, predictable bands, with a "binary overlay" because a £1.5 billion lawsuit is sitting on top of it. A lawsuit looks like a coin flip: you win, or you lose, and the number swings wildly either way. That instinct is reasonable. It is also, after four rounds of adversarial checking, wrong.

Here is what actually happened when the claim was priced properly instead of eyeballed. Instead of treating it as heads-you-win, tails-you-lose, the analysis built a four-branch outcome tree: 20% chance it's thrown out before trial, 45% chance Rightmove wins outright, 25% chance it settles for roughly £150m, 10% chance of an adverse judgment near £400m. Weight those by probability and the expected cash cost is £117.5m — about 16 pence per share. Add the more insidious second-order cost — a board that quietly stops pushing prices as hard while its lawyers are in the room — and the total comes to 47 pence. That's real money, but on a stock trading at 495.6p it is a rounding error, not a coin flip. A true binary risk (a drug either gets FDA approval or it doesn't, a merger either closes or it doesn't) has no meaningful middle; this one, once you build the tree, turns out to have a very calm, boring middle.

So what actually decided the verdict? Not the lawsuit. The analysis explicitly checked its own instrument panel — "is the dominant factor a binary regulatory event?" — and the honest answer came back false. The single factor that moved the final number more than anything else was the discount rate: the annual return an investor demands to hold this stock. That is about as "clean analytical" a variable as exists — every company has one — and yet twelve experts and four rounds of red-teaming never converged on it, because the correct number sits somewhere in a wide, unresolved band (9.7% to 11.2%). This is a clean analytical problem where the hardest number to pin down is the most ordinary one in the toolkit, not the exotic one in the headlines.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

The reusable lesson isn't about lawsuits or property portals — it's about where analytical risk actually hides. A framework for any future company:

  1. Find the one variable that flips the verdict, not the one that grabs headlines. Test every input across its full plausible range. Most won't move the answer much. Usually exactly one will — find it before you argue about anything else.
  2. Don't grant "binary" status to a risk until you've tried to build the probability tree. Headline-scary events (lawsuits, investigations, single-product bets) often collapse into a small, bounded expected cost once you separate outcomes and weight them. Reserve true option-like thinking for events with no meaningful middle ground.
  3. Watch for one witness wearing three costumes. A DCF, a P/E-multiple grid and a reverse-DCF can look like three independent confirmations when they are secretly the same equation rearranged. Before citing "convergence" as evidence, check whether the methods are truly independent or just relabeled.
  4. "Unresolved" is not "unresolvable." If the swing variable is a standard input like a discount rate, don't throw up your hands — go find the extra data point (here: the market's own implied UK equity risk premium) that narrows the band. An afternoon of research often beats another week of modeling around an unknown.
  5. For anything long-duration — a monopoly, a franchise, a bond-like cash flow — the discount rate does more work than the growth rate. Check it first, check it hardest, and never let it drift silently between sections of your own analysis.

4. How They Make Money

Roughly 19,000 estate-agency branches across the UK pay Rightmove a monthly subscription, billed in advance, averaging £1,621 a month — no long commitment required, yet customer retention is at a ten-year high. They pay it because Rightmove captures somewhere between 74% and 90% of all UK property-search attention (measurement providers disagree on the exact number, but not on the direction). A listing that isn't on Rightmove is, to most buyers, a house that doesn't exist. Almost all of the recent revenue growth — the underlying membership count itself is barely moving — comes from raising that monthly fee by about 6.4% a year. A small but fast-growing sideline (commercial property, mortgages, rentals — about 7% of revenue, growing 25% a year) adds extra legs to the stool.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

Rightmove sits at the center of a two-sided loop: buyers go where the listings are, agents list where the buyers are, and each side reinforces the other — the classic network effect. More than 85% of Rightmove's traffic arrives organically or directly, meaning people type "Rightmove" the way they say "Google it," not "search for a property website." The business runs at roughly 68% operating margin while owning no property, no inventory, no factories — it is a toll bridge, not a factory, and it barely needs to reinvest what it earns. A serious, well-funded challenger (CoStar, spending roughly three times Rightmove's marketing budget) has been attacking for over a year and has moved Rightmove's traffic by about negative one percent. That is not proof the moat is permanent — but it is real-time evidence of just how wide it currently is.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

It isn't losing the lawsuit outright — that outcome is small and already priced in. The real risk is that nearly 100% of Rightmove's recent growth has come from one lever: raising the monthly fee by about 6.4% a year, largely uncoupled from any growth in the number of paying agents. That lever is precisely what the Competition Appeal Tribunal claim puts on trial, and the danger isn't a courtroom loss — it's that the mere presence of 250-plus agents suing, a former competition regulator leading the case, and the resulting publicity makes Rightmove's board quietly gentler on future price increases, for years, regardless of how the case is decided. If the engine that has driven nearly all recent growth throttles back even modestly, the "monopoly toll" starts to look more like a "regulated utility" — and monopolies and regulated utilities are priced very differently.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Think of Rightmove like a rental property that reliably throws off rent every month — the tenants (agents) practically never leave, and the rent goes up every year. What you'd pay for that property depends enormously on the mortgage rate available to you, even if the rent itself never changes. Right now, the UK's "mortgage rate" — the 10-year government gilt — sits at 5.15%, a level not seen in over a decade, up sharply from the near-zero rates of the 2010s when Rightmove's historically rich valuations were set. Nothing about the house has gotten worse. But the return a rational buyer needs to clear has gone up, and that alone makes the same rental income worth less today than it was worth five years ago.

At 495.6p, the market is effectively assuming you can finance this purchase at the friendliest end of a fairly narrow rate band — about 9.7%. Move to the realistic midpoint of that band, 10.45%, and the fair value comes out closer to 445p. The analysis's "buy zone" runs 340p to 445p: below 445p you're being properly paid for the uncertainty in that one rate; below 340p — the worst-case scenario where the tribunal wins and price increases stall for a decade — you're getting a genuine bargain. Everyone agrees the house is beautifully built. The entire argument is about which mortgage rate you should assume when you write the check.
