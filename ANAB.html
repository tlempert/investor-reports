ANAB — The Contrarian Case

0. What kind of stock is this, actually

Forget the FINANCIAL PHYSICS and VALUATION ANCHORS blocks entirely — the dossier itself says they're computed on a consolidated pre-spin entity that stopped existing on 2026-04-20. An "OVERVALUED (Above Optimistic DCF)" verdict on a company with $135,970k of R&D and a $179,006k Biopharma operating loss [SEC] tells you nothing about a company that now runs on fewer than 10 contractor FTEs and under $10M of annualized OpEx [MEDIA per company release]. Any bear leaning on that table is citing a ghost.

What ANAB actually is, post-spin, is the purest form of my favorite setup: a spinoff. Index funds and biotech-specific holders that owned AnaptysBio for its clinical pipeline got handed First Tracks (TRAX) shares and, in many cases, dumped the little royalty stub they didn't ask for and don't know how to model. That's exactly the mechanism that makes spinoffs mispriced — nobody at the fund has "royalty shell with litigation overhang" in their mandate, so it gets sold reflexively, not analytically. A 40.6% short-of-float [SEARCH per Yahoo Finance] and three conflicting Sagard-payoff dates in the sources are symptoms of the same thing: nobody has done the work.

1. The Ten-Bagger Test

This is not a "fast grower" in the traditional Lynch sense (no same-store sales, no unit economics) — it's a turnaround-via-corporate-surgery: a bloated, cash-burning clinical company amputated its entire R&D organization and re-emerged as a pure contractual royalty claim. That reclassification is the whole thesis.

What's priced in: at $58.37 and ~29.61M shares [SEC], market cap is $1.71-1.74B [SEARCH]. Strip out ~$140-145M of net cash (~$4.73/share) [MEDIA, CALC] and the market is paying roughly $1.57-1.6B for two royalty streams that currently pay ANAB nothing, because every Jemperli dollar goes to Sagard until a $600M cumulative cap ($675M if paid after 2031-03-31) [MEDIA]. That is a very skeptical price for an asset sitting on top of a molecule whose owner (GSK) is guiding it at +27% AER/CER in the most recent quarter [MEDIA per GSK].

What's achievable: [MEDIA per GlobalData] projects Jemperli reaching $2.56B by 2030. Run that through the tiered royalty (8% to $1B / 12% to $1.5B / 20% to $2.5B / 25% above) and ANAB's marginal royalty rate on the last dollars of sales rises to 25% — an accelerator built into the contract, not into ANAB's execution. Once Sagard is satisfied (company's own estimate: as early as Q2 2027, on a ~10% QoQ growth assumption that recent quarters already appear to be beating; litigation counsel's own filing says Q2 2027-Q2 2028 [MEDIA, three sources]), essentially all of that scales straight to ANAB's ~$10M cost base. That's not a ten-bagger on today's numbers in twelve months — I won't pretend precision I don't have — but it is a legitimate multi-bagger setup over a 3-5 year window if the tiers keep climbing and the Chancery ruling doesn't gut the contract.

That's a real, decided data point in ANAB's favor on the procedural record — bears citing "the lawsuit could gut the royalty" are citing the claim that already lost, not the ones still pending. Milestone and royalty payments continue to be due during the proceedings [MEDIA], so the cash mechanics haven't stopped.

The backstop caps the obligation at $675M regardless of when it's paid [MEDIA], so the total leakage to Sagard is bounded even in the slow case. And the trigger is Jemperli sales growth — the same growth that also expands the royalty base ANAB eventually keeps. A buyer isn't betting on if, only on when.

3. The Hidden Growth

The headline story is "litigation overhang on a spun-off shell." The buried story is two-fold:

  1. Jemperli is compounding fast and the royalty rate itself is an accelerator. Sales roughly tripled 2023→2024 and nearly doubled 2024→2025 [MEDIA per TripleS], with GSK's own Q2 2026 print at +27% AER/CER [MEDIA per GSK]. Every dollar of growth above $1.5B moves ANAB from a 12% to a 20% royalty, and above $2.5B to 25% — the company doesn't need to do anything operationally to capture that; it's contractual.

1. TAM vs SAM — real market, tiny and shrinking sliver for ANAB specifically

4. THE DISINTERMEDIATION SCENARIO

The Buffett View

1. The Anti-Fragile Moat

Cancer treatment demand is economically defensive, but ANAB does not become stronger during chaos.

Competitor failure could help Jemperli, but that benefit belongs operationally to GSK. ANAB is a passive recipient.

Result: defensive underlying demand, but not anti-fragile.

The $1 Billion Competitor Test

A rival cannot recreate ANAB’s 2014 contract merely by spending $1B. That is the strongest part of the moat: a legally protected claim on Jemperli sales.

But the protection is brittle:

  • Tesaro is already trying to halve royalties and milestones and obtain a permanent licence [MEDIA].
  • Jemperli competes in a PD-1 field containing 768 recorded assets [MEDIA].

ANAB has no direct pricing power. GSK determines Jemperli’s commercial strategy, pricing, contracting, discounts, and market access.

Pricing-power verdict: none at ANAB; uncertain at Jemperli.

ANAB — Historian of Disruption: Pattern Matching

Framing note

This is not a technology-disruption case in the Christensen sense — nobody is attacking Jemperli with a cheaper, worse, faster-improving substitute from below. It is a legal-and-contractual disruption case: the moat (ip_patents, contract_concession) is under direct assault by the counterparty who is subordinate to it, via litigation that can rewrite the economics overnight, layered on top of a patent-cliff clock that was always going to run out (2035 US / 2036-or-2038 EU). The right historical analogues are therefore drawn from royalty-aggregator and IP-licensing companies, not from retail chains or camera makers. I have picked analogues on business-model structure (single-molecule/single-counterparty royalty claim, non-recourse monetization debt ahead of equity, binary litigation exposure, known patent-cliff date) rather than on industry.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

PDL BioPharma (Protein Design Labs) — died on schedule when the clock ran out. PDL held the "Queen patents," foundational antibody-humanization IP that earned royalties from essentially every therapeutic antibody blockbuster of the 2000s — Avastin, Herceptin, Tysabri, Xolair, Actemra among them. At its peak this was one of the highest-margin royalty businesses in biotech: near-100% licensing revenue, no manufacturing risk, no clinical risk. The patents expired December 2014. Management had years of advance warning — this was never a surprise — and attempted to convert into a diversified royalty-acquisition vehicle (funding Depomed and other royalty deals) rather than simply collect and wind down. It didn't work at scale: PDL formally dissolved and liquidated by 2020, six years after the cliff. What killed them: not competition, not obsolescence — a hard, known expiry date on the sole royalty-generating IP, exactly the structure of ANAB's Jemperli composition-of-matter expiry (2035 US / 2036 or 2038 EU, dossier flags the EU date as unresolved). Lesson for ANAB: a fixed-expiry royalty is a melting ice cube with a known melt date, and diversification-after-the-fact is hard to execute well even when management sees the cliff coming for a decade.

VirnetX — pure-play patent litigant, value entirely dependent on courtroom outcomes. VirnetX's whole balance sheet was a portfolio of network-security patents monetized exclusively through litigation against Apple and Microsoft. Even winning at trial (VirnetX won multi-hundred-million-dollar verdicts against Apple more than once) did not translate cleanly into cash or a stable share price — appeals, retrials, and enforcement delays stretched for years past each verdict, and the stock round-tripped repeatedly on procedural rulings rather than economic ones. What killed the thesis (not the company, which survives as a shell of its former market interest): the market learned that a "we will collect eventually" royalty claim trading on litigation news is a binary, multi-year, low-visibility asset that is nearly impossible to underwrite with a normal cost of capital. This is the closest structural match to ANAB's Event A: a trial has already happened (2026-07-14/17), post-trial briefing is set, and no decision date is disclosed — VirnetX shareholders lived through exactly this kind of undated wait, repeatedly.

Rambus — litigation-dependent licensor that lost badly and was repriced overnight. Rambus's memory-interface licensing model depended on winning antitrust and patent-validity fights with JEDEC members (Micron, Hynix, Infineon). When Rambus lost the 2008-2009 antitrust rulings and had key patent claims narrowed, the stock fell more than 80% from its highs inside about a year, because the entire equity value was a claim on future licensing cash flows contingent on litigation outcomes the market had previously assumed would go Rambus's way. Lesson for ANAB: when 100% of the value is a legal claim, a single adverse ruling does not haircut the stock proportionally — it can reprice the entire equity in one session, because there is no diversified cash-generating base underneath to hold the floor. ANAB is more concentrated than Rambus was (Rambus at least had multiple counterparties; ANAB has one royalty counterparty actively suing it).

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

Royalty Pharma — the diversification template ANAB does not yet follow. Royalty Pharma built a multi-billion-dollar royalty-aggregation business specifically by never depending on one molecule or one counterparty. It holds dozens of royalty streams across therapeutic areas and companies, so a single litigation loss, patent invalidation, or clinical setback on any one asset barely moves the total. What they did differently: systematically redeployed cash into new royalty acquisitions rather than concentrating risk or returning capital via buybacks while the platform was still small. ANAB, post-spin, is a two-molecule royalty holder (Jemperli + imsidolimab) with ~$140-145M in cash — a fraction of what would be needed to build Royalty-Pharma-style diversification, but the direction of travel (acquire a third, fourth, fifth royalty stream) is the survivor move, and nothing in the dossier indicates ANAB is pursuing it.

Ligand Pharmaceuticals — pivoted from failed drug developer into a diversified royalty/technology aggregator. Ligand's own drug pipeline underperformed for years; management's survivor move was to stop trying to be a developer and instead monetize technology platforms (Captisol formulation IP, and later a stable of milestone/royalty interests in partner-developed drugs) across many uncorrelated programs. No single partner's regulatory or legal outcome can sink Ligand. Timing: this pivot took most of a decade of deliberate reallocation, started well before any single royalty stream was at risk of expiring or being litigated away.

XOMA — explicit "royalty aggregator" conversion, closest structural cousin to what ANAB could become. XOMA converted from a chronically-unprofitable antibody developer into a royalty-acquisition vehicle post-2016, buying interests in other companies' approved and late-stage royalty streams specifically to avoid the single-asset, single-counterparty exposure that had nearly sunk it as a developer. AnaptysBio (ANAB) — A Feynman Lesson

Price: $58.37 · Date: 2026-08-27

1. What This Company Actually Does

Imagine you invented a recipe for a cake, sold the recipe to a giant bakery chain (GSK) in exchange for a slice of every cake they ever sell using it, and then — years later — sold your own kitchen, your staff, and your ovens to someone else entirely. You kept nothing but the receipt that says "8 cents of every dollar of cake sales, rising as sales grow, until the recipe's copyright runs out."

That's AnaptysBio today. In April 2026 it spun off its entire drug-discovery operation — the labs, the scientists, the experimental pipeline — into a separate company (First Tracks Biotherapeutics). What's left, trading under the ticker ANAB, isn't a biotech anymore. It's a royalty claim: a contractual right to a rising percentage of sales of a cancer drug called Jemperli, made and sold by GSK, plus a smaller claim on a second drug awaiting approval, plus about $140 million in cash. AnaptysBio no longer does science. It cashes checks — or rather, it's suing to make sure it keeps getting to.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

Your eye is drawn to the lawsuit. GSK's Tesaro unit is in Delaware Chancery Court arguing that AnaptysBio breached their 2014 agreement, and if Tesaro wins, the royalty gets cut in half and GSK gets a permanent license. Trial happened in July 2026; a ruling could land any time. Every headline about this stock will be about that ruling. That makes it look like a Binary/event-driven problem — one court decision determines everything, like a coin flip where you should size your bet as if you might lose it all.

That classification is a trap, and figuring out why is the most important thing you can learn from this company.

The analysis behind this report ran seven adversarial passes — different experts attacking each other's numbers — specifically to test whether the lawsuit outcome actually decides the investment. It doesn't. Even in the scenario where AnaptysBio wins outright and keeps its full royalty, at the only long-range sales forecast available (GlobalData's $2.56 billion by 2030), the stock is worth less than today's price at every reasonable discount rate above 8%. The court case is a real risk, but it isn't the variable that moves the price. What moves the price is something quieter and less dramatic: how fast Jemperli's sales growth decelerates between now and 2030, against a patent clock that stops the royalty around 2034–2035 no matter what.

That reclassifies this as closer to a Cyclical/timing problem wearing a binary costume. The "cycle" here isn't a commodity boom-bust — it's a drug's adoption curve bending from today's roughly 30%-a-year growth toward some slower, mature-product growth rate, inside a fixed window that a courtroom ruling barely touches. The tell that you've misclassified a problem is exactly this: a loud, discrete event dominates the story, but when you actually run the math assuming that event resolves in your favor, the conclusion doesn't change. When that happens, you're looking at the wrong variable. The real one is duller, continuous, and easy to skip past on the way to the exciting headline.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

A reusable checklist for any situation where a loud binary risk sits on top of a quieter continuous one:

  1. Solve the "you win" case first. Before modeling probabilities for the dramatic event, assume it resolves entirely in your favor and re-run the valuation. If the investment still fails your hurdle in that best case, the binary event is irrelevant to your decision — stop spending analytical energy on it and go find the variable that's actually deciding the price.
  2. Find the continuous variable and build a range, not a point estimate. Here it's the sales growth deceleration slope. One analyst's single forecast is a guess; a grid across several plausible sales paths and several discount rates shows you where the argument actually lives.
  3. Treat any claim with a stated end date as a wasting asset, not a perpetuity. A royalty, a patent, a lease, a franchise term — all of these are annuities with a countdown clock. This is the crucial idea: time is the enemy, not a neutral backdrop. If your collection window is fixed at, say, ten years total, and something delays your first payment by a year, you haven't just pushed the whole schedule back a year — you've permanently deleted one of your ten years of cash flow. Delay doesn't defer value on a wasting asset; it destroys it, because the far end of the clock doesn't move to compensate.
  4. Map who stands between you and the cash. A subordinated structure (something else that gets paid before you) changes both the timing and the effective size of your claim, sometimes nonlinearly — a small cut to gross revenue can produce a much larger cut to what reaches you once you're behind someone else in line.
  5. Choose your discount rate for the risk you actually hold, not a generic one. A single-asset, litigated, subordinated, expiring claim is not the same risk as a diversified operating company, even if a spreadsheet lets you type the same 8% into both.

4. How They Make Money

AnaptysBio's royalty on Jemperli rises in tiers as GSK's global sales climb: 8% on the first $1 billion of annual sales, 12% from $1–1.5 billion, 20% from $1.5–2.5 billion, and 25% above $2.5 billion. Jemperli did about $1.13 billion in sales in 2025, so it's already crossing into the richer tiers — which is also, not coincidentally, when GSK decided to sue. A second, flat 10% royalty on a drug called imsidolimab (licensed to Vanda Pharmaceuticals) awaits an FDA decision in December 2026.

But here's the catch: AnaptysBio already sold part of this future royalty stream to a financing firm, Sagard, for $300 million upfront cash. Until Sagard collects a cumulative $600 million back (expected around 2027–2028), Sagard gets paid first — AnaptysBio collects nothing from Jemperli in the meantime. Think of it as a landlord (AnaptysBio) who already sold the next few years of rent to a factoring company; the tenant's payments go straight past the landlord until that debt clears.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

Be honest: there is no moat here in the Buffett sense — no brand, no network effect, no switching cost, no operating advantage that compounds. The "moat" is a signed contract and a patent expiration date. It's hard to dislodge AnaptysBio from its royalty (GSK can't simply stop paying), but it's not hard to outgrow or out-earn — the whole arrangement terminates on a printed calendar date, and one lawsuit is actively trying to cut it in half right now. This is a claim, not a business. It cannot reinvest, cannot build new products, and pays no dividend while you wait.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

The obvious answer is "AnaptysBio loses the lawsuit." That would genuinely hurt — roughly halving the royalty and shrinking total equity value by around half. But the analysis's real conclusion is more unsettling: the bigger risk is that AnaptysBio wins the lawsuit and it still doesn't matter, because Jemperli's sales growth decelerates from its current ~30%-a-year pace toward the published long-range forecast rather than sustaining the near-27% pace bulls need. At that published forecast, the stock fails to clear a reasonable required return even with a clean legal victory. The courtroom is the risk everyone is watching; the growth curve is the risk that actually prices the stock.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Picture buying a house that comes with a certified demolition date already on file with the county — the structure legally stops existing around 2034–2035. Worse, a tenant (Sagard) is living in it rent-free, collecting all the income, until roughly 2028, and only then do you start collecting rent yourself. So you're not buying "a house that generates rent forever." You're buying "a house that generates rent for maybe six to eight years, after a two-year wait, before it's torn down." At $58.37, the market is pricing this house as if the rent will keep growing at close to its current fast pace right up until demolition. The verdict's math says: even if you assume the landlord wins every legal fight over how much rent he's owed, the price only makes sense if you also assume the tenant's business keeps growing faster than is typical for a maturing product. That's a bet on the growth curve, dressed up as a bet on a lawsuit.
