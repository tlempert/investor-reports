I. THE MOAT — NARROWING, NOT NARROW

Tribunal as filed: 0/5 SEVERE, 5/5 MODERATE (Buffett, Biologist, Historian, Anthropologist, Psychologist). Jobs filed the only SEVERE and holds no seat.

Revision 1 overruled Jobs badly. Revision 2 then over-credited him, and I now correct in the other direction, because a fact sitting in the dossier's own Financial Physics table falsifies my downgrade and no participant in this entire process used it.

The KFTC unbundling order dates from December 2022; the Korean Supreme Court affirmed it on 14 April 2023. Korean yards have held the right to perform the technical assistance themselves for over three and a half years. Technical assistance is rendered during construction — contemporaneously, not on the booking lag. So the exposure has been live through three full annual reports. Here is what happened:

In Revision 2 I called the technical-assistance exposure "unquantified in this record" and "the single most important missing number in the file." It is not missing — it is bounded. If 70–75% of the order book had begun unbundling a high-margin service line, a 68% EBITDA margin does not expand. Either the yards are not exercising the right or the line is too small to matter, and both readings falsify the downgrade. "Smaller than I thought" is the honest sentence; "unquantifiable" was an evasion that happened to flatter my conclusion.

Second correction: Korea's 2028 is a proof-of-concept target. The threat register grades it STATUS: proposed, and the government's own language is "promoting proof-of-concept results." A POC in 2028, plus the multi-year safety-critical requalification I myself insist on, plus a yard willing to stake a $250M hull on an unproven containment system, puts commercial series adoption well into the 2030s — outside my normalisation window, not inside it. I had struck KC-1 as non-predictive of KC-2C (correct — different developer, different decade) and simultaneously promoted a government press release into a moat regrade. That is an evidentiary double standard, and it ran from demanding terminal evidence in Revision 1 to accepting the earliest conceivable evidence in Revision 2 without stopping in the middle.

Grade: narrowing, not narrow — a wide moat with a dated fuse, not a breached one. Fifty years of cryogenic know-how, multi-year class requalification, and Chinese yards ordering NO96 Super+ for Q1 2030–Q4 2031 delivery are durable and real (with the caveat I owe from Revision 2: that revealed preference is Chinese, while both live threats are Korean). Against them: one indigenous system in the water at 7,500 m³ — 4.31% of commercial scale; one prior programme that ended in technical failure and litigation; and no indigenous system shipped as a production series, ever.

Consequence for the multiple: 17x, the middle of the admitted 15x–18x band — not the 18x top, because Franchise Premium remains DENIED on criterion 3 (GTT's revenue is re-won, not recurring: a subscription renews by inertia, a GTT royalty requires somebody to order a ship, and nobody has to); and not the 16x I used in Revision 2, because that grade was reached by measuring a remedy I could have bounded from the tape.

II. THE PRICE IS THE BULL CASE

Here is the claim, and it is the last one, because it needs no multiple cap, no ship size, no annualisation and no guidance midpoint:

> At €204.60 you are paying Aberdeen's own fair value. The market's implied perpetual growth rate is 3.400%. My conversion of Aberdeen's "+60% LNG demand from 2025 to 2040" over fourteen years is 3.414%. Those agree to 1.4 basis points. The other side of that coin is €78. You are being paid nothing to take a binary that thirteen participants agree cannot be resolved from this record.

The Gordon arithmetic, at my own 8.75% hurdle: implied g = r − 1/(ex-cash multiple). At €204.60 the ex-cash multiple is (204.60 − 7.97)/10.52 = 18.69x → g = 3.400%. Aberdeen: 1.60^(1/14) − 1 = 3.414%. And I conceded in Revision 2 that the tonne-mile correction runs against me — LNG trade is moving toward longer hauls as US Gulf and Qatar displace regional supply, so fleet requirement grows faster than volume, and 60% is a floor. If 60% is a floor, 3.414% is a floor, and the fair multiple in Aberdeen's world is above what the market is paying.

So I do not claim the price is expensive. I claim it is exactly fair — in one of two worlds, and I cannot tell you which world we are in.

The demand conflict is therefore decisive and unresolved, and I state it without hedging: IEEFA versus Aberdeen determines the value of this business and I do not know which is right. What I know is the shape of the bet: heads is roughly today's price, tails is €78 — a 62% loss — and the coin costs full price.

The strongest bear datum in the file still requires no annualisation, because it is a stock, not a flow: 338 ships on order equal 38.9% of the existing world fleet by capacity, contracted through 2031. That dates the cycle without settling the debate.

III. LYNCH'S RECOGNITION LAG — CONCEDED, AND IT NOW CUTS BOTH WAYS

Revenue is recognised as ships are built, on roughly the 2.5-year lag I use for discounting. FY2026 guidance therefore reports the 2023–24 booking cohort — a trough following the 2022 Qatar/Ukraine ordering spike — and carries close to zero information about 2026 demand. GTT Energy revenue −5.7% "due mainly to fewer LNG carriers under construction" is a fact about ships ordered three years ago. In Revision 1 I used the lag when it made my discounting work and ignored it when it undercut my evidence; in Revision 2 I ignored it again on the bull side. Lynch is right, twice.

The correction that runs the other way, printed because honesty requires it: FY2026 guidance is GROUP revenue, carrying a full year of Danelec/GTT Marine (~€64M annualised) against a partial year in FY2025. Strip the acquisition and core revenue falls ~9.3%, not the headline −5.4%. That deepens the 2023–24 trough; it says nothing about today's order flow, and it does not rescue the argument Lynch demolished.

Lynch's factual claim checks out — 65 orders at €12.5M+ apiece far exceeds the €387M recognised in the half, so the book grew. (Both of us should note €1.9B at Jun-26 against €1,591M at Dec-25 is not a clean comparison: group versus core, with Danelec consolidated in between.)

IV. THE ACCOUNTING ANOMALY — STANDS AS CORRECTED

Seven experts attributed the H1 2026 net-income-versus-EBIT gap to interest income on the net cash pile. That fails on arithmetic: €295–347M at a generous 3% earns ~€4.8M per half, about one-seventh of the gap, before netting ~€90M of Danelec debt. That demolition is the one piece of work in this file nobody else did, and it survives both red teams.

But measured against FY2025's actual 79.34% NI/EBIT conversion rather than H1 2025's 69.83% outlier, the anomaly is €13.0M, not €36.6M — two-thirds of my headline was a weak-base artifact, which is exactly what my own hypothesis said had happened. The Elogen branch fails a scale check: a €13M–37M charge against a subsidiary with €2.5M of revenue is not credible without an asset write-off the dossier never mentions. The French IP-box regime is the sole survivor, and it is supported rather than guessed: 79.3% conversion is arithmetically impossible at France's 25% headline rate, which caps conversion near 75% before any financial income. It is inference — the dossier contains no tax discussion — and I flag it as such.

The +17% is worthless as evidence of operating strength; EBIT is the operating number and EBIT fell 3.4%. But earnings quality is unchanged, not degraded — a structural tax benefit is real recurring cash that simply is not operating performance. I therefore treat the 79.3% conversion as real and use it consistently, including as the input behind guided EPS of €10.52.

The dossier's own growth figure, which I used 2% against without ever saying why. ROE 79.6% × retention 25% → sustainable g = 19.9%. I reject it, and the reason should have been printed: that formula assumes retained capital earns ROE. GTT's does not. Retained euros sit in a bank at ~3% and buy 17.6%-EBITDA-margin businesses against a 68% core. A royalty company with nothing to reinvest in cannot compound at its own ROE, and this is the same fact as the Danelec point.

Capital allocation, with the counter-facts I omitted. ~37.0M shares flat for four years — no dilution, no buyback, no SBC in evidence — and a 75% payout ratio on a €8.60 dividend, a 4.20% trailing yield at spot (6.3% at my trigger, 11.0% at the bear branch). VI. FINAL DECISION

Decision: WAIT. Buy Zone: €78–€135. Position Size: 0% at €204.60. Conviction: Moderate.

First, the TOO UNCERTAIN question, because my previous escape route is gone. Revision 2 escaped "on one ground only" — that the price sat above the fully-granted bull case. That ground is dead. And the tripwires now genuinely graze: I write in plain italics that I cannot resolve the load-bearing variable, and the margin of safety I require (€78, 62% below spot) is wide enough to look like distrust of my own estimate.

I argue out of it, and on different ground than before. TOO UNCERTAIN means don't buy, don't short, don't even watch — move on, and it exists for variables that never resolve: regime change, binary geopolitics, things fundamental tools structurally cannot price. This variable resolves, on a knowable schedule, from observable data. LNG-carrier order intake over FY2027–28 settles IEEFA versus Aberdeen with the tape rather than with a forecast. The business is comprehensible in one sentence, solvent past a 45% revenue decline, and has never diluted a shareholder. A binary that resolves with time and costs nothing to wait for is exactly what WAIT is for. Filing this as "too hard" would be laziness wearing discipline's coat — and equally, the difference between the two is not academic: TOO UNCERTAIN would mean stop reading the order data, and reading the order data is the whole job here.

Why the council's price cluster is real but must be discounted. Every one of the twelve published a trigger below spot — €108–128 (Burry), €114–136 (Cook, Jobs), €115–135 (Buffett), €137–149 (Bezos), €140–155 (Historian), €145–155 (Psychologist), €150–160 (Biologist, Anthropologist), €150–165 (Sherlock, Futurist, Lynch); mean midpoint €143. But this is one dossier read twelve times, not twelve witnesses, and I weight it accordingly: twelve people who cannot find a bull at this price is a fact about this price, not a proof about this business.

Why is it mispriced? It is not, in the ordinary sense — that is Revision 3's whole point. The market is paying Aberdeen's own number, competently. What is mispriced is the variance: you are charged the good outcome's price for a distribution whose bad tail is 62% lower. If the price came to €135 the opportunity would be Complexity plus Boredom — a €7.6B French mid-cap filed under "Oil Equipment and Services," whose real driver is shipbroker order-intake at m³ granularity that almost no equity investor tracks, and which files nothing with the SEC for the screens to chew on.

Moat Half-Life: 15+ years, and the fuse is legal rather than technical. The physics and class-approval moats are durable. The pricing mechanism is what is under attack — but the attack has been live since December 2022 and margins expanded through it, and Korea's own target is a proof of concept in 2028, not a delivered ship. My Revision 1 tripwire (a delivered series) was a lagging indicator; my Revision 2 tripwire (the technical-assistance line) turned out to be inferable from margins. The right watch item is the order-intake tape, which settles the question that actually matters.

Conviction: Moderate, and the moderation is now load-bearing rather than decorative. Two red teams have found the verdict right and my headline wrong. That pattern should make a reader trust the WAIT and distrust any single argument in this memo — including this one. The case that needs no machinery is four lines long: a wonderful business, an owner yield of 5.0–6.3% against a hurdle of 8.0–9.5%, a demand argument thirteen participants agree cannot be settled from this record, and nobody paying you to take it. That was true in Revision 1 and it is true now.

Do not confuse the clocks: the 2027–29 risk is the newbuild cycle, the 2030s risk is a regulator, and neither is a reason to sell a fifty-year franchise. Sit on your ass. There is no called strike here.

GTT.PA — Historian of Disruption

0. Framing the Comparison Set

GTT is not a platform business with network effects or a subscription franchise — it is a patent-licensing monopolist collecting a fixed royalty (~5% of vessel price) on a capital good it does not manufacture, sold into a market with three buyer nations (Korea, China, Japan) that have explicit industrial-policy incentive to build around it. That combination — narrow technical moat, geographically concentrated customer base, hostile domestic regulators in the customer's home country — is a specific historical pattern. The stock persona's default list (Yellow Pages, Blockbuster, MySpace) describes consumer-platform disruption by a superior product; it does not fit GTT's structure. The better analogues are foreign patent-licensing monopolists facing state-sponsored "indigenization" campaigns in their largest customer market.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

Xerox vs. Japanese copier makers (1970s–80s). Xerox held a near-total US/global monopoly on plain-paper copying via patents, defended aggressively through litigation and broad licensing refusal. The FTC brought an antitrust case culminating in a 1975 consent decree forcing Xerox to license its patents on reasonable terms to competitors — the direct structural analogue to the Korea Fair Trade Commission's December 2022 order forcing GTT to let Korean yards perform their own technical-assistance work, upheld on appeal by Korea's Supreme Court on 14 April 2023. Xerox's US market share in copiers collapsed from ~95% in 1972 to roughly 13% in the low/mid segment by the early-to-mid 1980s as Canon, Ricoh, and Minolta engineered around the patents and undercut on cost. Management's posture right before the collapse: confidence that patent protection and installed-base switching costs would hold — the same argument GTT made in its (failed) Korean Supreme Court appeal, that the tying arrangement was "a legitimate exercise of patent rights." Time from regulatory intervention to material share loss: roughly 7-10 years.

RCA and color-television licensing (1950s–70s). RCA developed and broadly licensed color-TV patents to Japanese manufacturers (Sony, Panasonic/Matsushita, Toshiba) rather than treat them as an existential threat, collecting royalty income while its own manufacturing know-how stagnated. Japanese licensees out-executed RCA on cost, reliability, and manufacturing scale; RCA exited television manufacturing entirely by 1986, sold first to GE then to Thomson. The lesson relevant to GTT: licensing your core IP to the exact companies with the industrial capacity to out-build you is not neutral — it can fund your own replacement, particularly relevant given GTT continues to supply Chinese yards (NO96 Super+ orders through 2031) that are simultaneously developing domestic alternatives.

De Beers diamond monopoly (1980s–2010s). Not a licensing case, but the closest analogue for "cartel/near-monopoly pricing power eroded by antitrust action plus new entrants." De Beers controlled ~85-90% of world diamond supply in the 1980s; US DOJ antitrust pressure kept it out of the US market directly for decades, and new mine entrants (Argyle in Australia, Alrosa in Russia) plus eventual internal restructuring left De Beers controlling roughly 35-40% of supply by the 2010s. The instructive point for GTT: even a deeply entrenched monopoly facing sustained antitrust pressure and credible new entrants took two to three decades to erode from ~90% to ~35% control — this is a slow-motion process, not a cliff, which cuts against assuming Korean/Chinese substitution happens on a 2-3 year clock.

2. SURVIVOR ANALOGUES (The Adapters)

ASML and Chinese EUV lithography. ASML holds a near-total monopoly on extreme-ultraviolet lithography, the enabling technology for leading-edge chips — structurally the closest match to GTT's position (single Western/European technical monopolist, hostile state-sponsored indigenization effort in a major customer geography, China). Despite over a decade of Chinese state investment (SMIC, SiCarrier, and others) aimed explicitly at reducing reliance on ASML, no domestic EUV-equivalent has reached production maturity. ASML's response was not denial: continuous heavy R&D reinvestment, moving the goalposts forward (High-NA EUV) faster than the technology could be replicated, and treating export-control politics as a cost of doing business rather than an existential threat to manage away. This is the "moat evolves by staying two generations ahead" pattern GTT would need to match — its 64 patents filed in 2023 and the new ammonia/methanol Approvals in Principle from Bureau Veritas are the right kind of action, just at a much smaller scale of committed capital than ASML's.

Qualcomm and Chinese antitrust/indigenization (2015). China's NDRC fined Qualcomm $975M in 2015 for antitrust violations in its patent-licensing model and forced restructured, lower royalty terms — a near-exact parallel to the KFTC's 2022 order and 2023 Supreme Court affirmation against GTT. Qualcomm did not fight the outcome into further denial; it absorbed the lower rate structure, kept its standard-essential patent position in 5G, and expanded into automotive and IoT licensing to diversify revenue. It remains dominant in premium mobile chipsets a decade later. The lesson: a lost antitrust fight over licensing terms is survivable if the underlying technical lead is real and the company reinvests rather than retrenches — but it is a genuine margin and structure hit, not a non-event.

Visa/Mastercard ceding China to UnionPay. Rather than fight a losing regulatory battle for the domestic Chinese payments market (where UnionPay was mandated), Visa and Mastercard effectively conceded that market and doubled down on cross-border and international network investment, where their moat was uncontested. Applied to GTT: if Korean and Chinese indigenous systems eventually win their domestic newbuild markets, the survivor move is not fighting every KFTC ruling to the Supreme Court but defending share where switching costs and safety-certification lock-in remain strongest (Japanese, European, Qatari yards/owners) rather than treating every geography as equally defensible.

Casualty signals present in the dossier: - GTT fought the Korean antitrust finding through to the Supreme Court and lost — the "legitimate exercise of patent rights" defense is structurally identical to Xerox's pre-collapse posture. This is a denial signal, now adjudicated against the company. - Diversification execution is weak, not absent, which is its own warning: GTT Marine (Danelec) runs at 17.6% EBITDA margin against a 68.1% core, and Elogen (hydrogen) revenue collapsed from €2.5M to €0.1M (-96.5%) H1-25→H1-26. Survivor signals present in the dossier: - No buybacks, no dilution (shares flat at ~37M for four years) — GTT is not doing the classic casualty move of extracting cash via buybacks instead of investing. The dossier does not resolve which.

Net read: GTT's capital discipline (no buybacks, continued patent filing, product-line extension) matches survivor behavior. GTT's response to the direct regulatory attack on its moat (fight-and-lose rather than absorb-and-adapt) and execution on diversification (Danelec/Elogen both underperforming) match casualty behavior. This is a genuinely mixed signal, not a clean case either way — which is why the verdict below is a moderate-confidence lean rather than a high-confidence call.

4. DISRUPTION TIMELINE

Anchoring on the Xerox analogue (regulatory intervention → material share loss took ~7-10 years) and the ASML/De Beers analogues (state-sponsored indigenization against a real technical lead took 10-30 years and in ASML's case still hasn't succeeded): GTT is roughly 3 years past the triggering regulatory event (Korean Supreme Court, April 2023), with the Xerox clock suggesting a further 4-7 years before the pattern would show up in market-share terms, if it plays out that way at all.

Where we are today, concretely: - No successful indigenous series delivery yet. Samsung Heavy's KC-2C is a single 7,500 m³ carrier, not a production series. KC-1 (the earlier Korean attempt) suffered technical problems, operational disruptions and lawsuits between its own developers — the historical track record of substitution attempts is genuinely poor so far, which is the strongest evidence for the survivor case. - The regulatory loss is real and adjudicated, not speculative — this is a harder fact than the "proposed" or "speculative" status of most other threats in the dossier's threat register. - The cyclical clock is shorter than the structural clock and arrives first. With 38.9% of the existing fleet already on order and independent sources flagging rate pressure and secondhand softness emerging from 2027-2028, the order-book-driven earnings power that's justifying today's 68% EBITDA margin plateaus or rolls over well before any indigenous containment system could plausibly reach production-series maturity.

Stage assessment: early-warning, transitioning toward mid-transition — the regulatory shot has been fired and lost, indigenous entrants exist and are shipping (if not yet succeeding), but no point-of-no-return signal has occurred. The point-of-no-return signal, per the dossier's own "what would change the verdict" section, is a Korean or Chinese yard delivering a series — not a one-off — on indigenous technology. That has not happened. Watch for it in 2027-2029, the same window the order book itself starts to thin.

The pattern most resembles ASML circa 2015-2020 — a genuine technical monopolist absorbing a real, state-backed indigenization push in its most important growth customer, responding by extending the technology (fuel-conversion AiPs, continued patent filing) rather than denying the threat, while watching early domestic substitutes fail on execution (KC-1's lawsuits mirror early Chinese lithography attempts). Gaztransport & Technigaz (GTT.PA) — Explained Simply

Price: €204.60 | Verdict: WAIT | Buy zone: €78–€135 | Position: 0%

1. What This Company Actually Does

Imagine a moving company that doesn't own a single truck. Instead, it invented the only insulated cargo box strong enough to carry a liquid so cold it would shatter ordinary steel — and every truck manufacturer in the world has to license that box design and pay a fee each time they build one. That's GTT. It doesn't build ships. It designs the paper-thin, multi-layer membrane that lines the inside of an LNG carrier's tanks, keeping natural gas liquid at −163°C — colder than the surface of Pluto on a warm day. Shipyards in Korea, China, and Japan build the ship; GTT collects roughly 5% of the vessel's price, about $12.5 million on a $250 million carrier, just for the lining.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

This is a cyclical/timing problem wearing a binary problem's overlay — and learning to see both layers at once is the whole point of studying this company.

The dominant layer is cyclical. GTT doesn't get paid when gas moves through the world; it gets paid when a shipyard lays a keel for a new LNG carrier, and it books that royalty as the ship is built, over roughly a two-and-a-half-year construction lag. That means today's blowout 68% margins and 51% net margins reflect ships that were ordered back in 2022–2024, during a scramble after Russia cut gas to Europe. Revenue already went flat in H1 2026 — not because demand for GTT's technology collapsed, but because the order flow underneath today's revenue was thinner two and a half years ago. This is a textbook shipbuilding cycle: booms in ordering, followed by lulls, showing up in earnings with a lag. Cyclical businesses are healthy at their core but earn unevenly, and you have to normalize across the cycle rather than trust any single year.

But underneath that familiar cyclical pattern sits something rarer: a genuine binary. Two credible, well-resourced forecasters looked at the same world and reached opposite conclusions about LNG demand after 2030. IEEFA says European LNG demand peaked in 2025 and grinds lower through 2030, with global gas demand potentially falling as much as 79% by 2050 under aggressive decarbonization. Aberdeen says global LNG demand grows roughly 60% by 2040, requiring a bigger fleet, not a smaller one. These aren't small disagreements about growth rates — they're contradictory pictures of whether this is a shrinking or expanding market. That is not a cyclical question you can smooth over with a longer time horizon; it is a fork in the road, and the two branches lead to genuinely different companies. One analyst on this council calculated the two outcomes are worth €78 and roughly €187–273 per share respectively — that gap is what a real binary looks like.

So: treat the near-term earnings noise (2026–2029) as ordinary cyclical timing. Treat the 2030-and-beyond demand question as a coin flip you cannot call from this desk.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

When a business is mostly one problem type with a second type layered on top, don't average the two frameworks — apply them in sequence, to different questions.

  1. Separate the clocks. Write down, explicitly, which years belong to the cyclical question and which belong to the binary question. Here: 2026–2029 is "where are we in the newbuild cycle," and 2030+ is "which demand forecast is right." Never let evidence from one clock answer a question on the other clock — a weak H1 2026 print tells you nothing about 2035 demand, because it reflects orders placed years before either forecast mattered.
  2. Use the cyclical tools on the cyclical clock. Normalize earnings across the cycle rather than extrapolating a peak or trough year. Ask "what does this company earn in an average year of the cycle," not "what did it earn last quarter."
  3. Use binary/event-driven tools on the binary clock. Don't build a single confident forecast — build two scenarios, price both, and ask what the market is currently paying you to assume. If the current price only makes sense under the optimistic branch, you are being asked to bet on that branch for free.
  4. Find the resolving evidence, and its date. A true binary eventually resolves with data, not opinion — here, LNG-carrier order intake in 2027–2028 will show whether shipowners are betting with Aberdeen or IEEFA. Identify that trigger and wait for it rather than guessing.
  5. Size and price for the fork, not the average. Never buy at a price that only clears if the optimistic branch wins. Demand a margin of safety versus the pessimistic branch, because that is the outcome you must be able to survive.

4. How They Make Money

It's a royalty, plain and simple. A Korean or Chinese shipyard wins a contract to build an LNG carrier. To build the tank that actually holds the liquid gas, it must license GTT's membrane design — there's no legal way around it, because GTT holds the patents. GTT charges about 5% of the total ship price as a licensing and technical-assistance fee, collected as construction proceeds. Because GTT doesn't build anything itself — no steel, no shipyard, no factory — almost every euro of that royalty falls straight to the bottom line. That's why returns on invested capital run above 100%: there's barely any capital invested to generate the return.

5. Why They're Hard to Kill

Fifty years of accumulated cryogenic engineering know-how and 64 patents filed in a single recent year form the core wall. On top of that sits a second wall that's arguably stronger: every containment system must be certified by marine classification societies (like Bureau Veritas), and swapping to a new, unproven system is a multi-year, safety-critical requalification — nobody wants to be the shipyard whose first indigenous LNG tank fails at sea. That combination — patents plus institutional inertia — has kept roughly 900 ships in the water running on GTT's design.

Be honest about the cracks, though. South Korea's Supreme Court affirmed that GTT abused its market dominance by tying licenses to mandatory technical-assistance contracts — a real, adjudicated legal defeat in GTT's single biggest customer market, not a rumor. And Korea has been trying to build its own alternative for years. The track record of those attempts is poor: KC-1, an earlier homegrown system, suffered technical failures and triggered lawsuits between its own developers. A newer system, KC-2C, has shipped on one small vessel — a fraction of commercial scale — while Korea's stated goal for a fully proven replacement is still just a 2028 "proof of concept," years away from a real production series. The moat is narrowing, not gone.

6. The One Thing That Could Go Wrong

The newbuild cycle could be a pull-forward, not a plateau. Right now there are 338 LNG carriers on order — equal to 38.9% of the entire existing fleet. Shipowners rushed to lock in capacity after the 2022 energy shock, and much of that order book is scheduled for delivery through 2029–2031. If that wave of ordering was driven by a one-time panic rather than durable long-term demand growth, the order book could run dry right around 2030 — precisely the moment the IEEFA-versus-Aberdeen debate is supposed to resolve. A cliff in new orders after the current book empties would hit GTT's revenue hard, since its income is earned on new construction, not on ships already sailing.

7. The Price Tag Problem

Think of buying this stock like buying a house in a neighborhood where two appraisers disagree sharply about the future. One appraiser says the neighborhood is about to boom — new jobs, new transit line — and the house is worth €200,000. The other says the area is in decline and the same house is worth €78,000. Now imagine the seller is asking exactly €200,000 — the optimistic appraisal, to the dollar.

That's GTT at €204.60. Working the math backward from today's price, the market is implicitly assuming LNG demand grows at almost exactly the rate Aberdeen's bullish forecast implies — the two numbers agree to within two hundredths of a percentage point. If the pessimistic IEEFA forecast is right instead, the fair price is closer to €78. You are being asked to pay full price for the optimistic appraisal while carrying all the risk that the pessimistic one turns out true — and nobody is discounting the sticker price to compensate you for that risk. The verdict isn't "this is a bad house." It's "you're being asked to bet the best-case appraisal is correct, and you get nothing extra for taking that bet." A margin of safety would mean paying a price that still makes sense even if the pessimists are right — which is what the €78–€135 buy zone represents.
