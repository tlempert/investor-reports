Fairfax is a stalwart with a turnaround kicker. The market is currently pricing this like a cyclical peaking out. I think the market is a pessimist here, and that's exactly the setup I look for.

1. THE TEN-BAGGER TEST

Nobody should expect FFH to be a literal ten-bagger from here — this is a $74.9B-portfolio insurance holding company, not a small-cap. But the realistic best case is a re-rating plus compounding story:

  • Growth actually achievable: Book value per share has compounded at a high-teens rate for years, and FY2025 alone saw it rise 21% [SEARCH per company release]. Even normalizing away the peak year, an insurer earning 16%+ ROIC and retaining most of it should compound book value at a low-double-digit to high-teens rate.
  • Growth the market is pricing: At 1.32x P/B [SEARCH per Yahoo Finance], the market is pricing Fairfax like a mediocre-return insurer, not a 16%+ ROIC compounder. Quality peers like Markel and W.R. Berkley (already in this corpus) trade on similar or higher multiples for comparable or lower returns on capital.
  • The gap: If Fairfax merely re-rates to 1.6-1.8x book (still a discount to best-in-class underwriters) while book value compounds at even 10-12% annually, you get a multi-year double from here without needing heroic assumptions. That's not a ten-bagger, but it's a Lynch-style "the market missed the growth" setup, just applied to a bigger, safer company.

2. THE "EVERYONE HATES IT" SIGNAL

Let's take the bear cases one at a time.

Bear case #1: "2025 was peak-cycle; earnings will mean-revert hard." Counter: Sure — but the dossier's own Graham Floor (CAD 3,293.54), which is earnings-based rather than the broken FCF-DCF, sits 41% above today's price. Counter: This is real, but it's a margin headwind on a business earning 7 points of underwriting profit (93% combined ratio) at the top of the cycle — there's room to give some of that back and still underwrite profitably. Fairfax has been through harder cycles (2022's -CAD 6.87B FCF) and come out the other side with 19 straight years of favorable reserve development. Softening pricing is a known, cyclical, survivable headwind, not a structural break.

Bear case #3: "Muddy Waters alleged asset-valuation manipulation." Counter: This is old news the market has already digested — the stock fell ~13% on the report, Watsa personally and forcefully rebutted it, and critically, Morningstar reviewed the allegations and did not change its fair value estimate [SEARCH/MEDIA]. Framing: This Is Not a Disruption Story, It's a Cycle Story

Before matching analogues, a scope note: the canonical disruption narrative (incumbent with scale/brand moat gets end-run by a cheaper, more convenient substitute that starts at the bottom of the market) does not map cleanly onto Fairfax. Insurance/reinsurance moats are built on regulatory license, capital scale, and claims-paying capacity — not on a value proposition that a scrappy entrant can simply out-convenience. The better historical frame for Fairfax is "holding company built on float + a skilled contrarian allocator" — and the relevant disruption question is not "will a startup kill this" but "does the moat survive the founder, and does the underwriting cycle turn into something structurally worse than a cycle." I'll address both.

1. DEAD ANALOGUES (The Casualties)

AIG (2005–2008): Scale Without Discipline

AIG was the largest insurer in the world by the mid-2000s — a diversified, multi-line, multi-geography conglomerate not unlike Fairfax's structure today (dozens of subsidiaries, an investment arm layered on top of underwriting). AIG's collapse wasn't disruption by a new entrant — it was capital allocation failure at scale: AIG Financial Products wrote $500B+ of credit default swaps against subprime CDOs, a non-core, poorly risk-managed bet layered on top of a genuinely good insurance franchise. Management (Joseph Cassano, head of AIGFP) said in August 2007: "It is hard for us, without being flippant, to even see a scenario within any kind of realm of reason that would see us losing one dollar in any of those transactions." Fifteen months later AIG required a $182B government bailout. The lesson for Fairfax is directly on point given the dossier's flags: Fairfax's moat is not threatened by an app, it's threatened by capital allocation drift — the Non-Insurance Companies segment's SG&A jump (CAD 1.36B → CAD 6.80B, 2022–2025) and Watsa's own concentrated, contrarian 13F (Orla Mining 25.8%, Under Armour 19.6%) are the closest thing to an AIGFP-style tail risk in this dossier: not a moat-erosion story, but a "smart guy makes a big idiosyncratic bet with float" story. AIG's core P&C business, notably, survived and is still a going concern today — it was the off-balance-sheet derivative bet that nearly killed the company, not the insurance franchise itself.

General Re / Lloyd's LMX Spiral (late 1980s–1990s): Underpricing the Cycle

The Lloyd's LMX spiral specifically: reinsurers kept reinsuring each other's cat risk in a closed loop, masking true aggregate exposure, until a string of catastrophes (Piper Alpha 1988, Hurricane Hugo 1989, European storms 1990) revealed the entire market had underpriced tail risk for years. Lloyd's Names faced ruinous, unlimited-liability losses; it took Lloyd's until the mid-1990s "Reconstruction and Renewal" to recapitalize. This is the direct historical precedent for the dossier's own flag: "Global reinsurance market is softening but not soft... alternative capital (ILS/cat bonds) delivered double-digit returns in 2025 and is expected to keep growing, adding structural pricing pressure." The mechanism is identical — new capital (then: Lloyd's Names/retro spirals; now: ILS/cat bonds) compresses pricing below true risk cost during the benign years, and the reckoning comes at the next major cat cycle. Fairfax's four major subsidiaries are all sub-95% combined ratio today (healthy), but this analogue says the risk is a 2-4 year lagging one, not an immediate one.

Blockbuster (2000–2010) — Included for Completeness, Weak Fit

Blockbuster is the standard disruption-case study but fits Fairfax poorly: Blockbuster had a single-format, single-channel business model (physical rental) with no float, no scale-diversification, and a management team that explicitly turned down acquiring Netflix for $50M in 2000. Fairfax's moat (licensed capital + diversified underwriting + a value-investing engine) has no equivalent one-dimensional failure point. Flagging this analogue mainly to say it does not apply — do not force a Blockbuster narrative onto an insurance holding company; the dossier's own Jobs section makes the same point about product-simplicity frameworks not fitting Fairfax.

Fairfax Financial Holdings (FFH.TO) — A Feynman-Style Walkthrough

1. What This Company Actually Does

Imagine you run a really trustworthy neighborhood umbrella-rental stand. People pay you $20 a year "just in case it rains" — a hurricane, a car crash, a factory fire. Most years, it doesn't rain on any given customer, so that $20 sits in your cash box. You don't just let it sit there, though — you invest it, and when it does rain for someone, you pay their claim out of the box.

That's Fairfax. It's an insurance company — actually about 20 insurance companies stitched together (Brit, Odyssey, Allied World, Zenith, and others) — that collects premiums from people and businesses insuring against fires, storms, lawsuits, and accidents. The pile of collected-but-not-yet-paid-out premiums is called "float," and Fairfax's 75-year-old CEO, Prem Watsa, invests that float personally, the way a value investor picks stocks. So Fairfax is really two businesses stacked on top of each other: an insurance company, and a hedge fund funded by other people's premium money.

2. What Kind of Problem This Is

This is a cyclical/timing problem, and understanding why matters more than any single number in the dossier.

Here's the mental trap most people fall into with insurers: they see FY2025's numbers — a 93% combined ratio (meaning 7 cents of profit on every premium dollar, on top of a 9.3% investment return) — and they extrapolate it forward, as if this is simply "how good Fairfax is now." It isn't. It's how good the insurance pricing environment was in 2025. Combined ratios don't move because management got smarter; they move because the whole industry's pricing power inflates and deflates in a cycle, like farmland or oil tankers or semiconductors.

Here's why the cycle exists, and it's worth internalizing because it recurs in every insurance and commodity business you'll ever look at: after a run of bad catastrophe years, insurers get scared, capital leaves the industry, and the survivors can charge fat premiums for the same risk — that's what happened through 2023-2025, and it's why all four of Fairfax's major subsidiaries posted sub-95% combined ratios simultaneously. But fat margins are a magnet. New capital shows up — traditional competitors expanding, and now also "insurance-linked securities," which are essentially bonds that let bond investors collect insurance premiums without running an insurance company. New capital chases the same risk, undercuts on price, and combined ratios drift back up toward 100% (breakeven) or worse. The dossier flags this directly: the reinsurance cycle is softening industry-wide in 2026, meaning FY2025's 93% may be the top of the cycle, not the resting state.

Why does the type of problem change your analysis? Because in a clean analytical business (say, a toll road with a 50-year contract), this year's earnings are a reasonable proxy for next year's. In a cyclical business, this year's earnings are almost useless as a forecast on their own — you have to ask "where are we in the cycle?" before you ask "how much did they make?" A great year at the top of a cycle is not the same signal as a great year in a business with no cycle at all. Layered on top of the underwriting cycle, Fairfax has a second, faster-moving cycle: its investment portfolio marks to market every quarter, which is why free cash flow swung from -CAD 6.87B in 2022 to +CAD 2.60B in 2025 — not because the insurance operations became four times healthier, but because Watsa's stock picks and bond prices moved. Two overlapping cycles, one earnings statement. That combination is exactly why this is a timing problem, not a "just check the P&L" problem.

3. How to Think About This Kind of Problem

When you're staring at a cyclical business — insurers, homebuilders, shipping, semiconductors, commodity producers, airlines, casinos — earnings and even multiples lie to you if you read them at face value. Here's a four-step framework to apply every time you meet one:

1. Normalize earnings across the full cycle, not the latest year. Don't ask "what did they earn in 2025?" Ask "what have they earned on average across a full up-and-down cycle — say, 5-10 years?" Fairfax's ROIC has actually been remarkably stable (16.0%-18.6% across 2022-2025, TTM 16.3%) even while free cash flow whipsawed — that stability in the return on capital, despite volatility in the cash flow timing, is a good early signal that the underlying business is sturdier than the noisy numbers suggest.

2. Find a steadier proxy metric that isn't cycle-distorted. For insurers, that's book value per share — Fairfax's rose 21% in FY2025 to CAD 1,250.14. Book value grows from retained underwriting profit plus investment gains, smoothing out some of the quarter-to-quarter mark-to-market chaos. When a business is cyclical, always hunt for the metric management and long-term owners actually track internally — it's rarely the headline earnings number.

3. Let multiples expand and contract with the cycle — and buy near the low end. A cyclical business deserves a lower multiple at the peak of its cycle (because next year's earnings are likely to be worse) and a higher multiple at the trough (because next year's earnings are likely to be better). This is the single most common mistake retail investors make with cyclicals: they buy the low P/E at the peak (looks "cheap," is actually expensive) and sell the high P/E at the trough (looks "expensive," is actually cheap). Munger's buy zone for Fairfax — CAD 2,290 to CAD 3,290 — is anchored not to peak earnings but to tangible book value, precisely to avoid this trap.

4. Ask "what protects me if my cycle-timing is wrong?" You will rarely nail the top or bottom of a cycle exactly. The protection is a floor asset value that doesn't depend on the cycle cooperating — for a bank or insurer, that's tangible book value; the underlying claims reserves and investment portfolio have real worth regardless of where combined ratios sit next year. Buy close to that floor, and being early or late matters much less.
